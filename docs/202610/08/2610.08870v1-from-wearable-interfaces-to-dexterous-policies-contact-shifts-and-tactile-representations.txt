# From Wearable Interfaces to Dexterous Policies: Contact Shifts and Tactile Representations 

Ruitong Tian 1, Fang Xu 1, Noah B. Wilson 2, Xianyao Li 1, and Eric Jing Du 1,†

Abstract — Unlike conventional teleoperation, wearable in-terfaces allow operators to collect dexterous demonstrations through their own hand motions while directly interacting with task objects. This direct interaction reduces dependence on the target robot during collection, but it also makes the collection hardware part of the physical process that generates each demonstration. Interface geometry can influence both how a task is performed and what tactile observations are recorded for learning. We study two versions of a DexUMI-family exoskeleton that share the same robot command definition, mapping procedure, and tactile module type but differ in hand-side geometry. The revised interface reduces reported physical demand, improves selected device ratings, enables tactile access in a precision grasp that is mechanically blocked by the baseline, and produces task-dependent changes in recorded contact. Lid twisting primarily exhibits a change in contact location, whereas egg carton opening primarily exhibits a change in contact oc-currence. We then train matched policies using either a binary aggregate input or a spatial-plus-force input. On lid twisting and egg carton opening, policies trained on revised-interface demonstrations achieve higher success than those trained on baseline demonstrations, with a significant pooled interface effect. Across these and two additional tasks, USB insertion and soldering tool pick and place, the spatial-plus-force input likewise outperforms binary aggregation. These results show that wearable collection hardware is part of the data-generation process for robot learning. Such interfaces should therefore be evaluated not only through operator experience, but also through the tactile interactions they make recordable and the policy performance their demonstrations support. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Learning dexterous manipulation policies requires demon-strations that preserve both high-dimensional hand motion and contact-rich interaction. Human-hand videos and motion-capture systems can collect data without a robot, but re-covering precise contact and robot-feasible joint commands remains difficult [1]. Robot-side teleoperation and hand-over-hand interfaces provide actions closer to the deployment embodiment, yet they require the physical robot hand during collection and can be cumbersome for contact-rich tasks [2]. Wearable interfaces offer a complementary solution: the operator manipulates objects directly, receives natural contact feedback, and records demonstrations without bringing the complete robot system into the collection environment [3–6]. In our system, the wearable interface maps the operator’s fingers to the joint-command space of a fully actuated 12-DoF XHand. Preserving this correspondence can impose robot-derived finger spacing, joint clearances, and linkage geometry on the human hand. Such constraints are particularly consequential for the thumb, whose natural opposition and circumduction do not necessarily align with the target robot’s kinematic structure. The resulting mismatch can increase physical bur-den, restrict motion, and create localized discomfort during repeated data collection [7–10]. Unlike a passive sensing system, a wearable collection interface participates directly in the physical interaction that generates each demonstration. A restrictive linkage can change a grasp trajectory, while material surrounding afingertip sensor can prevent the tactile surface from reaching the object. The resulting difference may appear as a change in whether contact is accessible, where contact occurs on the pad, or how often contact is detected. Tactile representation further determines which of these differences a learned policy can receive: a binary aggregate input preserves per-finger contact occurrence while discarding within-pad contact lo-cation and force, whereas a spatial-plus-force input retains contact location on the pad and aggregate fingertip force [11– 16]. This motivates our central question: when a revision of wearable collection hardware changes physical contact during demonstration collection, how do the collection in-terface and the tactile representation each affect downstream robot policy performance? We study this question through a controlled hand-side revision within a robot-aligned wearable interface. The baseline and revised interfaces retain the same 12-DoF XHand command definition, encoder assignment, calibration procedure, tactile module type, and robot plat-form, while differing in hand-side geometry. We first test whether the revision changes operator experience and tactile-pad accessibility. We then measure whether it changes where or how often contact is recorded in matched demonstrations. Finally, we train policies that cross the two collection inter-faces with a binary aggregate or a spatial-plus-force input and evaluate them on the same XHand, which measures the contribution of each factor to policy success. A. Demonstration Collection Interfaces 

Dexterous demonstrations can be collected from human observations, motion capture, and robot-mediated interfaces. Human video scales to diverse behavior, and motion-capture systems such as DexCap improve wrist and finger tracking without the target robot, but both still require human motion and contact to be converted into robot-feasible actions [1]. Robot-mediated teleoperation and hand-over-hand collection provide commands closer to the deployment platform but retain dependence on robot hardware during collection [2]. Portable handheld and visuotactile systems reduce that dependence and support direct object interaction through gripper-like or task-specific collection embodiments [17–21]. Wearable interfaces maintain direct correspondence with a target hand while allowing the operator to manipulate objects through their own fingers. Representative systems use robot-specific kinematics, shared mechanisms, or wearable sensing and feedback to acquire transferable multifinger demonstra-tions [3–7, 22]. This correspondence also places the collec-tion hardware inside the physical interaction. Wearability-oriented systems such as DexEXO and WHED introduce new exoskeleton designs for comfortable, natural collection and evaluate each design as a complete system [5, 8]. We instead hold the robot command, calibration, and sensing fixed and compare the tactile data and policies produced before and after a controlled hand-side revision. 

B. Mechanical Correspondence and Human Interaction 

Mechanical correspondence makes wearable demonstra-tions transferable, but it also allows robot-hand geometry to constrain the operator. Finger spacing, joint axes, clearances, and thumb geometry designed around a robot’s configuration can restrict natural finger trajectories or create localized pres-sure. Prior exoskeleton systems therefore emphasize passive accommodation, wearability, calibration, or returned haptic and fingertip cues [8–10, 23–25]. For demonstration learning, these constraints can also change the data itself. A restricted finger trajectory can alter a grasp, while material surrounding a fingertip sensor can prevent a physically possible contact from being measured. We therefore treat operator experience and recorded contact as two consequences of the same hand-side interface revi-sion. 

C. Tactile Representation and Downstream Learning 

Prior tactile policies use binary contact signals, per-finger force summaries, dense taxel arrays, spatial features, and multiresolution tactile representations [11–16, 26, 27]. These inputs preserve different properties of physical interaction. A binary aggregate signal can retain whether a finger is in con-tact while discarding within-pad location and force distribu-tion, whereas a spatial tactile representation can retain those distinctions. A contact difference can therefore be present in the recorded tactile array yet disappear before reaching the policy. We compare two complete policy inputs—binary aggregate and spatial plus force—derived from the same tactile measurements and evaluated on the same robot. III. I NTERFACE REVISION AND POLICY INPUTS

B. Shared XHand Command and Collection Pipeline 

Both interfaces use the same 12-DoF XHand command definition, encoder-channel assignment, mapping code, five-keypose calibration procedure, control frequency, tactile module type, and robot platform (Table I). The released DexUMI pipeline replays recorded actions on the physical XHand and applies segmentation, inpainting, and robot-hand compositing [3]. Visual processing is identical across all policy comparisons, so the experimental factors remain interface version and tactile representation. 

C. Recorded Data and Tactile Policy Inputs 

Each demonstration records raw wrist-camera frames, wrist pose, encoder angles, and a 3×120 ×3 tactile array from the thumb, index, and middle fingertips, where each of the 120 taxels measures three-axis force. IV. E VALUATION PROTOCOL 

The participant study asks whether the hand-side revi-sion changes operator burden and device experience. The accessibility test asks whether the revision changes which fingertip contacts can be physically recorded. The shared-task demonstration analysis asks whether the revision changes contact location or contact occurrence. A dataset-separability diagnostic tests which tactile feature family most clearly distinguishes the two interface datasets. Finally, matched robot rollouts measure how the collection interface and the tactile representation each affect policy success. 

A. Operator Experience and Tactile Accessibility 

Twenty (N=20) right-handed participants evaluated both interfaces in a randomized within-subject study. The protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and all participants gave informed consent (IRB number hidden for anonymous review). Participants were assigned to a baseline-first or revision-first order, with version labels and design intent concealed. With each interface, they completed lid twisting and egg carton opening before providing workload and device-experience ratings. Workload was assessed using the six raw NASA-TLX dimensions on a 0–20 scale [28], with Performance anchored so that higher values indicate better self-rated performance; overall workload is the unweighted mean of the six di-mensions with Performance reversed. Device experience was assessed using six custom items with the five-point response format used by SUS [29]. Physical Demand was the prespec-ified primary outcome. Paired differences were tested with two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with paired sign-flip tests as a sensitivity analysis, and we report standardized paired differences dz . The remaining NASA-TLX outcomes and the device-specific items were corrected within their respective families using false-discovery-rate (FDR) control. As a task-specific accessibility check, we used the preci-sion grasp on the soldering-tool handle shown in Fig. 1b. This test asks whether the revised fingertip housing changes the physical ability of the tactile pad to reach the object. B. Demonstration Collection and Contact Measures 

We collected lid-twisting and egg-carton-opening demon-strations from human participants with both interfaces for the contact analysis and policy training. A session is a consecutive block of demonstrations recorded in one sitting. Demonstrations with the two interfaces were collected al-ternately rather than in two separate phases. The contact analysis is computed at the session level over 20 sessions per interface for lid twisting and 16 baseline and 17 revised sessions for egg carton opening; baseline egg-carton sessions recorded before an encoder repair were excluded before analysis. Soldering tool pick and place (311 demonstrations) and USB insertion (319 demonstrations) were collected only with the revised interface and enter the robot evaluation (Fig. 3). Both tasks require grasping thin objects: with the baseline fingertip housing, which extends about 5.5 mm beyond the tactile pad, contact falls on the housing before the pad, so baseline demonstrations would not provide valid tactile data for the representation comparison. The collection session is the unit of inference. Demon-stration quantities are averaged within session, differences between collection conditions are tested with session-level permutation tests (20 versus 20 sessions on lid, 16 versus 17 on egg), and standardized differences are computed on session means. An automated check screened every saved demonstration for encoder-reference drift, encoder or tactile dropouts, flat channels, and insufficient camera overlap. C. Dataset Separability Diagnostic 

As a supporting diagnostic, a linear classifier predicts interface version from demonstration-level tactile summaries while holding out complete collection sessions. We compare contact-location features with contact-amount-and-timing features. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) measures how clearly each feature family separates the two datasets, with shuffled interface labels providing a chance control. These diagnostic features are distinct from the policy inputs. 

D. Policy Evaluation 

On each shared task we use Diffusion Policy [30] to train four policies by crossing collection interface with the binary aggregate and spatial-plus-force inputs. All policies share the released DexUMI configuration [3]: a DINO ViT-S/8 image encoder, fine-tuned with the policy, embeds one 224 ×224 

wrist-camera image; the tactile vector is concatenated with this embedding as the global condition of a 1-D temporal U-Net, which predicts 16 future actions (wrist pose and 12 hand-motor commands) from a single observation frame. No proprioceptive input is used. Each policy is trained for 400 epochs with an effective batch size of 297. USB insertion and soldering tool pick and place use revised-interface demon-strations under both inputs, giving twelve policy–task cells in all. Each shared-task condition trains on a matched budget of 341 lid-twisting and 252 egg-carton demonstrations; after the tactile quality screen, the larger dataset of each task is randomly subsampled to this budget. All policies deploy on the same 12-DoF XHand mounted on a Franka Emika robot arm. The collection interface enters only through the training data. Each policy–task cell is evaluated in 20 rollouts over the same pre-generated task-specific initial-state list. A rollout is successful under the following criteria:

V. R ESULTS 

A. Operator Experience and Tactile Accessibility 

The revised interface reduced the reported physical burden of data collection (Fig. 4). Mean Physical Demand decreased from 11 .0±5.5 under the baseline interface to 7.5±4.3 under the revised interface ( dz = −0.61 , p = 0 .017 ). Among the secondary workload outcomes, Effort and overall workload decreased (both q = 0.009 ) and self-rated Performance improved from 13.0 to 16.6 ( q = 0 .024 ), whereas Mental Demand, Temporal Demand, and Frustration did not differ after correction. Device ratings showed higher Comfort, increasing from 2.0 ± 0.9 to 3.5 ± 1.0 (dz = 1 .14 , q =0.002 ), together with lower motion restriction and greater willingness to reuse the device. These results show that the revision achieved its operator-side design objective. Trimming the fingertip housing also restores tactile-pad access in the evaluated precision grasp of the soldering-tool handle. Under the baseline geometry, the housing edge reaches the handle before the tactile pad, preventing direct tactile-pad contact; the flush revised hood allows the pad to contact the handle directly (Fig. 2c; Fig. 1b), enabling the soldering-tool demonstrations used in the robot evaluation. B. Task-Dependent Contact Changes 

The same interface revision produces different tactile consequences across the shared tasks. Lid twisting primarily changes where thumb contact occurs, whereas egg carton opening primarily changes how often index contact is de-tected. 

Contact location. During lid twisting, the thumb shows the clearest change in contact location. The session-mean contact centroid shifts by 8.6 mm in the pad plane from the baseline to the revised interface, including 7.4 mm along the pad long axis, with a session-level permutation p < 0.001 

(Fig. 5, left); the shift remains 8.3 and 8.2 mm at τ = 5 and 

τ = 2 . This establishes a task-specific spatial change in the recorded tactile interaction. 

Contact occurrence. Egg carton opening shows a differ-ent pattern. Index contact frame rate increases from 0.09 un-der the baseline interface to 0.18 under the revised interface, with an effect size of d = 1 .15 on session means ( p = 0 .003 ,

q = 0 .011 ; Fig. 5, right). The number of active taxels during detected contact changes little, from 2.7 to 3.0, indicating that the dominant difference is how often index contact is detected rather than the size of the active contact patch. 

Supporting diagnostic. The dataset-separability diagnos-tic supports the task-dependent interpretation. On lid twist-ing, contact-location features separate the interface datasets with AUROC 0.99, compared with 0.84 for contact-amount-and-timing features. On egg carton opening, the correspond-ing AUROCs are 0.82 and 0.80, indicating that both feature families retain comparable information about the interface difference. Shuffled labels remain near chance. The diagnos-tic therefore supports a predominantly spatial difference on lid twisting and a broader occurrence-related difference on carton opening.

The spatial-plus-force input achieves a higher success rate than binary aggregation in all six task–interface comparisons: by 0.20 and 0.15 on lid twisting with baseline and revised demonstra-tions, by 0.25 and 0.20 on egg carton opening, and by 0.10 on both USB insertion and soldering tool pick and place. Lid twisting and USB insertion remain the most difficult tasks. VI. D ISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Wearable geometry shapes data generation. Wearable collection hardware participates directly in the physical inter-action that generates a demonstration. The revised interface reduces operator burden and restores tactile-pad accessibility in a precision grasp that is blocked by the baseline geometry. On the shared tasks, it also changes the recorded tactile interaction in different ways, producing a spatial shift in thumb contact during lid twisting and a change in index contact occurrence during egg carton opening. These results show that wearable geometry affects both which interactions can be collected and what tactile evidence enters the dataset. 

Representation determines what remains available to the policy. The binary aggregate input preserves per-finger contact occurrence but discards within-pad contact location and force, whereas the spatial-plus-force input retains both. In the matched robot comparison, the spatial-plus-force input and revised-interface demonstrations each increase success significantly in the pooled comparisons, and the interface gain is of similar size under both inputs. Retaining the raw tactile arrays during collection makes this comparison possible without recollecting demonstrations. 

Remaining hardware constraints. A more wearable in-terface does not remove every constraint of robot-specific collection. Collection itself proceeds without the robot, but closing the visual gap between exoskeleton and robot-hand observations still relies on robot-side replay and image compositing, as in DexUMI [3]. Robot-hand precision also limits fine manipulation. Even with improved wearability, the precision of the robot hand and the mechanical constraints of the exoskeleton still limit which dexterous tasks can be collected with the interface and reproduced on the robot. 

Limitations. The study evaluates an integrated hand-side revision, so the individual effects of finger clearance, fingertip housing, and thumb geometry are not isolated. Each condition uses one trained policy instance, so rollout variability reflects initial-state variation rather than variation across policy training runs. The comparison between binary aggregate and spatial plus force is a representation-level comparison and does not isolate the contribution of spatial structure from force information within the richer input. The conclusions are limited to the evaluated objects and tasks. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This work studies wearable collection hardware as part of the data-generation process for dexterous robot learning. A controlled hand-side revision reduces reported operator burden, restores tactile-pad accessibility in a precision grasp, and produces task-dependent changes in recorded contact. Lid twisting primarily changes within-pad thumb contact lo-cation, whereas egg carton opening primarily changes index contact occurrence. In matched robot evaluation, revised-interface demonstrations and the spatial-plus-force input each increase policy success significantly in the pooled compar-isons, and the interface gain appears under both tactile inputs. Wearable collection systems should therefore be evaluated not only by how well they map human motion to robot commands, but also by what physical interactions they make recordable and what tactile information remains available for learning.
