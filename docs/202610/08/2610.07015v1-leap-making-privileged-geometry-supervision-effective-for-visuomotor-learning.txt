## ABSTRACT 

Privileged 3D supervision uses additional geometric information during training to guide RGB-based visuomotor policy learning, without requiring geometric in-puts at deployment. However, low reconstruction error does not ensure that visual representations capture geometry useful for control. We identify three limitations that weaken this supervision: proprioceptive shortcuts, dominant-view reliance, and reconstruction objectives dominated by task-irrelevant geometry. To address these limitations, we propose Latent Encoding with Aligned Privileged Geome-try (LEAP). Our framework uses an auxiliary decoder to reconstruct point clouds within the manipulation workspace from visual features alone, while retaining proprioception for action prediction. Alongside full reconstruction, we introduce wrist-view dropout and partial reconstruction targets matched to the retained wrist, encouraging the encoder to capture complementary local geometry. The auxiliary decoder is removed at inference, leaving only RGB observations and robot state as policy inputs. Extensive experiments on RoboTwin, ManiSkill, and real-world tasks demonstrate consistent and substantial improvements over Diffusion Policy and ACT, with only a small increase in parameter count and no reduction in infer-ence speed.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reliable robotic manipulation depends on the 3D relationships among objects and the robot. RGB-based imitation learning methods, such as Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023) and ACT (Zhao et al., 2023), learn complex skills from demonstrations, but their training objectives do not explicitly re-quire visual features to encode these relationships. Point-cloud policies such as DP3 (Ze et al., 2024) provide explicit geometric inputs, but require depth sensing and point-cloud processing at deployment, adding sensing requirements and computational cost. Privileged geometry supervision instead uses 3D information during training, through auxiliary geometric prediction (Li et al., 2025c; He et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2026) or representation alignment (Li et al., 2025b), while retaining an RGB policy at deployment. While RGB-based policies are easier to deploy, this line of work suffers from the following potential issue. Without explicit 3D observations at deployment, an RGB policy must infer task-relevant geometry from its images. A low geometric training loss does not establish that it has done so. In this paper, considering policies that take multi-view RGB images and robot state as input, we investigate the following two questions: (1) when does privileged supervision with depth-derived geometric information fail to make the visual representation capture task-relevant 3D structure? (2) how can privileged 3D supervision be formulated to circumvent this failure? To answer the first question, we identify three limitations of privileged geometry supervision: proprioceptive shortcuts ,

view dominance , and task-irrelevant global prediction . To address the second, we propose a novel training framework. We expand on those points next. A proprioceptive shortcut occurs when an auxiliary geometry decoder uses robot state to reduce reconstruction error without extracting the corresponding geometry from images. Such shortcuts have been studied in action prediction (Zhao et al., 2025), but their effect on privileged geometry supervision remains underexplored. View dominance occurs when the broad-coverage camera ex-plains much of a fused reconstruction target, leaving the contribution of other views unclear. Finally, 

task-irrelevant global prediction can devote much of the auxiliary reconstruction objective to back-ground and robot geometry while giving little weight to the local contact and occlusion geometry needed for control. Together, these limitations allow low geometric loss without ensuring that the visual features preserve the geometry relevant to manipulation. To overcome these limitations, we propose Latent Encoding with Aligned Privileged Geometry (LEAP), a training framework that directs privileged geometry supervision toward task-relevant visual learning. Its geometry decoder receives only visual features, limiting proprioceptive shortcuts. During training, we randomly mask views and matches a partial geometry target to the remaining views, making wrist contributions easier to identify. At deployment, the policy uses only multi-view RGB images and robot state. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We identify proprioceptive shortcuts, view dominance, and task-irrelevant global prediction as limitations of privileged geometry supervision for multi-view visuomotor learning. • We introduce LEAP, combining visual-only geometric reconstruction, view dropout with visibility-aligned partial reconstruction, and workspace-constrained supervision. 2• We evaluate these limitations and the corresponding designs through abundant controlled experiments and policy evaluations across simulation and real-robot settings with different RGB-based policy architectures. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Precise 3D manipulation can be achieved by directly using 3D observations or multiview RGB images as policy inputs, but also via geometric supervision. Next, we recall those three research directions and discuss related work on learning shortcuts. 

Explicit 3D Inputs for Robotic Manipulation. Shridhar et al. (2023) voxelize RGB-D inputs, Goyal et al. (2023) render virtual views from point clouds, and Gervet et al. (2023) use sensed depth to lift visual features into a 3D field. GNFactor shares a voxel representation between reconstruction and control (Ze et al., 2023b). Other policies encode point clouds (Ze et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a), predict trajectories from 3D scenes (Ke et al., 2024), or combine point maps with RGB (Jia et al., 2025). These approaches retain geometric observations or representations at deployment. LEAP uses geometry only for training supervision and acts from RGB and proprioception. 

Multi-View Learning and Camera Availability. Multi-view observations provide complemen-tary evidence for manipulation. Hsu et al. (2022) show that hand-centric and global views differ in their coverage and generalization behavior. MV-MWM (Seo et al., 2023) reconstructs masked views, while 3D-MVP (Qian et al., 2024) and DiffuView (Zhang et al., 2026a) use multi-view pre-training to learn spatial representations. MAD (Almuzairee et al., 2025) learns fused and per-view features for missing-camera robustness, and Multi-Camera View Scaling (Xie et al., 2026) uses syn-chronized views to increase the effective training data. Akinola et al. (2020) use camera dropout to make RGB policies robust to missing views. In contrast, our concern is the geometric objective under such masking: a low loss on a fused scene may leave the contribution of a wrist view unclear. 

Geometric Representation Learning for RGB Policies. Geometric supervision can improve RGB policies without requiring 3D observations at inference. DPR and SPA pretrain encoders through depth prediction and multi-view rendering, while eVGGT and GP3 transfer pretrained ge-ometric knowledge (Wang et al., 2024b; Zhu et al., 2024; Vuong et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025b). Wang et al. (2025) distill depth-guided spatial, temporal, and action cues into an RGB policy. Dur-ing policy learning, methods predict depth, future scenes, or 3D motion (Li et al., 2025c; He et al., 2026a; Qian et al., 2025a; Kim et al., 2026; He et al., 2026b), align features with geometric teach-ers (Li et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2026b;a; Shi et al., 2026), or use training-only 3D Gaussian prediction heads (Jiang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c). Other methods focus on interaction-relevant structure: object geometry (Ding et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026), subtask-relevant regions (Gao et al., 2026), or affordances and goal regions (Zheng et al., 2026). Yang et al. (2026a) combine depth and cross-view correspondence supervision, while Xu et al. (2026) jointly predict actions and future point maps from features that include proprioception. LEAP instead reconstructs workspace and view-matched partial point clouds from visual features alone, encouraging geometric learning from complementary camera views. 

Proprioceptive Shortcuts in Visuomotor Learning. Prior work identifies proprioceptive over-reliance (Lin et al., 2024) and optimization-driven visual suppression (Lu et al., 2026). State-free Policy (Zhao et al., 2025) removes proprioception to improve spatial generalization, while ReViP (Li et al., 2026) rebalances vision and proprioception to reduce false task completion. We instead exam-ine, both theoretically and empirically, how proprioceptive shortcuts can weaken privileged point-cloud supervision in multi-view imitation learning. To mitigate these shortcuts and focus supervision on task-relevant geometry, LEAP reconstructs workspace point clouds from visual features alone and supplements full reconstruction with visibility-aligned partial supervision under wrist-view dropout. This design encourages complementary local geometric representations while retaining propriocep-tion for action prediction. 33 BACKGROUND

## 4 METHOD 

We first identify three limitations of privileged geometric supervision: proprioceptive shortcuts, dominant-view reliance, and reconstruction objectives dominated by task-irrelevant geometry. These issues can allow low reconstruction error without ensuring that visual features capture the geometry needed for control. Guided by this analysis, we introduce LEAP, which aims to make geometric su-pervision an observation-aligned and policy-relevant constraint on visual representations. As shown in Figure 3, its core principles are to separate geometric learning from proprioceptive shortcuts, en-courage complementary information across views, and focus supervision on task-relevant geometry. The resulting policy requires only RGB observations and proprioceptive state at deployment. 4.1 WHEN DOES PRIVILEGED GEOMETRY BENEFIT POLICY LEARNING ?Low reconstruction error alone cannot indicate that a visual representation captures the geometry required for control. Proprioceptive Shortcut. Recent methods (Xu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026b) predict privileged geometry from both visual features and robot state. Robot state determines the robot’s configuration and may correlate with scene geometry, allowing the decoder to reduce reconstruction error without requiring visual features to encode the corresponding geometry. We investigate this shortcut using a DP policy trained with state-conditioned geometry reconstruc-tion on RoboTwin. With the encoder and decoder frozen, we replace either robot state or visual features with their training-set mean ( SMean , ZMean ) or shuffle them across evaluation samples (SShuffle , ZShuffle ), keeping the other input and reconstruction target unchanged. We measure re-construction quality using squared Chamfer error, which averages mean squared nearest-neighbor errors between predicted and target point clouds. As shown in Figure 2(a), both state interventions sharply increase reconstruction error, whereas the corresponding visual interventions produce much smaller increases. This difference suggests that the trained decoder relies strongly on proprioception, although it does not establish that visual features are completely invalid. Dominant-View Shortcut. Even when reconstruction uses only visual inputs, the representation may rely primarily on a subset of views. The head camera provides broad scene coverage, while 5wrist cameras reveal complementary local geometry. If the head view already explains most recon-struction target, low reconstruction error cannot indicate the contribution of wrist observations. We examine whether wrist features improve object-position prediction using frozen visual repre-sentations. For the DP baseline trained with full point-cloud reconstruction, DP Encoder uses only head-view features Zh, whereas DP Encoder (Multi-view) uses concatenated features [Zh, Z l, Z r ]

from the head and both wrist cameras. We additionally evaluate LEAP (Multi-view) , which uses the same three-view feature configuration but with representations learned through LEAP. In all settings, camera-specific features are extracted before fusion and kept frozen while a small MLP is trained to predict task-object 3D positions. We report mean Euclidean error on held-out episodes over three random seeds for MLP training. As shown in Figure 2(b), adding wrist features to the full-reconstruction DP baseline does not reduce mean prediction error on either task, whereas LEAP achieves lower mean error using the same three-view feature configuration. These results suggest that simply including wrist features does not guarantee better object-position prediction, motivat-ing supervision that encourages complementary geometric information across views. The method combines grounding geometry in visual representations, learning complementary geometry with visibility-aligned partial reconstruction, and workspace-constrained geometric supervision.

State-conditioned reconstruction can exploit proprioceptive information without requiring the corresponding geometry in visual features (Propo-sition 4.1). ## 5 EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets and Tasks. We evaluate LEAP on 50 RoboTwin tasks, six ManiSkill tasks, and three real-world manipulation tasks. To examine performance across different geometric requirements, we group the RoboTwin tasks into four categories: Object Spatial (14), Multi-object (14), Interaction 

(14), and Less-geometry (8). We report success rates for each category and the overall average across all 50 tasks. The ManiSkill and real-world experiments provide complementary evaluations across simulation environments and physical manipulation settings. More details in Section C.2. 

Baseline Methods. We compare against Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) (Zhao et al., 2023) and Diffusion Policy (DP) (Chi et al., 2023) as standard RGB-based imitation learning base-lines. We also include eVGGT (Vuong et al., 2025), GAP (Xu et al., 2026), and Spatial Forcing (Li et al., 2025b) as geometry-aware approaches; DP3 (Ze et al., 2024), which directly consumes point-cloud, as a geometry-input upper bound; and π0 (Black et al., 2025) and RDT (Liu et al., 2025) as robot foundation models. For the DP-based comparisons, we integrate the eVGGT visual en-coder and adapt Spatial Forcing’s representation-alignment strategy to DP, denoting these variants as eVGGT-DP and Spatial Forcing-DP. We apply LEAP to both policy architectures, yielding DP-LEAP and ACT-LEAP, to evaluate its effectiveness across different action prediction frameworks. 

Training and Evaluation. Our RGB policies use observations from three cameras, including one head camera and two wrist cameras, together with robot proprioceptive state. We train DP-based policies for 600 epochs and ACT-based policies for 4,000 epochs, using three independent random seeds. For LEAP, privileged point-cloud supervision is used only during training; inference requires only RGB observations and proprioception. We summarize performance using task success rates, reporting the mean and standard deviation across seeds. More implementation details in Section C. 5.2 PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS

## 6 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATION 

We presented LEAP, which improves RGB visuomotor learning through visual-only reconstruction, workspace-constrained targets, and wrist-view dropout with aligned partial supervision alongside full reconstruction. Across abundant experiments, it improves Diffusion Policy and ACT in all four categories, achieving average performance comparable to their point-cloud counterparts while requiring only RGB and robot state at deployment. Ablations support each component, highlighting the value of observation-aligned, task-relevant geometric supervision. The current implementation uses predefined workspace bounds; automating their selection remains future work.

Structured 3D Manipulation. Zhu et al. (2023) use object-centric 3D representations to improve robustness to background and camera changes. Lift3D reconstructs depth in task-relevant image regions before adapting a pretrained 2D model to encode point clouds (Jia et al., 2024). PointVLA injects point-cloud features into a pretrained vision-language-action model (Li et al., 2025a). These methods retain explicit 3D representations for policy inference, whereas LEAP uses geometry as training supervision for a policy that takes RGB images and proprioception as inputs. 

Other Routes to Spatial Supervision. Ze et al. (2023a) pretrain voxel-based 3D representations before visual reinforcement learning, while Zhou et al. (2023) use NeRF-synthesized wrist views and corrective actions to augment demonstrations for an RGB policy. Depth Helps trains with depth and predicts virtual depth from RGB at inference (Pang et al., 2024). ReconVLA instead reconstructs image regions around manipulated objects to focus visual attention (Song et al., 2025). LEAP uses full and view-matched partial point-cloud reconstruction as auxiliary supervision during policy training. 

Camera Fusion and View-Specific Features. Jangir et al. (2022) combine external and wrist images through cross-view attention. Multi-view Disentanglement learns both shared and camera-specific features to support deployment with fewer views (Dunion & Albrecht, 2024). Cortical Pol-icy combines geometrically aligned static-view features with a dynamic egocentric stream (Zhang et al., 2026b). LEAP addresses a complementary question by matching its partial geometric target to the head–wrist views retained during masking. 

State Conditioning and Geometric Prediction. de Haan et al. (2019) show how predictive ob-servation cues can lead imitation policies to learn incorrect causal relationships. Robo3R uses RGB images and robot state to reconstruct metric scene geometry for downstream manipulation (Yang et al., 2026b). In LEAP, reconstruction serves as supervision for the visual encoder, so the auxiliary decoder excludes robot state while the action predictor retains it.

C.3 HYPERPARAMETERS 

Hyperparameters. We train DP-LEAP and ACT-LEAP on 50 demonstrations per RoboTwin task. Both policies take RGB images from a head camera and two wrist cameras, together with a 14-D robot state. Each camera has an independently trained ResNet-18 encoder, and a two-layer trans-former fuses the visual features. During training, we retain the head view and sample one wrist view for partial geometry supervision. A visual-only decoder reconstructs full and partial 1024-point clouds in the world frame; the geometry loss uses a 10-epoch linear warmup. DP-LEAP uses a con-ditional 1-D U-Net and is trained for 600 epochs, while ACT-LEAP uses a CVAE Transformer and is trained for 4000 epochs. Point clouds and the geometry decoder are used only during training.

Data Collection and Evaluation Protocol. We collect 50 expert trajectories per RoboTwin task, 100 expert trajectories per ManiSkill task, and 50 expert trajectories per real-world task. We compute the success rate separately for each seed and report the mean and standard deviation across the three seeds. For real-world ex-periments, we use a single random seed and evaluate each policy over 20 rollouts per task, reporting the percentage of successful trials. C.4 ONE -A RM CAMERA CONFIGURATION 

For one-arm tasks, we use three RGB views: a head camera h, a wrist camera w, and an additional outside camera o with broad scene coverage. Because either h or o remains available when the other is dropped, we uniformly mask any one of the three cameras: 

M ∈ {h, w }, {o, w }, {h, o } . (16) When the wrist is retained, we construct the partial target from wrist-visible points, as above. When the wrist is masked, we instead use points approximately visible from the retained outside cam-era. Thus, the partial target is always aligned with an available view, while the two configurations containing the wrist encourage the policy to use its local geometric observations.
