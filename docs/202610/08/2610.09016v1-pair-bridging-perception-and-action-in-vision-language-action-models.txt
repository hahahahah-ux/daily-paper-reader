## ABSTRACT 

Vision–language–action (VLA) models map visual observations and language in-structions to continuous robot actions. This task requires a transition from repre-sentations that describe the scene and instruction to representations that support action generation. Many continuous-action VLAs leave this transition implicit and supervise it mainly through the final action-prediction loss. We introduce PAIR ,a framework that learns a shared perception–action representation between these two spaces. During training, a Masked Action Autoencoder encodes expert action chunks into horizon-aligned Action Latent Tokens. A Bridge Module extracts task-relevant features from the current visual–language representations. PAIR aligns these features with the Action Latent Tokens to form Bridge Tokens that preserve task information and capture the structure of expert actions. The Bridge Tokens are then projected into the action-token space and injected into the initial Action Tokens, providing an action-ready starting point for Action Expert refine-ment. At inference, the autoencoder is removed, and the Bridge Tokens are gener-ated only from the current observation and instruction. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and CALVIN ABC →D show gains for the evaluated OpenVLA-OFT and VLA-Adapter models. On LIBERO-Plus, PAIR raises VLA-Adapter’s success rate from 59.1% to 64.2%. On CALVIN, it increases VLA-Adapter’s average completed sequence length from 4.42 to 4.53. Across seven real-world tasks, PAIR raises OpenVLA-OFT’s success rate from 51.4% to 65.0%. Repre-sentation analyses show that Bridge Tokens retain task information while making continuous-action information accessible before Action Expert refinement. These results support a shared intermediate representation as a useful interface between perception and action in continuous-action VLAs. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies have emerged as a promising framework for robot control. By mapping visual observations and language instructions to continuous actions, they unify visual grounding, language understanding, and control within a single policy. Recent models have achieved strong performance across diverse manipulation tasks (Brohan et al., 2022; 2023; Kim et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2026). Yet this unified formulation hides a difficult representational transition. The visual-language perception space and the action space are organized around fundamentally different semantics. The former describes the current world and the intended task, including scene content, object relations, and instruction-level intent. The latter specifies how the robot should act through temporally ordered, executable control. We refer to this semantic mismatch as the 

perception–action representation gap .Existing VLA policies learn to traverse this gap primarily through end-to-end action supervision. The final action loss specifies which action should be produced, while the representation that con-nects visual-language semantics to action semantics is left implicit. Action tokens must therefore discover within the policy both what information is relevant to control and how it should be reorga-nized into an action-ready representation. Recent work has sought to facilitate this transition by exposing control-relevant structure through visual traces, affordances, visual reasoning, and learned latent actions (Zheng et al., 2025; Nasiriany et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026b). This raises a central question: 

Can we learn the missing intermediate representation directly from perception and action—a bridge that carries task semantics from one space and executable structure from the other? 

We introduce PAIR, a framework for learning a Perception–Action Intermediate Representation 

directly from paired robot demonstrations. From the action side, a Masked Action Autoencoder encodes expert action chunks into Action Latent Tokens. Reconstructing the demonstrated actions requires these tokens to preserve the temporal and executable structure. From the perception side, a Bridge Module condenses the current visual-language representations into compact, task-relevant features. Aligning these features with the Action Latent Tokens transfers action structure into the perception-derived representation, forming Bridge Tokens that jointly encode task semantics and action information. Perception supplies the semantic content, expert actions supply the executable structure, and alignment integrates both into a unified bridge. PAIR then projects the Bridge Tokens into the action-token space and injects them at the begin-ning of action decoding. This early Bridge Injection primes the action tokens with task-aware and action-structured information, providing an explicit starting point for the perception-to-action tran-sition. The policy can then refine this prior through its original action-decoding process rather than constructing an action-ready representation from the final action objective alone. Experiments on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2026), and CALVIN ABC →D(Mees et al., 2022) demonstrate consistent gains across OpenVLA-OFT and VLA-Adapter. PAIR raises the average success rate of VLA-Adapter from 97.3% to 98.4% on LIBERO and from 59.1% to 64.2% on LIBERO-Plus. On CALVIN, it improves the average completed sequence length from 4.28 to 4.40 with OpenVLA-OFT and from 4.42 to 4.53 with VLA-Adapter. Across seven real-world tasks, PAIR further raises the average success rate of OpenVLA-OFT from 51.4% to 65.0%. These improvements span standard manipulation, zero-shot distribution shifts and long-horizon ex-ecution. Representation analyses further show that Bridge Tokens retain task context while exposing action structure before decoding, supporting their role as an explicit interface between perception and action. 

Contributions. Our contributions are as follows: • We characterize the perception–action representation gap in continuous-action VLAs as the im-plicit transition from visual–language semantics to executable action semantics under final action supervision. 2• We introduce PAIR, which learns perception-derived Bridge Tokens whose task semantics are organized by expert-action structure, and uses early Bridge Injection to provide Action Tokens with an action-aware starting point for refinement. • We demonstrate consistent gains across standard manipulation, zero-shot distribution shifts, long-horizon execution, and real-world control, together with representation-level evidence that Bridge Tokens retain task semantics while exposing action structure before Action Expert re-finement. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Intermediate representations for perception-to-action learning. Intermediate representations make control-relevant structure explicit before action prediction. Motion-oriented approaches en-code trajectories, visual traces, or future scene changes (Niu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Spatial representations introduce geometric information into perception and action modeling (Qu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), while affordance-based methods describe possible inter-actions or key robot configurations (Nasiriany et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2026). These approaches define the intermediate representation through a chosen physical abstraction, such as motion, geometry, or affordance. PAIR does not prescribe a particular physical meaning for the bridge. Its content is learned from the two spaces it is meant to connect: visual–language context provides task seman-tics, while expert actions organize the representation around executable structure. In this way, task context and action structure jointly determine what the intermediate representation retains. 

Latent action representations and tokenization. Latent action methods learn compact repre-sentations of robot behavior for pretraining, prediction, and control. LAPA derives discrete latent actions from visual transitions for VLA pretraining without robot action labels (Ye et al., 2025), while UniVLA learns task-centric latent actions for policy learning (Bu et al., 2025). FAST and VQ-VLA tokenize continuous action chunks for token-based policies (Pertsch et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a). These approaches primarily structure how actions themselves are represented, tok-enized, or predicted. LARA (Liu et al., 2026b) moves closer to the perception–action interface by aligning latent visual dynamics with intermediate Action Expert features during generation. PAIR targets a different object: a perception-derived intermediate representation that is shaped by expert-action structure during training and made available before Action Expert refinement begins. 

## 3 METHOD 

We consider a robot demonstration consisting of a visual observation o, a language instruction ℓ,and an H-step expert action chunk A ∈ RH×da . The VLA backbone encodes the observation and instruction into visual–language representations HV L = fV L (o, ℓ ), which condition an Action Expert to predict continuous actions. A useful representation between perception and action should carry two complementary forms of in-formation: the task semantics that determine what behavior is required, and the executable structure that determines how the behavior should unfold. As illustrated in Figure 2, PAIR learns these prop-erties from the two sides of the transition. A Masked Action Autoencoder captures action structure from expert trajectories, while a Bridge Module extracts compact task-relevant information from the visual–language context. Aligning their horizon-wise representations forms Bridge Tokens, which are injected into Action Tokens at the beginning of the Action Expert. At inference, the Action Autoencoder is removed and the Bridge Module predicts the task-conditioned, horizon-wise tokens directly from the current observation and instruction. 3.1 MASKED ACTION AUTOENCODER 

We first construct the action side of the intermediate representation. The desired representation should preserve the information that makes an expert trajectory executable, including its control content and temporal organization. The two training stages follow the construction of the intermediate representation. The first stage learns action structure by optimizing the Masked Action Autoencoder with LAE . The second stage freezes the autoencoder and transfers this structure to the perception-derived Bridge Tokens while learning to use them inside the policy. Let Lact denote the original continuous action prediction loss of the VLA policy. The second-stage objective is 

LPAIR = Lact + λalign Lalign , (6) where λalign controls the contribution of the representation alignment objective. The VLA policy and bridge module are optimized jointly in this stage. During inference, the Bridge Module extracts Bridge Tokens from the current visual–language rep-resentations. Bridge Injection then primes the Action Tokens at the beginning of the Action Expert for continuous action prediction.

Benchmarks and metrics. LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) evaluates task-conditioned manipulation across the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites, which vary spatial relationships, manipulated ob-jects, task goals, and action-sequence length. We evaluate each task over 50 rollouts from the official initial states and report success rates for each suite and their average. Following the official protocol, LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2026) evaluates the corresponding LIBERO-trained checkpoints zero-shot under changes in camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language instruction, lighting, background, sensor noise, and object layout. CALVIN ABC →D (Mees et al., 2022) trains policies in environ-ments A–C and evaluates 1,000 five-task chains in unseen environment D. We report success at completing at least one to five consecutive tasks and average completed sequence length, capturing both individual-task performance and sustained execution. Additional protocols are in Appendix C. 

Backbones and implementation. We evaluate PAIR with VLA-Adapter (Wang et al., 2026) on LIBERO/Plus, both VLA-Adapter and OpenVLA-OFT (Kim et al., 2025a) on CALVIN, and OpenVLA-OFT on the real robot. Each + PAIR row augments the preceding backbone. We train on four NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPUs; detailed training settings are provided in Appendix C. 4.2 SIMULATION RESULTS

## 5 CONCLUSION 

PAIR introduces an explicit intermediate representation for the perception-to-action transition in continuous-action VLAs. Bridge Tokens combine visual–language context with action structure and prime Action Tokens at the entrance of the Action Expert. Across LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, CALVIN, and seven real-world tasks, PAIR improves two VLA backbones in standard manipulation, zero-shot generalization, long-horizon execution, and physical control. Representation analyses show that the learned bridge carries both task and action information, while ablations identify action alignment and early injection as its key ingredients. These results establish explicit perception– action intermediates as a practical design principle for VLA policies. 9AI U SE STATEMENT

Additional settings. The Action Autoencoder is trained for 50k steps on LIBERO demonstrations for LIBERO and zero-shot LIBERO-Plus, on CALVIN demonstrations for CALVIN, and separately on each task’s demonstrations for the seven real-world tasks. We use AdamW for both stages. The Action Autoencoder uses weight decay 10 −4 and clips the gradient norm at 1.0. Policy training uses bfloat16, image augmentation, and alignment weight λalign = 0 .1.

PAIR architecture. The Action Autoencoder comprises a two-layer pre-norm Transformer en-coder and a one-layer pre-norm Transformer decoder, both using self-attention over the action chunk with four attention heads. During training, action steps are randomly masked with probability 0.5 and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.05 is added before reconstruction. Both OpenVLA-OFT and VLA-Adapter use eight Bridge Tokens, matching the eight action-step tokens at the in-put to their final Action Experts. The Bridge Module forms them from learned horizon-aligned queries using one pre-norm cross-attention block over the visual–language tokens, followed by one pre-norm self-attention block; both use eight attention heads and residual MLP blocks. The align-ment and injection projections are two-layer MLPs with GELU activations. Injection is scaled by a zero-initialized per-token gate implemented by layer normalization, a scalar linear projection, and a tanh nonlinearity. C.2 SIMULATION BENCHMARKS 

LIBERO. The Action Autoencoder is trained on LIBERO data, and the VLA-Adapter policy is trained on the corresponding suite. LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) evaluates task-conditioned manip-ulation across the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites. These suites vary spatial relationships, manipulated objects, task goals, and the length of the required action sequence. We evaluate each task for 50 rollouts from the official initial states using 256 ×256 environment observations, and report each suite’s success rate and their average. 

LIBERO-Plus. Following the official evaluation protocol (Fei et al., 2026), we directly eval-uate the corresponding LIBERO-trained checkpoints zero-shot. The seven perturbation cate-gories cover camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language instruction, lighting, background, sen-sor noise, and object layout. Per-category and overall success assess generalization beyond the original training conditions. 

CALVIN. We use the ABC →D split (Mees et al., 2022): policies train in environments A–C and execute 1,000 five-task chains in unseen environment D. OpenVLA-OFT executes each eight-step 15 action chunk open-loop before replanning. We report success at completing at least one to five con-secutive tasks and average completed sequence length, measuring both individual-task performance and sustained execution.
