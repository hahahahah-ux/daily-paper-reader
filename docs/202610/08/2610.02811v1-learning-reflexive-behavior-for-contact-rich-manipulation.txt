# Learning Reflexive Behavior for Contact-Rich Manipulation 

Quan Nguyen, Yunho Kim, Joonho Lee ∗

All authors are with Neuromeka Co., Ltd. * Corresponding author. Email: joonho.lee@neuromeka.com .

Abstract — During contact-rich manipulation, interactions be-tween a robot and its environment carry information about local geometry: a surface prevents penetration, a bore guides a peg. A controller that exploits these interactions can can comply with environmental constraints while preserving task intent. Robot-learning systems commonly use position, hybrid force–position, or Cartesian impedance control. Their prescribed tracking objectives, stiffness, or force-control directions may not match local constraints and may degrade performance. We learn a proprioceptive reflex policy in simulation on three simple interaction primitives: a spring, a plane, and a rail. The policy maps task-space commands to joint-position targets using state history, without direct force or geometrical measurements. Once trained, it serves as a frozen execution layer beneath higher-level controllers. We evaluate it in dual-arm box lifting, peg insertion, and surface following. In box lifting, the reflex kept the force below the threshold while the baseline failed. In rough-surface following it stayed below the 10 N reference, on par with tuned hybrid force-position control. In 0.02 mm peg insertion It reduced mean estimated contact force to less than half that of the baseline while increasing hardware success rates from at most 22 % to 36–58 %. By separating contact response from command generation, the reflex policy provides motion planners, learned policies, and teleoperators with robust contact-rich execution. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In contact-rich manipulation, environmental geometry shapes how a robot or its tools can move and provides guidance for completing a task. A surface supports sliding while restricting penetration, and a groove guides motion along a path. By maintaining contact while yielding in constrained directions, a robot can exploit this geometry to guide execution despite errors in perception, grasping, or nominal motion. High-level commands from a motion planner, a learned policy, or a human operator may specify the intended behav-ior without matching the exact geometry of the current task instance [1]–[3]. For example, a surface-search command is useful only if execution maintains contact while moving over the actual surface. Li and Brock [1] separate task structure from instance-specific geometry and recover the latter through interaction with environmental constraints. Compliant execution beneath high-level policies similarly supports adaptation to local contact [2]. These observations motivate an execution capability that makes environmental constraints useful to a broad range of command sources. We present a method for training a proprioceptive reflex policy through reinforcement learning (RL) using three prim-itive interaction scenarios shown in Fig. 1 (bottom). The results show that the learned reflex supports stable lifting under imperfect motion commands, enables insertion under hole-position uncertainty, and follows rough surfaces while lim-iting contact loading. Our contribution is a reusable reflex-training paradigm that uses simple interaction primitives to train policies that adapt task-level motion commands to local contact. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Environmental Constraints and Compliant Manipulation 

Li and Brock [1] represent manipulation with environ-mental constraint (EC) primitives, recover instance geometry through exploratory interaction, and execute through compli-ant contact; their plane primitive, for example, maintains sur-face contact during motion and search. Minimalist Compli-ance Control [2] places actuator-based wrench estimation and task-space admittance beneath visual, imitation, and model-based policies. Both separate task intent from the response to local contact, the division our reflex adopts. Impedance control specifies a mechanical relationship between motion error and interaction force [7], and admittance control turns measured force into reference motion for an inner position loop [8]; these classical schemes are the reference points against which we position a learned reflex. We ask whether learning from structured interactions improves on them when the nominal geometry is imperfect. 

B. Learned Interaction Control 

FORGE [4] conditions assembly policies on a maximum allowable contact force and penalizes simulated force above that threshold during RL.The policy learns to modulate its actions so that the threshold is respected regardless of controller gains. This force-thresholded RL formulation is the central insight our training objective shares: the excess-force cost in Sec. III-B plays the role of the FORGE penalty, and the peg-insertion study (Sec. IV-C) builds on its assembly setup. Portela et al. [9] and Zhi et al. [5] learn force or position–force control for legged manipulators from robot-state history without force sensors. Zhi et al. reward tracking of a target position that is offset from the command by the net force through a static impedance relation, so that position tracking, force application, and compliance follow from one reward. Our compliant reference reward is based on this reward design. Direction Matters [10] predicts a contact-force direction for admittance control. Lee et al. [6] supply a collision-avoidance controller with a binary interaction-mode command that determines whether tool contact is permitted. Inspired by this design, we use a compliance-mode input to tell the policy whether to prioritize following the commanded motion or yielding to contact forces. Unlike these systems, the reflex policy adapts joint-space references beneath an independently supplied task command. 

C. Proprioceptive Control and Local Reflexes 

Proprioceptive control and local reflexes have been studied as mechanisms for adapting robot motion during physical interaction. Hyun et al. [11] combine high-level gait com-mands with proprioceptive impedance control on the MIT Cheetah, while Wensing et al. [12] connect effective physical interaction to backdrivable actuation and low mechanical impedance. In manipulation, SaLoutos et al. [13] use local anti-slip and regrasping reflexes to correct imperfect teleop-erator grasps. Their reflexes use fingertip force/contact sens-ing; our reflex policy observes joint and kinematic history. These results motivate learning proprioceptive reflexes from environmental interaction and evaluating their reuse across manipulation conditions. III. M ETHOD 

As in operational-space control, where a contact task is characterized by its constrained and free directions [14], we approximate environmental constraints with three interac-tion primitives—spring, plane, and rail—that apply virtual forces to the simulated robot’s end-effector (EE) (Sec. III-A). Through RL, we train a proprioceptive reflex policy to follow task-space commands while accommodating these interactions (Sec. III-B). The policy uses command and pro-prioceptive history to generate joint-position targets. During training, the sampled interaction primitives define compli-ant position and interaction-force targets, while a separate penalty discourages excessive loading.

4) Training details: We train the policy with proximal policy optimization (PPO) [16] in an asymmetric actor– critic setup [17]. The actor encodes the 25-step observation history with two temporal convolution layers (32 channels, kernel size 3) into a 512-dimensional latent. An multilayer perceptron (MLP) with hidden widths 512 and 256 and exponential linear unit (ELU) activations then parameter-izes the Gaussian policy. The critic shares this structure and additionally receives the ground-truth interaction force as privileged information, which the deployed actor never observes. The simulation runs at 150 Hz, i.e., three physics steps per policy step, with high position and velocity solver iteration counts. IV. R ESULTS 

We evaluate the reflex policy on three tasks: dual-arm box lifting, peg insertion, and surface following. Commands come from scripted trajectories or a learned high-level pol-icy, and experiments run in simulation and on two robots with different actuation. We ask two questions: (Q1) When commands are imperfect, such as excessive commanded penetration or an erroneous hole position, does the reflex policy limit contact force and sustain task success where conventional execution layers fail? (Q2) Is this benefit simply a matter of compliance, or does it require the constraint structure of the interaction primitives? We address Q2 from two sides: classical compliant controllers swept over gains, and reflex variants trained with one primitive removed. Ad-ditional details and videos are provided in the supplementary material. We compare against three classical execution layers. IK + PD, the baseline in every experiment, converts task-space commands to joint targets with IK and tracks them with each robot’s joint-level controller (Sec. IV-A). Impedance is a Cartesian impedance controller, used in the insertion and surface following studies. Hybrid is hybrid motion–force control, used in the surface following study.

B. Dual-arm Robot Box Lifting 

In bimanual lifting, the two palms press on opposite faces of the box, and the squeezing force must stay within what the arms and the box can safely sustain. Under position control, this force is set indirectly: it scales with the controller stiffness and with how far the EE targets lie inside the box, so an error in commanded grasp width becomes squeezing force. We use this task to address Q1 with a single controlled source of command error, the commanded penetration depth.

C. Contact-Guided Peg Insertion 

Tight-clearance insertion cannot be solved by positioning alone: the clearance (0.02–0.1 mm) is orders of magnitude smaller than the error in the estimated hole position, so the peg must find and enter the hole through contact. Under a stiff position control, the same position error turns into large lateral forces at the rim, where the peg can jam. In this study the commands come from a learned policy instead of a script: we train a high-level insertion policy on top of the frozen reflex and compare it with policies trained on top of classical controllers. The study addresses Q1, with hole-position error as the command error, and Q2, through the Impedance baseline and the primitive ablations.

D. Surface Following 

Surface-following tasks such as wiping and deburring require the tool to slide along a surface while pressing on it with a bounded force. In practice, the surface geometry is known only approximately, so the commanded path lies partly below the surface. We reproduce this situation by commanding a trajectory below the nominal surface, over terrain whose height is unknown to the controller (Fig. 4A). Now there is conflict between tracking the command and limiting the contact force, while motion along the surface should be unaffected. Stiff position control resolves this conflict in favor of tracking and soft control in favor of low loading, which makes the task a direct test of the tradeoff that gain tuning moves along. The study runs in simulation with the QDD arm and addresses Q1, with commanded penetration and unknown surface height as the command error, and Q2, through a gain sweep of the classical baselines. 

1) Experimental setup: We test on one flat surface and 12 rough surfaces generated with Perlin noise, with height variations of ±20 mm that are unknown to the controllers (Fig. 4A). All controllers receive the same nominal trajec-tory: a 2 s approach followed by two 8 s horizontal cycles, at a commanded penetration of 15, 25, 35, or 45 mm below the nominal surface. Fig. 4. Simulated surface following results. (A) Setup and an example rough surface. (B, C) Flat and rough surfaces: contact force, tangential error, and force p95 versus commanded penetration. Boxes show the median, interquartile range, and 5th–95th percentiles during contact. Dashed lines mark Fth = 10 N. (D) Force p95 versus free-space position error; circle size indicates tangential RMSE. Baselines span stiffness scales of 0 .25 × to 2 ×;the reflex is fixed. 

We compare the reflex with IK + PD, Impedance, and Hybrid. The reflex was trained with Fth = 10 N and is not tuned for this task. Its compliance mode is switched by a simple contact rule: mt = 1 while the estimated contact force exceeds 1 N, and mt = 0 otherwise. Hybrid regulates the force along the fixed world z-axis to 5 N, using a force estimate from joint torques, and controls motion in the remaining directions. On rough surfaces, this axis need not coincide with the local surface normal. We report the 95th percentile (p95) of the contact force and the tangential tracking RMSE, both during surface following, and the position RMS error during the free-space approach. 

2) Contact loading and surface adaptation: The reflex matched Hybrid in contact loading and tangential tracking on both surface types (Fig. 4B, C). On the flat surface, the contact-force p95 was 7.5 N for the reflex and 7.4 N for Hybrid. On rough surfaces, it was 7.4 and 8.7 N, with tangential RMSE of 31.3 and 32.6 mm, respectively. As commanded penetration increased from 15 to 45 mm, the loading of the reflex and Hybrid changed little and stayed below the 10 N threshold. In contrast, the loading of IK + PD rose with penetration on both surface types, and Impedance became sensitive to penetration on rough surfaces. IK + PD also had the largest tangential errors, although it is the stiffest of the controllers: stiffer position tracking does not improve motion along a constrained surface. The reflex thus absorbs the mismatch between the com-manded trajectory and the real surface along the normal direction, at little in tangential motion relative to Hybrid. Unlike Hybrid, it is given neither a force-control axis nor a force setpoint. 

3) Tracking–loading balance and tuning: Figure 4D com-pares the fixed reflex with the baselines at stiffness scales 

s ∈ { 0.25 , 0.5, 1, 2}. For every baseline, the stiffness gains are scaled by s and the damping gains by √s, which keeps the damping ratio constant, as in Franka’s Cartesian impedance example [27]. For IK + PD and Impedance, stiffness trades approach accuracy against contact loading: stiffening reduced the approach error but raised the loading, and softening did the reverse. Neither direction improved tangential tracking. No tested setting reached the region occupied by the reflex, and only Hybrid, at its best stiffness scales, came close. This answers the classical side of Q2: gain tuning alone does not reproduce the benefit. Hybrid sets the reference in this task, because its force-control axis and force setpoint give it the constraint structure in advance. The reflex comes close to this reference without being given either. V. C ONCLUSION 

We showed that the low-level control layer beneath a task-level command source strongly affects contact-rich perfor-mance, and that a proprioceptive reflex can fill this role. The reflex is trained in simulation on three simple inter-action primitives and runs on top of a compliant joint-level controller. Without observing forces or constraint geometry, it limited contact forces and sustained task success under imperfect commands and uncertain geometry. On hardware, it completed dual-arm box lifting at every commanded pene-tration depth, whereas IK + PD diverged at the largest one. In peg insertion at 0.02 mm tolerance, it raised success from at most 22% to 36–58% with less than half the mean estimated contact force. In simulated surface following over unknown terrain, its tradeoff between free-space tracking and contact loading was close to that of hybrid motion–force control, although it was given neither a force-control axis nor a force setpoint. Gain tuning of IK + PD and Impedance did not reach this tradeoff, and removing any one primitive from training lowered simulated insertion success at the unseen tolerances. Future work will address three limitations. First, evaluation on a broader range of contact-rich tasks is needed to establish how far the approach generalizes. Second, the interaction primitives model translational loading and constraints only. They do not capture coupling between translation and rota-tion, such as opening a door with a rigid grasp. We plan to investigate rotational primitives in the future. Third, the reflex depends on an explicit compliance-mode input to distinguish nominal tracking from compliant interaction. This input must come from the high-level command source or from separate mode-selection logic, such as the force-threshold rule in Sec. IV-D, which complicates integration with policies that output only motion commands. We will investigate two ways to remove this requirement: having the high-level agent learn the mode together with its motion commands, as the insertion policy in Sec. IV-C already does, and letting the reflex adapt its compliance online from its proprioceptive history. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS
