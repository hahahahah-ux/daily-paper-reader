# ABSTRACT 

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a powerful paradigm for training agents, yet its success rests on domain expertise of human engineers who design informative reward signals for every new task. Unsupervised RL aims to reduce this engi-neering with intrinsic motivation (IM): reward signals that emerge from the agent environment interaction itself. Existing IM objectives, however, involve the selec-tion of information variables, which re-introduces domain expertise the field has sought to eliminate. We introduce Forward CIP (F-CIP), an RL-native formula-tion of the Controllable Information Production (CIP) objective, which is defined by the system’s dynamics alone and requires no such selection. We prove that F-CIP is compatible with RL and demonstrate its effectiveness with existing algo-rithms. Training agents with F-CIP results in unsupervised discovery of primitive behaviors such as balancing and maintaining controllability, which are essential for more complex robot behaviors. Paired with a simple forward-velocity reward, our method produces coordinated gaits such as hopping and running which oth-erwise require reward engineering to learn. # 1 INTRODUCTION 

The field of intrinsic motivation (IM) seeks to identify the goal-agnostic principles of self-motivation in biological systems and to replicate them in artificial ones. One such principle is the edge of chaos 

(EOC) (Zhang et al., 2025b; Tower, 2024; Wissner-Gross & Freer, 2013): the hypothesis that intelli-gence arises from an agent’s ability to operate at the boundary between stable and chaotic dynamics. In physically embodied agents, this boundary corresponds to states which are maximally unstable yet still controllable through active regulation. The upright stance of a humanoid is the canonical example: it affords walking, running, and hopping, yet it is also the most precarious configuration, since small mistakes readily lead to collapse. The EOC is thus distinct from pure chaos, where pertur-bations grow faster than a policy can suppress. In this work, we make the EOC directly optimizable as a reinforcement learning (RL) objective. A plethora of IM objectives have been proposed, yet they are employed almost exclusively in aux-iliary roles: as exploration bonuses added to an extrinsic reward (Raileanu & Rockt¨ aschel, 2020; Pathak et al., 2017; Burda et al., 2019b), or as pre-training signals whose value is only realized once a downstream task is imposed (Liu & Abbeel, 2021; Eysenbach et al., 2019; Laskin et al., 2021). In neither role does the objective require that the behaviors it produces be useful in their own right: curiosity rewards vanish as the learned model improves, and diversity objectives are indifferent to whether the states being explored are dynamically trivial or rich. In both cases the objective is a heuristic designed by human intuition rather than derived from dynamical systems theory, lacking a principle (such as the EOC) that distinguishes significant states from insignificant ones. Recently Shah & Tiomkin (2026) introduced Controllable Information Production (CIP), an IM ob-jective that closes this gap. CIP is grounded in the sum of positive open-loop Lyapunov Exponents (Wolf et al., 1985; Pesin, 1977), a global property of dynamical systems that governs the entropy rate (in bit /sec ) at which the dynamics generate new distinguishable trajectories. Whereas visitation en-tropy weights all states alike, CIP weights states by the complexity of the dynamics passing through them, and is therefore maximized not by uniform state coverage but by occupying the most dynam-ically sensitive regions. A controller maximizing CIP was shown to drive agents to EOC regimes across a suite of benchmark tasks, including raising a humanoid-like system from a passive config-uration to an upright stand. This demonstrated that IM grounded in a principled dynamical quantity can serve not merely as an exploration bonus, but rather as the primary objective of the agent. Existing optimizers of CIP, however, are sampling-based MPC planners (Howell et al., 2022; Pin-neri et al., 2021; Williams et al., 2015) which are ill-suited to the CIP objective. Such planners must forward-simulate a large number of candidate trajectories at every timestep and select the best ac-tion sequences. Because evaluating CIP requires running a Riccati recursion over the local Jacobians of each rollout, the cost of every discarded candidate trajectory is far higher than under a standard per-state cost. Gradient-based planners (Zhang et al., 2025a; Tassa et al., 2012) avoid the need for many rollouts and instead require a differentiable cost evaluated around a local trajectory. Since the CIP objective already depends on the state Jacobian, differentiating it for planning would de-mand second-order derivatives of the dynamics, which are impractical to obtain in high-dimensional systems. RL (Sutton et al., 1998) is a natural alternative to MPC: it amortizes the sampling cost into a learning phase and delivers a reactive feedback policy. The resultant policy is faster to use at test time because it can be run without expensive re-planning at each step. Yet RL imposes two structural requirements on its objective: (i) the reward must be emitted incrementally during the forward agent-environment interaction, and (ii) the reward must be Markovian in the agent’s state to support bootstrapped value learning. CIP in its original form violates both. It is computed by a backward-in-time Riccati recur-sion: the increment at the current time depends on a matrix propagated from the terminal time, i.e., on the entire future trajectory. We introduce Forward CIP (F-CIP) 1: a forward-in-time incremental decomposition of CIP that makes it, for the first time, directly optimizable by RL. This resolves requirement (i) by exposing an instantaneous per-step reward, and requirement (ii) by identifying the information matrix St as a sufficient statistic of the past: augmenting the state with St exactly restores the Markov property

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Existing IM objectives fall broadly into four families: curiosity, skill discovery, state-entropy maxi-mization, and EOC. We review each and position CIP against them. 

Curiosity. Curiosity-driven methods reward the surprise of a learned dynamics model, measured as prediction error (Pathak et al., 2017; Burda et al., 2019a), error of a random target network (Burda et al., 2019b), or ensemble disagreement (Pathak et al., 2019). These objectives are defined relative to the deficiencies of the learned model rather than a property of the environment itself. Conse-quently, the reward signal can vanish as the model improves (Raileanu & Rockt¨ aschel, 2020), and it is maximized by irreducibly unpredictable stimuli regardless of their behavioral relevance, e.g., the noisy-TV problem (Burda et al., 2019b). CIP instead rewards a dynamical invariant of the system: its signal neither vanishes with learning progress nor depends on model error. 

Skill-Discovery. Skill-discovery methods (Zheng et al., 2025) maximize the mutual information between a latent skill variable and the states that the agent visits under that skill (Eysenbach et al., 2019; Sharma et al., 2019; Gregor et al., 2016). This principle, however, is agnostic to where the state space is behaviorally significant. For example a diverse set of trivially stable skills that par-tition the state space satisfies the objective as well as a set of dynamically rich ones. CIP takes an alternative approach: it concentrates the agent at the states of maximal dynamical instability rather than spreading it over the entire state space. 

State-entropy maximization. A related family directly maximizes the entropy of the state-visitation distribution (Liu & Abbeel, 2021; Lee et al., 2025). Although both visitation entropy and CIP are entropic quantities, they measure different things: visitation entropy is a static property of the state-visitation distribution, whereas CIP is the rate at which uncertainty grows in open-loop along trajectories induced by the agent. The two are independent: an agent can achieve maximal vis-itation entropy while residing mostly in regions of low CIP, and conversely can hold a single EOC state of high CIP with near-zero visitation entropy. 

Edge of Chaos. The closest objectives to our work are also based on the EOC principle. Empow-erment (Klyubin et al., 2005) is the channel capacity from the agent’s actions to its future states; it generalizes the controllability Gramian of linear systems and has been related to the finite-time Lya-punov exponent (LE) spectrum (Tiomkin et al., 2024). Empowerment, however, has resisted exact factorization into a sum of rewards: channel capacity over a multi-step horizon is a supremum over action distributions that does not separate into per-step increments. Consequently, RL with empow-erment has relied on variational lower bounds and fixed-horizon estimators (Karl et al., 2022). CIP shares the EOC principle but is defined directly at the level of LE, and was previously optimized only by sampling-based MPC for a similar reason: its backward recursion admits no online reward. The present work removes this obstruction. 3Preprint. Under review. Similar to our method, Nguyen et al. (2025) also use the sum of positive LEs as a reward, but compute it offline along passive trajectories over a fixed horizon. This design has three limitations. First, precomputing LEs over a large continuous state space is computationally expensive and does not scale. Second, the fixed horizon introduces a truncation hyperparameter. Third, each per-step reward is already a sum of positive LEs over the horizon, so the return accumulates overlapping sums and has no clear interpretation. In F-CIP, the reward is an incremental per-step quantity, and the value function itself learns the sum of positive LEs. To our knowledge, F-CIP is the first dynamics-grounded intrinsic RL objective with an exact per-step decomposition, requiring neither a variational bound nor a fixed optimization horizon.

# 4 PROPOSED METHOD FOR FORWARD ESTIMATION OF CIP 

We factor the calculation of CIP into a sum of increments emitted during the forward agent-environment interaction, so that each increment can serve as an instantaneous reward. Our key in-sight comes from the control-estimation duality (Todorov, 2008): the backward Riccati recursion in Equation 1 is a control-side object, and its estimation-side dual is a forward-in-time covariance iteration whose increments depend only on the current Jacobian fxt and a propagated information matrix St. We develop this in four steps: the forward iteration converges to the same open-loop KSE as the backward recursion (Lemma 1); a log-domain form of the iteration is numerically stable and exposes the per-step reward (Lemma 2); the augmented state zt := ( xt, S t) defines a legitimate MDP (Proposition 3); and the infinite-horizon return of that MDP is exactly CIP (Theorem 4). # 6 DISCUSSION 

We introduced F-CIP, a practical scheme for maximizing CIP with standard RL algorithms. Whereas CIP was previously optimizable only by expensive MPC planners, our exact per-step decomposition makes it compatible with bootstrapped value learning: the learned value function recovers the true 

Λ+ of a known chaotic attractor ( Q1. ), maximizing the F-CIP reward alone produces self-righting and upright stabilization where standard IM objectives, do not ( Q2. ), and F-CIP pre-training replaces the postural reward engineering that locomotion tasks otherwise require ( Q3. ). These results support a broader position: intrinsic motivation need not be confined to an exploration bonus. Traditionally in continuous control, IM objectives serve almost exclusively as auxiliary sig-nals. This aligns with their underlying design, as principles like coverage and surprise are inherently geared toward exploration. CIP is a different IM signal: because it targets EOC states directly, it pro-duces useful behavior in both roles. As a primary objective it discovers self-righting with no external signal; as an auxiliary term it supplies an inductive bias toward controllable instability that reward engineers otherwise encode by hand. 8Preprint. Under review. Our method has three main limitations. First, the reward requires analytical Jacobians. Differen-tiable simulators such as MuJoCo-MJX (Todorov et al., 2012) provide these efficiently, and F-CIP is well suited to this setting; physical reality, however, offers no Jacobians, so sim-to-real transfer will require either training entirely in simulation or learning the Jacobians from data. Second, the mem-ory matrix St grows quadratically in the state dimension. On-policy training requires every parallel environment to carry its own St, and off-policy training would require storing it per-transition in a replay buffer, so scaling to high-dimensional robots demands a compressed representation. Third, CIP admits undesireable maximizers, analogous to the noisy-TV problem of curiosity (Burda et al., 2019b;a): in the pendulum systems the agent can accumulate reward by spinning freely, and in the hopper by uncontrolled thrashing. Adding joint damping removed these optima in our environments: damping taxes indiscriminate velocity accumulation while leaving configuration-dependent instabil-ity rewarded. However, this is an environment-level modification rather than an objective-level one, and a principled resolution remains open. Each limitation suggests future work. Jacobians can in principle be extracted from a learned world model: one-step forward models of the kind trained by curiosity methods (Pathak et al., 2017) are dif-ferentiable by construction, which would make F-CIP fully learning-based and simulator-agnostic. The St bottleneck can be addressed with feature extractors that respect the symmetric positive-semi-definite structure of the memory, such as SPDNet (Huang & Van Gool, 2017), mirroring the use of pretrained encoders for image observations in RL (Parisi et al., 2022). Finally, we plan to investigate alternative formulations of CIP to isolate its desirable maximizers via principled restrictions on the agent’s control power and velocities.

For the experiments involving pure CIP maximization we evaluate on four systems of increasing dimensionality: cart pole, double pendulum, triple pendulum, and gibbon, a planar humanoid-like system. These are the benchmarks of Shah & Tiomkin (2026), with modifications: we add joint damping to the triple pendulum ( 3.5) and gibbon ( 5.0). Without damping, the policy discovers triv-ial maximizers of CIP, spinning the free joints to accumulate unbounded velocities rather than seek-ing unstable configurations. Damping bounds the attainable velocities and thereby removes these degenerate optima. We also reduce the gear strength on the original gibbon environment to 50 .0.For experiments regarding locomotion: hopper and walker, we use the default environments from the DeepMind control suite (Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020). For both we set the internal joint damping to 1.0. Without damping we observed uncontrolled thrashing behavior as a maximizer of the CIP objective. A.5 TRAINING AND EVALUATION 

All baseline methods are trained under identical environmental conditions: the same environments, gear and damping settings, and the same reset distribution ( x0 ∼ ρ0(·)). They differ only in the intrinsic objective. Episodes reset to the hanging configuration in cart pole, double pendulum, and triple pendulum, so that the intrinsic reward alone must drive the exploration from the passive state to the upright one, without the agent ever being seeded there. In the gibbon environment, resets are split evenly between the hanging configuration and the vicinity of the upright stand. For hopper and walker the environments are dropped from a height above the ground with a small perturbation noise applied to its initial position. A.6 BASELINES
