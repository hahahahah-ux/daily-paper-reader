## Abstract 

World action models (WAMs) combine robot action gen-eration with future state prediction. Existing WAMs typi-cally predict videos or learned visual latents, which rep-resent interaction geometry only implicitly and may retain appearance information unrelated to control. We intro-duce SkeleWAM , a compact WAM that represents a manip-ulation scene as a sparse 3D skeleton composed of robot joints, object centers, and interaction points. Constructed online from current RGB-D observations and robot propri-oception, the skeleton provides a unified geometric state for action generation and future skeleton prediction. Fu-ture skeleton prediction provides additional geometric su-pervision for action learning without requiring visual re-construction. At inference, SkeleWAM generates actions di-rectly from the current skeleton and language instruction, while Medoid Action Consensus (MAC) serves as an auxil-iary consensus strategy for stochastic action samples. On LIBERO-Plus, SkeleWAM achieves an overall success rate of 85.9% with 57.1M parameters, outperforming Cosmos-Policy by 3.7 percentage points. These results demonstrate that sparse 3D robot–object structure provides an effec-tive state space for robust and parameter-efficient world action learning. The project is available at https:// skelewam-project.github.io/ .

## 1. Introduction 

Learning-based robotic manipulation has rapidly evolved from task-specific visuomotor policies to general-purpose robot foundation models. ACT learns action chunks from visual observations, while Diffusion Policy and DP3 model expressive action distributions using 2D and 3D visual rep-resentations, respectively [5, 21, 22]. As model pretrain-ing and robot datasets continue to scale, generalist policies and vision-language-action (VLA) models, including RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA, π0, and π0.5, have demonstrated increas-ingly broad capabilities across diverse manipulation tasks and environments [1, 2, 8, 9, 15]. World action models (WAMs) jointly model robot action generation and future state prediction, where the latter provides additional super-vision for learning how manipulation scenes evolve under robot actions [11, 12, 19, 20]. Many existing WAMs rep-resent future states as generated videos or learned visual latents [4, 14, 19]. Although latent prediction reduces the computational cost of explicit video generation, the infor-mation retained by visual latents is largely shaped by the un-derlying visual encoder and its pretraining objective. More importantly, compressing visual observations does not by it-self isolate the geometric variables that govern robot–object interactions, such as their spatial configurations and rela-tive motions. This raises a fundamental question: can a sparse representation of robot–object structure support ef-fective world action learning while substantially reducing model complexity? 

Our key observation is that the aspects of a manipulation scene most relevant to control can be represented as a skele-ton consisting of a sparse set of 3D landmarks on the robot and manipulated objects. The robot is represented by the 3D positions of its joints, while each object is represented by its center and a set of interaction points connected to that cen-ter. The object center captures its overall spatial location, whereas the interaction points identify locations relevant to robot contact and manipulation. Together, these landmarks provide a compact and explicit representation of the robot configuration and object interaction geometry. We use this unified skeleton as the state representation and jointly learn robot action generation and future skeleton prediction with-out reconstructing visual appearance. Building on this representation, we introduce Skele-WAM , a compact world action model that operates on sparse skeleton states. Given current RGB-D observations and robot proprioception, SkeleWAM constructs the skele-ton online and encodes it as a set of geometric tokens. Dur-ing training, the model jointly learns robot action generation and future skeleton prediction, allowing future prediction to supervise the shared geometric representation. At infer-ence, the future prediction branch is omitted, and actions are generated directly from the current skeleton and lan-guage instruction. ## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Visuomotor and Vision-Language-Action Mod-els 

Visuomotor policies learn direct mappings from observa-tions to robot actions. ACT predicts temporally coher-ent action chunks [22], while Diffusion Policy and DP3 model multimodal action distributions using image and 3D point-cloud observations, respectively [5, 21]. Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend this paradigm by in-corporating pretrained vision-language representations for language-conditioned multitask control. RT-2 and Open-VLA adapt vision-language representations to action gen-eration [2, 9], while π0 and π0.5 improve continuous con-trol and generalization through flow matching and hetero-geneous co-training [1, 8]. More recent methods improve policy adaptation, action encoding, and model compact-ness through OpenVLA-OFT, π0-FAST, and NORA, re-spectively [7, 10, 16]. Despite these advances, such policies primarily optimize action prediction and do not explicitly model how manipulation scenes evolve under robot actions. 22.2. World Action Models 

To capture how manipulation scenes evolve under robot ac-tions, world action models augment action learning with future state prediction. WorldVLA and UniVLA jointly model action generation and visual world evolution [3, 18], while DreamZero, GE-Act, and Cosmos-Policy leverage pretrained video models for robot control and planning [11, 13, 19]. VLA-JEPA replaces pixel reconstruction with the prediction of future latent target representations [17], whereas Fast-WAM retains future video prediction as a co-training objective but removes future generation at infer-ence [20]. Although latent objectives reduce the compu-tational cost and dependence on pixel reconstruction, both videos and visual latents represent interaction geometry only implicitly rather than parameterizing it as explicit state variables. In contrast, SkeleWAM defines both current and future states directly in a sparse 3D skeleton space com-posed of robot joints, object centers, and interaction points. It jointly learns action generation and future skeleton pre-diction, allowing future geometric prediction to supervise representation learning during training without being re-quired for action generation at inference.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Simulation Setup 

Benchmark and evaluation. We evaluate SkeleWAM on the full LIBERO-Plus benchmark [6], which contains 10,030 variants across seven perturbation categories. We report zero-shot task success rates without fine-tuning on the evaluation variants. Table 1 compares SkeleWAM with VLA policies and world-model-based methods. 

Baselines. We compare SkeleWAM with representa-tive VLA policies, including OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, NORA, UniVLA, π0, π0-Fast, and π0.5. We also include methods incorporating world modeling or future-state pre-diction, including WorldVLA, Fast-WAM, VLA-JEPA, GE-Act, and Cosmos-Policy. SkeleWAM uses a frozen pre-trained perception network to estimate object centers and interaction points from RGB-D observations. These object nodes are combined with robot keypoints obtained through forward kinematics to form the current skeleton. Skele-WAM (sim-state) directly obtains the corresponding object coordinates from simulator states and serves as a privileged-input reference. 

Implementation details. We train and evaluate Skele-WAM on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Training runs for 60K steps with an effective batch size of 48, jointly pre-dicting Ha = 32 action steps and eight future skeleton states. Action and future-skeleton tokens do not attend to each other. At inference, we use 10 flow-integration steps and execute He = 16 actions before replanning. MAC sam-ples K = 3 action trajectories from independent Gaussian noise initializations and computes consensus over their first 

h = 10 motion steps. Future-skeleton prediction is omit-ted at inference. Additional implementation details are pro-vided in the supplementary material.

4.4. Ablation Studies 

We ablate six design choices: object skeleton composition, future-skeleton supervision, cross-branch attention, action execution horizon, the number of MAC candidates, and the consensus window. All experiments use the RGB-D setting on the full LIBERO-Plus benchmark, with fixed training data, optimization settings, and evaluation variants. Unless varied explicitly, we use Ha = 32 , He = 16 , K = 3 , and 

h = 10 . Tables 3 and 4 summarize the model and inference ablations, respectively. 

Object skeleton composition. Keeping robot keypoints unchanged, we compare object representations containing centers only, interaction points only, and both types of points. The combined representation achieves 85.9% suc-cess, outperforming centers only (82.3%) and interaction points only (77.7%) by 3.6 and 8.2 percentage points, re-spectively (Table 3, panel a). Object centers provide sta-ble spatial references, while interaction points describe manipulation-relevant regions. Their combination therefore provides a more complete representation of object interac-tion geometry. 

Contribution of future-skeleton prediction. Removing the future-skeleton loss and future tokens reduces success from 85.9% to 80.1%, while retaining the same current-skeleton processing and action-generation backbone (Ta-ble 3, panel b). Since both variants use the same action-only inference procedure, the 5.8-point improvement comes from future-skeleton supervision during training. This re-sult shows that predicting future geometric states helps the shared world expert learn representations that benefit action generation. 

Cross-branch attention. We compare future-to-action (S+ → A), action-to-future ( A → S+), and no cross-branch attention, while retaining the current-skeleton con-text and both training objectives. No cross-branch attention achieves 85.9% success, compared with 85.6% for S+ → A

and 84.8% for A → S+ (Table 3, panel c). These relatively small differences, together with the larger drop after remov-ing future-skeleton supervision, indicate that the main ben-efit comes from co-training through the shared world ex-pert rather than direct information exchange between the two prediction branches. We therefore use no cross-branch attention, which also allows the future-skeleton branch to be removed at inference.

h = 10 , executing 5, 16, and 32 actions before replan-ning yields 80.7%, 85.9%, and 81.8% success, respectively (Table 4, panel a). The 16-step horizon improves over the shorter and longer settings by 5.2 and 4.1 percentage points. This intermediate horizon balances feedback from updated observations with temporally consistent action execution. We therefore use He = 16 by default.

## 5. Conclusion 

We introduced SkeleWAM, a compact world action model that represents manipulation scenes as sparse 3D skeletons composed of robot joints, object centers, and interaction points. By jointly learning robot action generation and fu-ture skeleton prediction, SkeleWAM uses future geometric dynamics as auxiliary supervision during training without requiring visual reconstruction or additional world predic-tion at inference. We further introduced Medoid Action Consensus as a lightweight strategy for selecting a repre-sentative trajectory from stochastic action samples without relying on a reward model. Experiments on the full LIBERO-Plus benchmark and five real-world manipulation tasks demonstrate that explicit robot–object geometry provides an effective and parameter-efficient state space for world action learning. Skele-WAM achieves strong zero-shot robustness with substan-tially fewer parameters than the compared VLA and world-model-based methods, while the ablations confirm the ben-efits of combining object centers with interaction points and of predicting future skeleton states during training. The remaining performance gap under large layout changes suggests that sparse geometry alone does not fully cap-ture the variation required for spatial generalization. Fu-ture work may address this limitation by incorporating uncertainty-aware perception, adaptive interaction land-marks, and richer relational structure while preserving the compactness of the skeleton representation.
