Abstract— Active vision manipulation requires a policy to
control both its camera and its end-effectors, yet camera
motion determines which evidence remains visible within finite
observation windows. Acquiring a new view can displace task-
critical cues, while retaining a view forgoes potentially useful
observations. We formulate this as an evidence-aware retain–
acquire problem and present ActiveWAM, a unified world–
action model that learns observation and manipulation jointly.
To this end, we propose training-time inversion which constrains
a frozen video prior by task-bearing source evidence and
visible temporal changes, eliminating the need for test-time
inversion or candidate ranking. At deployment, the policy
generates bimanual and pan/tilt actions—including stay and
reacquisition behaviors—from view-aware history, and updates
context from newly measured RGB observations. Future-video
prediction serves as a co-training signal, while action generation
requires neither future-video decoding nor optimal viewpoint
annotations. We introduce RoboTwin-AV, a 50-task benchmark
with executable pan/tilt control and automatically generated
demonstrations. ActiveWAM improves TAVIS out-of-distribution
success by up to 17.0 percentage points over the strongest
baselines, achieves 20.0 additional points over Fast-WAM on
RoboTwin-AV, and outperforms it by 26.7 points on real-world
physical kitchen tasks.
I. INTRODUCTION
General-purpose manipulation requires robots to both
acquire task-relevant visual evidence and act on it. Vision–
Language–Action (VLA) policies connect observations and
instructions to commands [1], [2], while World–Action
Models (WAMs) additionally learn the visual consequences
of those commands [3], [4], [5]. In bimanual manipulation,
coordinated arm actions change the scene while camera
actions determine which spatial relations remain visible within
the policy’s finite observation window.
Useful observation is not equivalent to removing occlusion.
During approach, a view should expose object–end-effector
relations; during placement, the destination becomes equally
important. Even in an unobstructed workspace, moving a
well-positioned camera can discard context. The appropriate
observation behavior includes retaining a good view, not only
searching for a better one. We call this the retain–acquire
problem: a policy must preserve task-critical evidence in its
recent representation while acquiring or reacquiring physical
evidence when the current view is insufficient.
Recent benchmarks demonstrate that active camera control
depends on task structure rather than universal heuristics.

AV-ALOHA evaluates active, static, and wrist cameras in bi-
manual tasks [6]; TAVIS studies conditional selection, search,
and waiting behaviors under distribution shifts [7]; AVR
combines viewpoint and focal-length control [8]; See2Act
couples action refinement to target-centric views [9]. These
results establish that observation control cannot rely on fixed
rules and expose a temporal challenge: changing the view
can acquire new evidence while removing previously useful
evidence.
Pretrained representations [10], [11] and domain random-
ization [12] support visual transfer, yet history still mixes task
evidence with appearance and camera motion. Stem-OB uses
diffusion inversion to improve visual generalization [13], but
a generic transform neither specifies which historical evidence
must survive nor preserves temporal changes that can overturn
earlier decisions.
We address the joint question: how can a world–action
model learn to retain task-relevant evidence while controlling
which evidence it acquires next? Our key insight is to couple
a task-selective training transformation with executable obser-
vation actions through the same demonstrated continuation.
The transformation changes the representation of an observed
history; head and arm actions change the physical observation
stream. Neither substitutes for the other: transforming a
history cannot reveal an unobserved surface, and moving
a camera cannot by itself ensure that a previously seen cue
remains available.
We present ActiveWAM, an evidence-aware active vision
WAM that combines task-guided history inversion with
unified head–arm generation (Fig. 1). Training-Time Inversion
(TTI) uses a frozen video prior constrained by task-bearing
source evidence and visible temporal changes. A view-aware
history adapter represents camera identity, pose, exposure
time, and validity alongside current RGB. The policy learns
from raw and transformed conditions with identical original
targets. Future-video prediction provides a co-training signal;
deployment uses raw observations to generate one head–arm
trajectory, without online inversion, candidate ranking, or
manually annotated optimal viewpoints.
To evaluate this retain–acquire coupling at bimanual-
task scale, we introduce RoboTwin-AV, an active vision
benchmark extension of RoboTwin 2.0 [14] retaining all
50 task definitions while adding executable pan/tilt control,
synchronized RGB, and automatically generated demonstra-
tions. We evaluate on the independently released TAVIS
benchmark and use RoboTwin-AV for large-scale controlled
comparisons, complemented by fixed-camera generalization
tests and physical trials.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We formulate active vision manipulation as evidence-
aware retain–acquire behavior and introduce task-guided
inversion that preserves task evidence and visible tem-
poral changes within a finite history window while
transferring the training signal to a raw-input policy.
• We propose ActiveWAM, a unified policy that generates
executable head–arm actions from view-aware history,
including stay and reacquisition behaviors; future-video
prediction is used as a co-training signal rather than a
required test-time output.
• We introduce RoboTwin-AV, a 50-task benchmark ex-
tension with executable pan/tilt control, and evaluate
the retain–acquire interaction through factorial control,
collector-shift tests, external baselines, and physical
bimanual manipulation.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Active Observation and Camera Control
Active cameras can expose occluded cues and track moving
objects, yet each view change replaces previously visible
information with new observations. This creates a retain–
acquire trade-off: acquiring new evidence competes with
retaining prior cues within finite history windows. AV-
ALOHA evaluates active, static, and wrist cameras across
bimanual tasks [6]. TAVIS introduces task-dependent gaze,
waiting, search, and exploration under distribution shifts [7].
AVR combines viewpoint and focal-length control [8]. These
results establish that observation control must adapt to the
evidence each manipulation stage requires.
Existing learned approaches treat observation and manipu-
lation separately. Sun et al. decouple gaze via look-at inverse
kinematics, reducing policy complexity but preventing joint
reasoning over camera and arm consequences [15]. See2Act
couples action refinement with test-time viewpoint search,
incurring computational overhead and lacking the closed-loop
reactivity manipulation requires [9]. Decoupled control cannot
capture joint consequences of camera and arm actions. We
address this through evidence-aware retain–acquire generation
within one world–action model trained end-to-end, using
training-time history inversion to transfer evidence retention
to raw-input deployment.
B. World–Action Models and Unified Generation
Generalist policies scale robot learning through shared
transformers [16], [17], [18]. World–Action Models (WAMs)
extend this paradigm by jointly generating future-video
predictions and executable actions. DreamZero and DiT4DiT
couple video generation with action outputs [19], [20].
EasyWAM-Unified provides a unified transformer interface
for joint future/action generation [5], while Fast-WAM
demonstrates that video prediction can serve as a co-training
signal without requiring future-video decoding at test time [3].
We build on this unified-generation framework, extending it
to couple task-selective history transformation with physical
sensing actions. Following Fast-WAM, we evaluate video
co-training and action-only inference roles separately.
C. Observation Inversion and Temporal Consistency
Diffusion-based inversion enables deterministic recon-
struction along learned trajectories [21], [22]. Video editing
methods extend this principle through source-guided trans-
formations, using attention control and inter-frame feature
correspondences to maintain temporal consistency [23], [24],
[25]. In robotics, Stem-OB applies observation inversion to
transfer transformed-input training benefits to raw-image

B. Task-Guided History Inversion
Active vision manipulation introduces a dual challenge:
head actions change both what is observed and where task-
critical cues appear. To address retention, we train the policy
on transformed histories that preserve task-relevant evidence,
teaching it which cues must survive view changes.
We extend observation inversion [13] to observed video
history. A frozen Wan T2V video prior [26] processes each
camera’s history independently. Cameras are not temporal
neighbors, and the prior receives neither future observations
nor task-privileged information. Bidirectional processing
within Ht remains causal for decision t because every frame
has already been observed. The prior supplies a source-
constrained transformation direction, while the recorded
action continuation and future outcome remain fixed, ensuring
that inversion introduces no new physical evidence. Training-
Time Inversion (TTI) generates paired examples for learning,
while deployment uses raw history.

C. Unified Active Vision World–Action Modeling
Having addressed the retention side of the retain–acquire
problem through task-guided history inversion (Sec. III-B), we
now turn to the acquisition side. Arm actions alter the scene
configuration, while head actions determine which spatial
relations remain visible within the finite history window. We
unify these mechanisms within a single world–action model
that learns what evidence to retain through inversion-based
training and what to acquire through executable head control.
a) View-aware history: To enable the policy to distin-
guish camera motion from scene changes, we augment the
history representation with camera-specific metadata. Building
on EasyWAM-Unified [5], we encode each camera’s history
independently through a frozen video VAE, then augment the
visual tokens with camera identity, measured pose, relative
exposure time, and validity flags. A temporal resampler
aggregates these tokens into a fixed-size summary:

native task success predicates rather than agreement with
the collector. Future-video targets remain in their recorded
camera streams to preserve the visual effects of head motion.
Demonstrations associate search with reacquisition, approach
with object–hand coverage, and placement with object–goal
framing. They also include stay, whose frequency alone does
not establish a new observation strategy. Inversion changes
the observed condition while retaining the original action and
future-video targets, encouraging consistent decisions when
task-relevant cues survive.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
Experimental questions: Our experimental evaluation
is organized around four central questions. (Q1) Does task-
guided inversion preserve task-relevant evidence across view
changes? (Q2) Do learned observation actions improve over
fixed or rule-based control? (Q3) What roles do video co-
training and paired learning play? (Q4) Can the system com-
pose multi-stage physical tasks with acceptable efficiency?
A. Benchmarks and Experimental Setup
TAVIS [7] provides native Head/Hands trajectories with
96 test episodes per task, seed, and split. Its pose-OOD
condition perturbs end-effector positions (σ = 10 cm) and
neck yaw/pitch (σ = 10◦) to test spatial robustness.
We extend RoboTwin 2.0 [14] with synchronized RGB
observations and executable pan/tilt control across all 50 tasks,
creating RoboTwin-AV. To generate active-vision demon-
strations, we implement a gaze collector that uses instance
masks and rendered depth to compute waypoints, with a
0.06-rad stay deadband to avoid unnecessary head motion.
Collector-privileged inputs (masks, depth) are withheld at
test time; success evaluation follows native task predicates.
We train on 100 successful trajectories per task and evaluate
on 100 test episodes per task, seed, and condition (clean,
appearance, initial-head-pose, compound). Train/test splits are
created before temporal windowing, and failures or timeouts
are retained in the test set. Figure 3 illustrates representative
simulated and physical task sequences. For simulation results,
each configuration is tested under three different seeds.

B. Main Benchmark Comparisons
a) Results on TAVIS: On TAVIS, ActiveWAM improves
over the strongest listed baseline by up to 17.0 percentage
points (Table I). The gains are pronounced under pose-OOD
conditions: +13.0/+10.6 pp on Head tasks and +12.0/+11.0 pp
on Hands tasks for GR1T2/Reachy2, respectively. These
results show higher task success under end-effector and
camera pose perturbations. We examine the contributions
of history inversion and observation control through the
controlled comparisons below. To characterize gaze timing,
we measure gaze–action lead time on Head ID tasks. Mean
lead times are 0.75 s (GR1T2) and 0.78 s (Reachy2), with 97%
of gaze actions classified as valid (878 and 763 successful
episodes, respectively).
b) Results on RoboTwin-AV: On RoboTwin-AV (Ta-
ble II), ActiveWAM achieves gains of +4.0, +14.4, +9.0,
and +19.3 percentage points over the strongest listed base-
line under clean, appearance, pose, and compound shifts,
respectively. Under compound shifts, ActiveWAM exceeds
Fast-WAM by 20.0 percentage points. It also achieves
53.3±1.5% success compared with 41.7±1.5% for the raw-
history baseline. The matched ablations below examine which
components contribute to this improvement.
c) Fixed-camera generalization: We also evaluate fixed-
camera benchmarks to examine whether the training approach
remains useful when the policy cannot move the camera. On
LIBERO-Plus, ActiveWAM achieves 76.9% success compared
with 68.2% for raw history, 72.5% for task-safe augmentation,
and 71.9% for source-feature auxiliary supervision (Table V).
On LIBERO, success is 98.7% compared with 98.4% for raw
history. These results show an improvement on LIBERO-
Plus while the reported LIBERO scores remain close. The
interaction between inversion and head control is examined
separately on RoboTwin-AV.

D. Composed Real-Robot Tasks
We deploy ActiveWAM on an AirbotPlay fixed-base dual-
arm platform with a fixed focal Realsense D455 RGB
camera on an IQR pan/tilt 2-DOF head. Each of three
task segments composes two separately trained policies: (i)
slicing—place cucumber horizontally, retrieve knife, then
slice; (ii) egg frying—pour oil, crack egg while the left
arm grasps the pan handle, then retrieve spatula and stir;
(iii) mixing—pour cucumber and cooked egg, then retrieve
spatula and stir-fry. Egg stirring retains the pan-handle grasp;
stage switching is prescribed. Human inter-segment actions
enter neither evaluation nor montage. Table VIII reports
Stage 1 and total-task success for the same 20 complete
trials per task and method, yielding 60 trials per method
and 300 trials across the five methods. ActiveWAM achieves
66.7%/50.0% Stage 1/total-task success, versus 60.0%/36.7%
without inversion and 48.3%/23.3% for Fast-WAM.
E. Deployment Efficiency and Failure Analysis
On an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, action inference takes ap-
proximately 165 ms. Action chunking yields an effective rate
near 15 Hz, while interpolation maintains 25 Hz commands.
ActiveWAM completes the full task in 30 of 60 physical
trials. We review all 30 failed trials and assign each to one
primary category: manipulation errors (12 trials, 40.0%),


premature termination (10 trials, 33.3%), targets outside the
pan/tilt camera’s reachable field of view (5 trials, 16.7%),
and hardware issues (3 trials, 10.0%). These percentages are
calculated over the 30 failed trials. The categories describe
the failure modes without establishing underlying causes.
V. CONCLUSION
ActiveWAM formulates active vision manipulation as
evidence-aware retain–acquire control, where a policy must
preserve task-critical cues within a finite history window
while controlling which evidence it acquires next. Task-
guided history inversion preserves task-relevant evidence and
visible temporal changes during training, while unified world–
action generation produces executable bimanual and head
actions from raw observations at deployment. Across TAVIS,
RoboTwin-AV, and physical tasks, the method improves
over strong baselines by up to 19.3 percentage points under
compound distribution shifts. Matched factorial and collector
controls confirm that evidence retention and active observation
are complementary. The current system uses a fixed-focal-
length head, short history window, and prescribed stage
switching; natural extensions include wider fields of view,
optical zoom, end-to-end segmentation, and longer-horizon
memory mechanisms.
