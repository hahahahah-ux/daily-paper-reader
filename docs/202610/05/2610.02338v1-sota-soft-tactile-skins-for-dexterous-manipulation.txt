Abstract — A growing body of work suggests that tactile sensing gives robot policies contact information that com-plements vision in dexterous manipulation. However, visuo-tactile robot data remains scarce: dexterous demonstrations require teleoperating robots, which limits dataset scale. Human demonstrations are far cheaper to collect and offer a path to scale this data, but only if human and robot hands carry tactile sensors with corresponding signals. This requires sensors that conform to different hand geometries, cover the full hand, and share a common layout across embodiments. We present SoTa, a low-cost capacitive tactile skin that provides full-hand coverage on humans and robots while preserving a shared layout of 202 taxels across corresponding finger and palm regions. Our multilayer design with fabric electrodes enables in-house fabrication of thin, soft skins with customizable geometry for under $10 in materials per skin. The sensor retains over 97% of its initial response span after 10,000 loading-unloading cycles with traces retaining continuity through 1,280 tight-fist folding cycles. The shared taxel layout supports human-robot co-training with a common tactile encoder and no learned cross-sensor mapping. Across three contact-rich manipulation tasks, tactile observations improve in-distribution success over vision-only policies. With a fixed robot demonstration budget, adding human demonstrations more than doubles mean success across eight evaluation conditions, from 22.8% to 45.9%, improving success in all five out-of-distribution conditions. We plan to open-source the resources needed to fabricate and operate these skins. Project website: https://sota-skin.github.io

Many dexterous manipulation tasks, such as lifting a frag-ile cup, wiping dirt from a table, or inserting a charger into a power strip, require carefully controlled contact between

objects and different parts of the hand. Vision-based policies can perform a wide range of manipulation tasks [1]–[6], but they observe contact only indirectly, and a growing body of work suggests that tactile or force observations improve performance on contact-rich tasks [7]–[15]. Scaling visuo-tactile policy learning, however, is limited by data. Dexterous robot demonstrations require teleopera-tion [16]–[18], which is slow and depends on costly hard-ware. Human demonstrations are much cheaper to collect and could supplement robot data, but only if human hands carry tactile sensors whose signals correspond to the robot’s. Existing visuo-tactile systems rarely meet this requirement. Most sense contact only at the fingertips [7], [9], [11], [19], so they miss contacts on the phalanges and palm in tasks such as table wiping and plug insertion, and most are built for a single embodiment. A sensor that supports learning from both humans and robots must therefore cover the full hand, conform to different hand geometries, withstand repeated contact and flexing, and present the same tactile structure on both embodiments. We present SoTa, a low-cost capacitive tactile skin that meets these requirements. Our laser-patterned, multilayer fabric-electrode design produces thin, soft skins whose ge-ometry is fitted to each hand while preserving a shared layout of 202 taxels, with corresponding indices assigned to the same semantic hand regions. Each skin costs under $10 in materials, responds to forces from 20mN to 10N, retains over 97% of its initial response span after 10,000 loading–unloading cycles, and keeps trace continuity through 1,280 tight-fist folding cycles. Because both embodiments share the same layout, human and robot observations use arXiv:2610.02338v1 [cs.RO] 1 Oct 2026 a common tactile encoder without a learned cross-sensor mapping. Human demonstrations contain no action labels (as robot demonstrations do), so we incorporate human demonstrations through an auxiliary objective that predicts task phases from human and robot visuo-tactile observations, while action supervision comes only from robot data. We evaluate the system on three dexterous manipulation tasks involving contact with different parts of the hand: box reorientation, cup picking, and plug insertion. Tactile obser-vations improve in-distribution success on all three tasks, although these benefits do not consistently extend to out-of-distribution conditions. With a fixed robot-demonstration budget, adding human visuo-tactile demonstrations improves every evaluated condition relative to robot-only visuo-tactile training, raising average success from 22.8% to 45.9%. Our contributions are: (1) a soft, flexible, low-cost, and customizable capacitive tactile skin with coverage across the fingers and palm and a shared 202-taxel layout for human and robot hands; (2) characterization of the sensor’s response, cyclic stability, flexing durability, and motion-induced arti-facts; and (3) a language-supervised training pipeline that incorporates human visuo-tactile demonstrations into robot policy learning, with real robot experiments demonstrating improvements over robot-only training. We will release the resources needed to fabricate and operate these skins. II. R ELATED WORK 

Visuo-tactile and force-aware policy learning. A grow-ing body of work suggests that combining vision with touch or force can improve precision and sample efficiency on contact-rich tasks, both at execution time and as arepresentation-learning signal [7], [8], [10]–[12], [19], [34], [35]. Most visuo-tactile policies, however, rely on localized or sparse sensing. Work on multi-fingered hands [7], [8], [11] has used fingertip sensors [7], [11] or a small set of force-sensing resistors on the palm and finger links [8], while gripper-based systems use sparse magnetic patches on parallel-jaw grippers [9], or dense arrays on soft parallel-jaw fingers [10]. A parallel line of work injects force or torque signals directly into vision-language-action models [13], [14], [35] or force-conditioned imitation learning [12], [15]. Most of these approaches are trained on robot data, which is costly to collect for dexterous hands. Methods that draw on human demonstrations do so with limited or heterogeneous sensing: [15] measures force at a single human fingertip, and FTP-1 [36] pretrains a generalist tactile policy on human and robot data from heterogeneous sensors, using sensor-specific encoders. We build on FTP-1 with a tactile sensing interface that provides full-hand coverage and a shared taxel layout across human and robot hands, so that human and robot demonstrations can share a single tactile encoder without a separate cross-sensor alignment stage. 

Tactile interfaces for dexterous manipulation. Tactile sensing for dexterous manipulation has advanced through both robot-mountable tactile skins and human-worn tactile gloves. Robot skins use magnetic [9], [20]–[22] and ca-pacitive [23] sensing, with coverage ranging from localized patches and fingertips to the full hand [24]. Human-worn sys-tems such as STAG [25], FlexGlove [27] or OpenTouch [31] provide full-hand tactile sensing, and FlexGlove additionally customizes sensor geometry to each user. These systems, however, are designed for a single embodiment. Interfaces designed for both embodiments use mechanically matched structures [28], [29] or identical sensors [26], [30], but they constrain the wearer to robot-specific hardware or provide coverage limited to the fingertips and palm. SoTa instead fits a thin, flexible, and low-cost skin to each hand while preserv-ing a shared 202-taxel layout across the fingertips, phalanges, and palm, so human and robot observations correspond by construction. Human-robot co-training for policy learning. Prior work incorporates human demonstrations either as action targets, using tracked or retargeted human motion [37]–[39], or through auxiliary prediction objectives during co-training, such as predicting future frames [40], future end-effector trajectories [41], or 3D scene flow [42]. Language prediction and cross-modal objectives also provide supervision for pol-icy learning and the integration of additional sensing modal-ities [43], [44]. In visuo-tactile policy learning, prior work incorporates human demonstrations by aligning human and robot tactile representations using paired demonstrations [32] or automatically constructed pseudo-pairs [33]. Unlike prior work, we combine full-hand tactile sensing with language-supervised representation learning. Our shared taxel layout allows human and robot observations to use a common tactile encoder, while task-phase labels provide supervision without requiring observation pairs across the two datasets. Human demonstrations contribute to this auxiliary objective without motion retargeting or action labels. Our experiments demonstrate improvements over robot-only training. III. T HE SOTA SENSOR 

We describe our SoTa sensor design (Section III-A), our visuo-tactile dexterous manipulation data collection setup, which supports human and robot demonstration collection (Section III-B), and our policy learning architecture, with a shared tactile encoder across embodiments (Section III-C). 

A. Sensor Design and Fabrication 

We build tactile skins for both the human demonstrator and the robot hand. The devices are mechanically customized to each embodiment but share the same capacitive sensing principle, taxel size, and taxel count. Taxel spacing varies with hand geometry while preserving region-level semantic correspondence across embodiments. In our current design, the skin provides a dense 202 -taxel pressure map with 40 taxels assigned to the thumb, 24 taxels to the index finger, 16 taxels to each of the middle, ring, and pinky fingers, with the remaining taxels distributed across the palm. The thin-film skins are fabricated using a laser-enabled metal-fabric-electrode patterning process and adopt the mul-tilayer capacitive stack shown in Fig. 2(b). An 80 μm-thick conductive fabric (MOS Equipment) is heat-pressed onto the 25 μm-thick polyimide support layer (McMaster-Carr) using a 30 μm-thick iron-on TPU film (BENECREAT). The conductive surface is then selectively ablated by UV laser to define electrically isolated traces for both the top and bottom electrode layers. A sandpaper-molded SEBS dielectric layer is sandwiched between the two patterned electrode layers to form a parallel-plate piezocapacitive sensing stack. After alignment, the multilayer device is sealed along the edges using Kapton tape and a soft SEBS layer is cast over its outer surface. Electrode traces are routed to the readout board through customized flexible flat cables (JLCPCB). The complete fabrication workflow takes approximately six hours of laser time, followed by two hours of manual assembly. The 202 taxels are sequentially scanned by a compact readout board measuring approximately 39 .6 mm × 36 mm .The same board is used across embodiments, with wired se-rial communication for the robot and wireless Wi-Fi stream-ing for the human-worn glove. An ESP32-S3 (Espressif Systems) coordinates taxel multiplexing and data acquisition. Taxels are selected through 74HC4067 analog multiplexers (Nexperia) and measured using a PCAP04 capacitance-to-digital converter (ScioSense). With an 850 mAh LiPo battery mounted beneath the board, the glove supports approximately seven hours of untethered operation at ∼ 30 fps .SoTa exhibits clear capacitive responses to applied loads ranging from 20 mN to 10 N in Fig. 2(c). Synchronization and post-processing. All vision, pro-prioceptive, and tactile streams are timestamped at capture using a shared wall clock and stored per episode together with task metadata. For each demonstration, we apply the following per-taxel post-processing pipeline: (1) subtract a baseline computed as the mean of the first 30 frames, and set those baseline frames to zero; (2) suppress spikes in the baseline-subtracted signal; (3) normalize by the baseline and apply an exponential transformation; and (4) linearly rescale the normalized values from [0 .1, 2.0] to [0 , 1] , clipping values outside this range. Implementation details are documented alongside the released code. 

C. Policy Learning Architecture 

We build our visuo-tactile policy on FTP-1 [36], a gener-alist tactile manipulation policy pretrained on approximately 

3,000 hours of human and robot demonstrations spanning 

21 tactile sensors. FTP-1 combines a PaliGemma vision-language backbone, a tactile transformer expert, and a flow-matching action expert. Sensor-specific tactile encoders map tactile measurements into a shared, function-area-based token representation. We initialize the shared model components from the pretrained FTP-1 checkpoint and train new tactile encoders for our skins from scratch. Human and robot skins share these encoders and the same functional-area representation. We introduce a training-only language expert that predicts the current episode-segment annotation from visual-language and tactile features. This branch attends to the vision-language and tactile representations, but not to the action branch. Its predictions are supervised with cross-entropy over the pretrained language vocabulary, with gradients stopped through the vocabulary projection weights. The action expert does not attend to the language expert. Segment annotations are obtained by manually assigning task phase phrases to frame intervals in each demonstration via an intuitive web-based annotation interface. For example, box reorientation uses “approach the box,” “reorient the box,” and “release.” These phrases are mapped to canonical phase tokens and expanded into per-frame targets. We evaluate three aspects of SoTa. First, we test whether tactile observations improve contact-rich manipulation rel-ative to vision-only policies. Second, we examine the ef-fects of auxiliary language supervision and human-robot co-training under a fixed robot data budget. Finally, we characterize sensor durability and motion-induced artifacts.

Results. The addition of tactile feedback improves in-distribution success on all three tasks (Figure 6) and im-proves box OOD success substantially. However, it does not improve OOD generalization for cup picking or plug inser-tion, and reduces cup OOD success relative to the vision-only policy. We hypothesize that these differences reflect how contact patterns vary across training and evaluation objects. Box reorientation involves broad, sustained contact that may transfer across boxes of different sizes. Cup picking and plug insertion depend on more localized contacts that may be sensitive to object geometry. With only one robot training object per task, policies may therefore learn tactile features that do not generalize consistently to unfamiliar objects. 

C. Auxiliary Supervision and Human-Robot Co-Training 

We also examine the contributions of auxiliary supervision and human-robot co-training. All methods use the same FTP-1 initialization and task-specific robot demonstration dataset. Human-robot co-training additionally uses 150 hu-man demonstrations per task. Robot-only two-stage training, RV,T +L V,T, retains repre-sentation pretraining and visuo-tactile language supervision but excludes human demonstrations; this tests whether aux-iliary training on robot data alone can reproduce the full method’s gains. Vision-only auxiliary training, RV,T + H V,retains human demonstrations but excludes tactile inputs from the auxiliary objective for both human and robot data; this tests whether co-training benefits from incorporating touch into auxiliary representation learning. In this variant, the tactile encoder and expert remain frozen during represen-tation pretraining and are updated only through robot action supervision during policy learning. We also include the robot-only reference, RV,T, which uses action supervision without representation pretraining or auxiliary supervision. 

Results. Full human-robot visuo-tactile co-training, 

RV,T + H V,T, exceeds the robot-only reference, RV,T,in every reported condition (Figure 7), increasing mean success from 22.8% to 45.9%, with the largest relative gains OOD. The two ablations examine the roles of human demonstrations and tactile auxiliary supervision. Robot-only two-stage training, RV,T + L V,T, does not reproduce the full method’s OOD gains: improvements over the reference are inconsistent across tasks, and cup ID per-formance decreases. The full method achieves higher success than this variant in all five OOD conditions, supporting the benefit of adding human demonstrations to visuo-tactile aux-iliary training. Vision-only human co-training, RV,T + H V,underperforms both the full method and the reference in most conditions. Thus, additional human data with vision-only auxiliary supervision does not reproduce the full method’s gains. We use the same input modalities for auxiliary phase prediction across human and robot data, keeping the auxiliary training setup consistent across embodiments. Accordingly, this ablation evaluates tactile auxiliary supervision as awhole; it does not isolate the contribution of human touch, which would require an asymmetric modality configuration. 

Analysis. Co-training improves OOD performance where robot-only training struggles, consistent with human demon-strations broadening the training distribution’s object varia-tion relative to the single-instance robot dataset. The human dataset spans three objects per task, one overlapping with the robot-training object, compared to the robot dataset’s single instance. This broader coverage at training time may have helped policies generalize beyond the training objects and configurations.

D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

We presented SoTa, a low-cost, durable capacitive tactile skin that provides full-hand coverage on human and robot hands. Our motivation is that visuo-tactile robot data is expensive to collect through teleoperation, while human demonstrations are cheap but only useful if both embodi-ments produce corresponding tactile signals. Across box reorientation, cup picking, and plug insertion tasks, tactile observations improve in-distribution success. With a fixed robot-demonstration budget, adding hu-man visuo-tactile demonstrations improves every evaluated condition, raising average success from 22.8% to 45.9%, with the largest gains out of distribution. These results suggest that tactile human demonstrations are a practical way to supplement costly robot teleoperation. Several questions remain open. We used a fixed set of 150 human demonstrations per task, so the effects of increasing the size and diversity of the human dataset, and the extent to which human demonstrations can replace robot data, remain to be measured. We also did not compare our shared taxel layout against learned cross-sensor mapping approaches. Fi-nally, our hardware tests demonstrate stability under repeated loading and small motion-induced responses, but long-term wear under sustained use remains to be evaluated. By releasing the fabrication workflow, sensor designs, Gerber files for ordering the readout boards, and firmware, we aim to enable other research groups to reproduce and adapt the sensing platform. Because the glove is wearable and untethered, it can collect tactile demonstrations across diverse objects, tasks, and demonstrators without a robot. Future work will study how far this scaling can go and extend the skin to other end effectors while preserving the shared taxel layout. VI. A CKNOWLEDGEMENTS
