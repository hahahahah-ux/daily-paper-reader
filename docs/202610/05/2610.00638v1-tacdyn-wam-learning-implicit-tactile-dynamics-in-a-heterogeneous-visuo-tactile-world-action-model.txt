# ABSTRACT 

World action models improve robotic manipulation by conditioning actions on predicted futures, yet existing tactile variants largely inherit video-generation pipelines that reconstruct future tactile observations through iterative denoising. Such prediction can become unreliable under deployment drift: small changes in contact position or force may substantially alter tactile pixels even when the underlying contact evolution remains predictable. We introduce TacDyn-WAM, a heterogeneous visuo-tactile world action model that predicts implicit tactile dynamics rather than reconstructing future tactile observations. It learns TacRep, a dynamics-aware tactile target space trained through masked spatio-temporal pre-diction on tactile clips and regularized by relational structure distillation. A vi-sual expert and an Implicit Tactile Dynamics Expert predict future visual and tactile representations in separate target spaces while interacting through joint attention; the tactile expert predicts future representations and their changes at multiple horizons in a single forward pass, and a read-only tactile memory sup-plies the current tactile state. On UniVTAC, TacDyn-WAM achieves an average success rate of 81.5% using only the provided demonstrations, reaching state-of-the-art-level performance and remaining competitive with models pretrained on large-scale visuo-tactile trajectories. Ablations confirm the benefits of both tactile pathways and TacRep over pixel-reconstruction and static alternatives. On five real-robot tasks, TacDyn-WAM reaches 71.0% average success, and modest-scale pretraining raises it to 85.0%, further validating our method. Project page. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

World models forecast how observations evolve, while world action models use this foresight for policy learning, allowing actions to depend on predicted futures rather than current observations alone (Cai et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026c; Ye et al., 2026a). Yet in existing world action models, this foresight comes primarily from vision. In contact-rich or precision manipula-tion tasks, such as insertion and alignment under occlusion, cameras cannot fully resolve contact states, local geometry, or interaction forces. Early efforts incorporated tactile sensing into VLA models, giving rise to vision-tactile-language-action (VTLA) models that encode tactile observa-tions alongside vision and language (Huang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Bi et al., 2026b; Cheng et al., 2026). By understanding the current tactile state, they improve contact-rich manipulation over vision-only policies. However, they do not predict how this state will evolve, leaving policies reactive to tactile feedback. Recent tactile world action models close this gap by predicting future tactile observations as part of the world model (NeoteAI Team et al., 2026a; Lou et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026b; Wu et al., 2026). These models have achieved promising results, with some further benefiting from large-scale visuo-tactile pretraining. Despite this progress, a core design question remains: what should tactile world models predict? Most existing methods simply reuse video-generation pipelines: the same VAE encodes camera and tactile frames, while video backbones iteratively denoise future tactile latents. This slows inference and ties prediction to pixel reconstruction, which is poorly suited to tactile observations. Expert demonstrations cover only tactile images near correct contacts, whereas closed-loop errors may 1arXiv:2610.00638v1 [cs.RO] 30 Sep 2026 Preprint cause policies to drift from them. This drift may leave camera views nearly unchanged, yet even slight changes in contact position or force can substantially alter tactile images, pushing them farther from the demonstration distribution. Despite these pixel-level changes, contact evolution—whether an imprint deepens, slides, or rotates—often remains predictable. We therefore argue for implicit tactile dynamics modeling : predicting contact evolution in a dynamics-aware representation space rather than reconstructing future tactile observations. Latent prediction is a step toward this goal, but not every latent space is equally suited to forecasting tactile evolution. N0-VTLA (NeoteAI Team et al., 2026b) predicts the net tactile change at a single horizon in the feature space of a frozen DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023). However, DINOv2 is trained on static natural images and is not explicitly optimized to capture frame-to-frame tactile evolution; recent tactile representation learning also shows the limitations of single-frame encoders in model-ing such dynamics (Feng et al., 2026). In contrast, V-JEPA 2.1 (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026) learns representations by predicting masked spatio-temporal features in videos, encouraging its encoder to capture predictable motion rather than exact pixel details. Building on this objective, we learn a target space from tactile video clips that captures temporal change, while preserving spatial structure to support robust prediction under tactile distribution shifts. Moreover, because a single net change can hide intermediate contact events when a squeeze or slip returns to a similar final state, we predict both future representations and their changes from the present at multiple horizons. This captures contact evolution rather than only its endpoint. In this paper, we present TacDyn-WAM, a heterogeneous visuo-tactile world action model whose two world-model experts use separate parameters and target spaces. The visual expert predicts future visual latents, while the Implicit Tactile Dynamics Expert (ITDE) predicts tactile evolution in the TacRep space. Joint attention couples their predictions at every layer and allows the Action Expert to use both. The ITDE requires only a single forward pass without iterative denoising. A separate Tactile Understanding Memory provides direct access to the current tactile state, while staged training progressively integrates the tactile modules with the pretrained experts. On UniVTAC (Chen et al., 2026), TacDyn-WAM achieves state-of-the-art-level performance us-ing only the provided demonstrations. It is competitive with the two strongest models on this benchmark, N0-VTLA and N0-TWAM, both of which rely on large-scale visuo-tactile pretrain-ing. Notably, N0-TWAM follows the video-generation recipe and has roughly twice the parameters of TacDyn-WAM. Ablations show the benefits of both tactile pathways and TacRep’s advantage over pixel-reconstruction and static alternatives. On five real-robot tasks, TacDyn-WAM trained on per-task demonstrations alone reaches 71.0% average success, 16 points above the tactile policy FTP-1, and modest-scale pretraining on about 6,000 real visuo-tactile trajectories raises it to 85.0%. In summary, this work makes the following contributions: • We propose TacDyn-WAM, a heterogeneous visuo-tactile world action model that jointly predicts future visual latents and tactile evolution in distinct target spaces, while a read-only Tactile Understanding Memory provides the current tactile state. • We introduce TacRep, a dynamics-aware representation space for implicit tactile dynamics prediction, enabling one-pass multi-horizon prediction without pixel reconstruction or iter-ative denoising. • TacDyn-WAM achieves state-of-the-art-level performance on UniVTAC using only the provided demonstrations, and ablations validate the design. On a real robot, it outperforms vision-only and tactile baselines and benefits further from modest-scale tactile pretraining.

Why the Target Space Matters. As argued in Section 1, deployment drift can expose a policy to tactile images outside the demonstration distribution. Their pixels may change substantially even when the underlying physical trend remains broadly consistent. Reconstructing future pixels or VAE latents ties predictions to these unstable pixel patterns and may therefore produce unreliable forecasts under such shifts. We instead let the ITDE predict tactile dynamics in a representation space designed to capture contact evolution while tolerating pixel-level deviations. Context Tokens and Future Queries. As shown in Figure 3, the frozen TacRep Encoder converts the four-frame tactile clip from each sensor into two temporal feature maps. Each map is average-pooled to a 4 × 4 grid and projected to the ITDE hidden dimension, yielding 32 TacRep Context Tokens per sensor. For each of the three prediction horizons h ∈ H = {5, 15 , 25 } frames, we append 16 learnable Future Token Queries arranged on a 4 × 4 spatial grid. Both context and future tokens receive spatial and modality embeddings, together with a continuous time embedding determined by their frame offsets. The current robot state st is injected after every Transformer layer through a zero-initialized conditioning block.

It has the same layer geometry as the visual and action experts so that all experts can share attention, but it is initialized from scratch. The outputs of the 32 context tokens and the 48 future tokens together form the tactile block T that the other experts read.

Compact Tactile Memory. The understanding pathway summarizes the current tactile state: we encode the same four-frame tactile clip with a frozen AnyTouch2 encoder (Feng et al., 2026) pre-trained on a large multi-sensor corpus. Since its output contains several hundred tokens per sensor, the Compact Attentive Pooler compresses them into 10 tokens: one global token from the class em-bedding and nine local tokens obtained by pooling the latest temporal feature map into a 3 × 3 grid. These tokens are refined through cross-attention over the full AnyTouch2 sequence. A two-layer 

Compact Tactile Q-Former then fuses tokens across sensors through self-attention and conditions them on the prefix U through cross-attention. We use AnyTouch2 only in the understanding path-way: its reconstruction-based pretraining preserves fine-grained tactile information about the current contact, whereas the ITDE operates in the TacRep space designed specifically for predicting future tactile dynamics without pixel reconstruction. 

Read-Only Integration. The memory tokens stay outside the residual streams of all experts. Each joint-attention layer maps them into additional keys and values through its own lightweight pro-jections, and only the experts permitted by the causal mask can read them. Because the memory contributes no queries, its tokens are never updated. They occupy fixed position IDs right after the prefix, add only about 1% to the parameter count, and leave the original blocks unaffected when masked out. 2.5 HETEROGENEOUS JOINT ATTENTION AND STAGED TRAINING 

Joint Attention. At each layer, the prefix, visual, tactile, and action blocks compute separate queries, keys, and values, which are concatenated and processed by joint attention under a block-wise causal mask (Figure 4). In the final stage, the prefix attends only to itself; the visual and tactile blocks attend to the prefix, memory, and both world model blocks; and the action block attends to all context and causally to itself. 

Staged Training. Because the ITDE, tactile memory, and readouts are randomly initialized, training the entire model from the outset could disrupt the pretrained experts. We therefore use four stages and gradually open their attention connections. Stage 1: TacRep Training learns the tactile represen-tation as described in Section 2.2 and produces the frozen TacRep Encoder used by all later stages. 

Stage 2: Tactile World Grounding trains the ITDE and its readouts with Ltac , while the other experts remain frozen; the ITDE reads the prefix and visual block but is not yet visible to them. Stage 3: Tactile–Action Alignment trains the tactile memory and Action Expert with Lact , allowing the action block to read both tactile pathways. # 3 EXPERIMENTS 

3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmark. UniVTAC (Chen et al., 2026) is a challenging simulation benchmark for contact-rich manipulation, featuring a parallel gripper with a visuo-tactile sensor on each finger. It comprises eight tasks spanning pose reasoning, shape perception, and precise contact interaction (Table 1). 

Baselines. Table 1 compares TacDyn-WAM against vision-only and tactile baselines, including three tactile methods with large-scale tactile pretraining; we report their published UniVTAC results and re-evaluate our base model InternVLA-A1 under our own protocol (Appendix F). 

Protocol and Implementation. We use the marker-free tactile RGB images from the GelSight Mini sensors on both gripper fingers, together with a head camera and a wrist camera. Following the benchmark, we train one policy per task on the demonstrations it provides for that task and report the success rate over 100 rollouts per task. Training details are given in Appendix D and evaluation details in Appendix H. 3.2 MAIN RESULTS ON UNI VTAC Table 1 reports the results. TacDyn-WAM reaches an average success rate of 81.5%, second only to the two N0 models, and is within 3 points of the best baseline on Insert Tube, Pull-out Key, Lift Bottle, and Grasp Classify. 

Comparison with Vision-Only Models. TacDyn-WAM improves its base model InternVLA-A1 by 25.7 points and exceeds StarVLA-α, the strongest vision-only VLA, by 25.4 points. The vision-only world action models fall further behind, with the best of them, Fast-WAM, at 48.0%, and TacDyn-WAM reaches at least 72% on seven of the eight tasks. 

Comparison with Tactile Models. The UniVTAC policy and VITaL, which add a tactile encoder to ACT, reach 48.0% and 40.5%; RDP and TacForcing add execution-time tactile adaptation and reach 42.2% and 65.3%, while Tactile-WAM, with tactile asymmetric attention, reaches 30.0%. FTP-1 is pretrained on about 3,000 hours of tactile data, yet stays 18.6 points below TacDyn-WAM. N0-VTLA and N0-TWAM are pretrained on more than 30,000 hours of visuo-tactile trajectories from six embodiments and 450 tasks, and N0-TWAM has 7.2B parameters. TacDyn-WAM has about 3.6B parameters and is trained on the per-task demonstrations alone, yet it is only 1.6 and 3.0 points behind them, and it beats N0-TWAM on Pull-out Key, Lift Bottle, and Grasp Classify. When N0-TWAM is pretrained on 20% of its corpus, its average drops to 65.4%, 16.1 points below ours. We further pretrain TacDyn-WAM on a modest amount of real visuo-tactile data as the starting point for the real-robot experiments in Section 3.5. 

Data. OmniViTac (Zheng et al., 2026) is a real-world visuo-tactile dataset of 21,879 trajectories over 86 tasks, collected with an xArm-7 and a handheld TacUMI device with the same parallel-jaw gripper. Since our real-robot tasks are mostly related to assembly, we select a subset of 6,000 tra-jectories: 2,500 from assembly, 2,000 from grasping, 1,000 from wiping, and 500 from adjustment, leaving out cutting and peeling. We also include the 300 demonstrations that we collected for the five real-robot tasks in Section 3.5, 60 per task. Both sources use the same Xense vision-based tactile sensor on each finger, and we directly use its output tactile difference images, which already remove the no-contact background.

# A RELATED WORK 

A.1 VISION –T ACTILE –L ANGUAGE –A CTION MODELS 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models such as the π series transfer large-scale vision-language pretraining to robot control (Black et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2026a). Because vision alone cannot fully resolve local contact geometry, stability, or interaction forces, vision–tactile–language–action (VTLA) models additionally condition policies on tactile observa-tions. Representative approaches include Tactile-VLA, OmniVTLA, and N0-VTLA, and further work extends this line to tactile-language control, contact-aware fusion, and tactile–force align-ment (Huang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026; NeoteAI Team et al., 2026b; Hao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a;b; Huang et al., 2026b; Jones et al., 2025). Despite their different fusion mechanisms, these methods use tactile signals mainly for perception, where the policy reacts to the current or recent tactile observation. VLA-Touch further introduces tactile feedback at both the VLM and action-refinement levels, but likewise does not explicitly model future tactile evolution (Bi et al., 2026b). In contrast, TacDyn-WAM combines its current-state Tactile Understanding Memory with an explicit predictive tactile dynamics pathway. A complementary line uses tactile or force signals for reactive action refinement, ranging from slow– fast policies such as Reactive Diffusion Policy and OmniVTA to residual correction (Xue et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026; Bi et al., 2026b; Zhou et al., 2026b; He et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c; Zhou et al., 2026a). These methods target rapid feedback after contact, whereas TacDyn-WAM models tactile dynamics inside the world model rather than in an execution-time correction stage. A.2 TACTILE REPRESENTATION LEARNING 

Tactile representations are commonly learned through reconstruction or alignment across sensors and modalities (Shan et al., 2026). Reconstruction and masked-modeling methods preserve local detail, whereas alignment across sensors and modalities improves transfer and semantic grounding; representative examples include Sparsh, M3L, UniT, AnyTouch, and AnyTouch 2 (Higuera et al., 2024; Sferrazza et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; 2026). Subsequent work extends these ideas to visuo-tactile pretraining, language-grounded fusion, and generalist policies across tactile sensors (George et al., 2025; Jones et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2026a). However, reconstruction ties features to exact sensor pixel patterns, and semantic alignment can discard subtle temporal cues such as slip, compression, or contact rotation. ViTacFormer predicts future touch as an auxiliary policy objective (Heng et al., 2025), but explicit predictive representation learning remains less explored. Inspired by the joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) paradigm (Assran et al., 2025; Mur-Labadia et al., 2026), TacRep instead learns a target space from tactile clips that emphasizes predictable contact evolution without reconstructing future pixels. It further regularizes local patch relations with a structural prior, so that the spatial structure of the contact pattern remains stable when tactile inputs deviate from the demonstrations. A.3 TACTILE WORLD ACTION MODELS 

World action models (WAMs) couple future-state prediction with action generation. Existing WAMs predominantly model visual or semantic dynamics (Yuan et al., 2026c; Ye et al., 2026c; Bi et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). Recent tactile WAMs can be distinguished by their prediction targets and inference procedures. 

Generative tactile prediction. A prominent family reuses video-generation pipelines, such as 

N0-TWAM, Dream-Tac, VTAM, and Tactile-WAM: they encode tactile images with the same video VAE as camera frames and generate future tactile observations through iterative denoising (NeoteAI Team et al., 2026a; Lou et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026b; Wu et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2026b; Jin et al., 2026). OmniVTA and VT-WAM instead learn a tactile-specific VAE before generative prediction (Zheng et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2026a). Although these designs retain fine spatial detail, their targets remain reconstruction-oriented and inference requires multiple denoising steps. Moreover, pixel similarity does not always track physical similarity: small tactile-image changes may indicate critical slip or rotation, whereas larger pixel changes may be less relevant to control. 14 Preprint 

Feature and low-dimensional prediction. TacForeSight and ContactWorld avoid image gener-ation by predicting compact tactile features in one forward pass (Zang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c), and N0-VTLA predicts the net change of frozen DINOv2 features over a single hori-zon (NeoteAI Team et al., 2026b). Force-aware WAMs instead predict numeric interaction signals, such as wrist wrench in FAWAM or finger torque in TACO (He et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). These targets reduce prediction cost, but their utility depends on whether the representation preserves the spatial and temporal contact structure needed for control. These approaches shift the central design question from how accurately tactile pixels can be reconstructed to which latent dynamics are useful for choosing actions. TacDyn-WAM follows the one-pass predictive direction, but predicts in a target space learned for tactile dynamics rather than a frozen or reconstruction-oriented one, and keeps this space separate from the visual latent space of the base model. A.4 TACTILE FUSION MECHANISMS 

Tactile fusion mechanisms can be broadly grouped into attention conditioning, contact-dependent gating, and expert-structured fusion (Shan et al., 2026). Token-based VTLA models typically insert tactile features into a shared sequence or expose them through self- or cross-attention; Tactile-VLA and OmniVTLA are representative examples (Huang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026; Hao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; NeoteAI Team et al., 2026b). This design is expressive, but unrestricted fusion can allow a strong vision-language pathway to dominate optimization or let noisy free-space touch interfere with visual features. Gated methods make tactile influence dependent on contact state. TacVLA and ReTac-ACT learn gates conditioned on the contact or robot state, while OmniVTA uses a lightweight module to fuse the difference between predicted and observed tactile features (Zhang et al., 2026b; Ruan et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). Similar principles appear as contact-derived attention biases or asymmetric masks in tactile WAMs (Lou et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). Expert-structured methods instead preserve modality-specific parameters: ForceVLA uses a force-aware MoE, while N0-TWAM and VT-WAM separate visual, tactile, and action experts (Yu et al., 2025; NeoteAI Team et al., 2026a; Tian et al., 2026a). Gating and expert separation solve related but different problems: a gate controls the contribution of touch across interaction phases (Wang et al., 2026a), whereas expert separation limits parameter interference between heterogeneous modalities. TacDyn-WAM likewise specializes parameters by modality, but does not rely on a learned sparse router. Its experts communicate through block-wise joint attention under stage-specific causal masks. In parallel, the Tactile Understanding Memory enters only as read-only keys and values rather than as an additional expert. This design separates current contact understanding, future con-tact prediction, and action generation, while staged training prevents randomly initialized tactile modules from immediately disrupting the pretrained experts.

Raw tactile images contain a fixed gel texture, an illumination gradient, and lens vignetting that are identical in every frame and carry no information about contact. Subtracting the background removes these static patterns, flattens the non-contact area to a uniform value, and leaves only the deformation caused by the current contact, which makes the input closer to what the model should attend to and reduces the patches that the TacRep Encoder must learn to ignore.

# F BASELINES 

We compare with the vision-only and visuo-tactile baselines listed in Table 1. Except for InternVLA-A1, all results are taken from the published UniVTAC evaluations of the respective papers. 

Vision-only Policies. π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025) is a VLA model built on π0 that co-trains on heterogeneous data and decodes action chunks with a flow-matching action expert. StarVLA-α (Ye et al., 2026b) is a deliberately minimal VLA design that pairs a strong vision-language backbone with a simple action head. InternVLA-A1 (Cai et al., 2026a) is our base model, a mixture-of-transformers world action model with understanding, visual generation, and action ex-perts (Section 2.1); we re-evaluate it under the same protocol as TacDyn-WAM so that the gain over the base model is measured under identical conditions. Xiaomi-Robotics-0 (Cai et al., 2026b) is an open-source VLA model trained on large-scale cross-embodiment trajectories and vision-language data with asynchronous real-time execution. GigaWorld-Policy (Ye et al., 2026a) is an action-centered world action model that learns pixel-action dynamics with optional video gener-ation. LingBot-VA (Li et al., 2026) is an autoregressive diffusion framework that learns frame prediction and policy execution in a shared latent space with a mixture-of-transformers backbone. Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026c) keeps video co-training during training but skips future prediction at test time. None of these methods uses tactile input. 

Visuo-tactile Policies. ACT+UniVTAC (Chen et al., 2026) augments ACT with the UniVTAC tactile encoder, which is pretrained on simulated tactile data with reconstruction and geometric supervision. VITaL (George et al., 2025) pretrains a visuo-tactile encoder and feeds its features to an ACT-style policy. RDP (Xue et al., 2025) is a slow-fast design in which a slow latent diffusion policy predicts action chunks and a fast asymmetric tokenizer performs closed-loop tactile feedback control at high frequency. TacForcing (Zhou et al., 2026a) replaces the action expert of a VLA with a streaming action expert that conditions actions nearing execution on the tactile observations acquired during execution. Tactile-WAM (Wu et al., 2026) is a world action model that predicts future tactile latents with a video VAE and uses asymmetric attention so that video queries do not read tactile keys while action queries do. The last three methods in this group are additionally pretrained on large-scale tactile data. FTP-1 (Yuan et al., 2026a) is a generalist tactile policy that maps image, array, and state-based tactile signals from 21 sensors into unified latent tokens through heterogeneous encoders, pretrained on about 3,000 hours of tactile manipulation data. N0-VTLA (NeoteAI Team et al., 2026b) builds on π0.5, encodes tactile difference images with a frozen DINOv2, and conditions the action expert on latent tokens that predict the net tactile change over the action chunk. N0-TWAM (NeoteAI Team et al., 2026a) is a 7.2B video diffusion world action model with separate video, tactile, and action experts, which predicts future video and tactile frames in a shared VAE latent space by iterative denoising. Both N0 models are pretrained on more than 30,000 hours of visuo-tactile robot data from six embodiments and 450 tasks; the 20% data variant of N0-TWAM is pretrained on one fifth of this corpus.

Platform. The robot is a Franka Research 3 arm with a parallel gripper. Each finger carries one Xense vision-based tactile sensor, and the scene is observed by a wrist camera and a third-person camera. Demonstrations are collected by teleoperation with a leader arm, 60 per task. Figure 7 shows the five tasks. 

Tasks. Each task is specified by a fixed language instruction, and a trial is successful only if the final state meets the criterion below. • Stack Cups (“pick up the paper cup and stack it on top of the other cup”): grasp a paper cup and nest it onto a second cup. The trial succeeds if the cup rests stably inside the other cup. Misaligned cups tend to jam, and a policy that keeps pushing downward fails, whereas a policy that lifts and re-aligns can recover. • Remove Plug (“grasp the plug and pull it out of the socket”): grasp a plug seated in a socket and pull it out.
