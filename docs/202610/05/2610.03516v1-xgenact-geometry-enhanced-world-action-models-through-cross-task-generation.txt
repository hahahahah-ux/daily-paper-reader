ABSTRACT
World action models (WAMs) have advanced robot control by predicting how
observations and actions evolve over time. Despite this progress, RGB and ac-
tion based future prediction does not explicitly address the spatial understanding
needed for robot manipulation. Existing efforts often add a limited set of spatial
prediction tasks through specialized heads or branches, leaving both the range of
spatial supervision and the model architecture fragmented. We introduce XGen-
Act, a world action model that represents RGB observations, robot actions, metric
depth, surface normals, and functional role segmentation as RGB videos through
deterministic codecs. By sampling perception and action tasks during training,
XGenAct uses one video diffusion transformer and one objective to learn tem-
poral prediction across these spaces without modality specific learned heads. On
held out RLBench tasks, structured perception training improves average closed
loop success over RGB only training, and XGenAct achieves 52% success in the
five task external comparison, versus 26% for the strongest evaluated baselines.
It also predicts future depth and segmentation more accurately than the evaluated
pipelines that generate RGB first and then apply a frozen perception expert.

INTRODUCTION
World action models (WAMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for generative robot control
by jointly predicting future RGB videos and robot actions (Ye et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025; Zhen
et al., 2026). Compared with vision language action models that directly predict actions, WAMs

use video generation to explicitly model how a scene evolves over time. This generative objective
provides dense supervision for visual dynamics and temporal context, which can in turn improve
action prediction. WAMs therefore offer a direct way to address the temporal reasoning required for
robot control. However, their progress has been primarily temporal rather than spatial. Robots oper-
ate in a three dimensional world that evolves over time, while most WAMs represent the predicted
world only as two dimensional RGB video. Although RGB observations contain implicit geometric
cues, appearance prediction alone does not explicitly capture metric depth, surface orientation, or
other spatial structures required for physical interaction. This limited spatial understanding remains
a major bottleneck to further improving WAMs.
Recent work has begun to address this limitation by introducing spatial information into WAMs.
One possible direction is to predict explicit three dimensional or four dimensional representations,
such as point clouds or point trajectories. (Zhen et al., 2025; Dharmarajan et al., 2025) While these
representations provide rich geometric structure, incorporating dense reconstruction directly into a
generative control model requires additional representations and decoding procedures, making ef-
ficient joint prediction with robot actions nontrivial. A more practical alternative is to supervise
spatial understanding through image aligned signals, including depth, optical flow, geometric fea-
tures, and semantic maps. Several recent WAMs have adopted this strategy to enrich RGB based
future prediction (Ma et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). However,
these spatial signals are typically introduced through auxiliary prediction heads, gated residual mod-
ules, or modality specific branches, each with its own representation and training objective. Their
coverage also remains fragmented. Depth receives the most attention, while few methods incorpo-
rate complementary information such as surface orientation and task dependent functional regions
within a single model. We argue that spatial supervision should not remain an auxiliary component
attached to an RGB based WAM. Instead, multiple geometric and semantic prediction tasks should
be expressed within the same generative space used to model observations and actions, allowing
structured spatial knowledge to directly shape the shared action generator.
Inspired by ACTIONIMAGES Zhen et al. (2026), which represents 7 DoF robot actions as multi-
view RGB videos, we consider image space as a common interface for both perception and control.
This representation is particularly suitable for structured visual signals because metric depth, surface
normals, and segmentation can also be expressed as decodable RGB images (Gabeur et al., 2026;
Wang et al., 2026). Based on this insight, we introduce XGENACT, a geometry enhanced world
action model that represents RGB observations, metric depth, surface normals, functional role seg-
mentation, and 7 DoF robot actions as RGB videos through deterministic codecs. Every stream is
encoded by the same frozen video VAE into a shared latent space. During training, each sample
selects a perception action template that pairs one perception stream with the action stream, rather
than combining all perception modalities simultaneously. All templates are processed by the same
diffusion transformer, output head, and flow matching objective, without modality specific learned
heads, auxiliary losses, or manually balanced loss coefficients. By varying the perception task across
training samples, this cross task formulation exposes the shared action generator to complementary
geometric and semantic supervision. Experiments on held-out RLBench tasks demonstrate that
XGENACT achieves the highest average closed-loop success among eight external policy baselines
while retaining the ability to predict future RGB, depth, surface normals, and segmentation.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• Geometry enriched image space. We extend image space world action modeling beyond
RGB and actions by encoding depth, surface normals, and functional role segmentation in
the same visual modality, adding complementary geometric and semantic information.
• Head free and extensible task interface. Task templates and conditioning plans enable
all perception and action tasks to share the same frozen VAE, DiT, output head, and flow
matching objective, without modality specific learned heads or auxiliary losses.
• Improved robot control through cross-task generation. Experiments on held-out RL-
Bench tasks show that structured geometric and semantic generation improves average
closed-loop success by up to 28.75 percentage points over RGB-only training at matched
compute and action budgets, while retaining future RGB generation.

RELATED WORK
Structured world prediction for action learning.
World action models (WAMs) jointly learn
structured visual future prediction and robot action generation, often using a shared video diffu-
sion backbone (Ye et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025; Zhen et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Chen et al.,
2026b). While most WAMs predict RGB futures, recent methods introduce geometric or semantic
supervision. PAD (Guo et al., 2024) extends joint image and action denoising to depth using sepa-
rate modality encoders and decoders. GeoSem-WAM (Ma et al., 2026), DreamWAM (Yuan et al.,
2026a), WAM4D (Li et al., 2026), and X-WAM (Guo et al., 2026) incorporate geometry through
auxiliary branches, residual modules, or training-only readouts. RynnWorld-4D (Zhao et al., 2026)
generates RGB, depth, and optical flow using a three branch architecture, then predicts actions with
a downstream inverse dynamics head. These methods demonstrate the value of structured future
supervision, but rely on modality specific components, objectives, or action modules.
SA-WAM (Lopetegui Gonzalez et al., 2026) is closest to our representation. It encodes RGB and
metric depth with the same frozen video tokenizer and jointly denoises them with actions in one la-
tent sequence. However, actions occupy dedicated latent positions, and the model uses a fixed RGB,
depth, and action layout. In contrast, XGENACT represents both structured perception and action as
video streams and samples multiple perception action templates within one shared generative model.
Perception as generation.
A complementary line of work formulates visual understanding
through image or video generation. Painter (Wang et al., 2023) and DICEPTION (Zhao et al.,
2025) express multiple perception tasks in a common visual space. Vision Banana (Gabeur et al.,
2026) and GenCeption (Wang et al., 2026) generate depth, segmentation, surface normals, and cor-
respondence from task prompts. SUV (Yuan et al., 2026b) applies a similar formulation to driving,
but retains a separate action expert. These methods establish generation as a common interface for
perception, but do not examine whether a mixture of structured perception tasks can improve a robot
action generator expressed in the same output space.
Visual representations of action.
RT-Trajectory (Gu et al., 2023) and TraceVLA (Zheng et al.,
2025) augment observations with visual motion cues but still predict action tokens. PIVOT (Nasiri-
any et al., 2024) visualizes candidate actions for a vision language model to select. Action Im-
ages (Zhen et al., 2026) instead represents a 7-DoF end effector pose as multiview RGB canvases
generated by a video model. XGENACT extends this image space beyond RGB observations and
actions by incorporating metric depth, surface normals, and functional role segmentation. This con-
nects perception as generation with action as generation, allowing multiple geometric and semantic
tasks to supervise one action generator without modality specific heads or auxiliary losses.

3.1
OVERVIEW
We build XGENACT on the Action Images formulation (Zhen et al., 2026) using a Wan2.2-TI2V-
5B video diffusion backbone (Wan Team, 2025). Following Action Images, a 7-DoF robot action
is projected into multiple camera views and rendered as an RGB video that can be decoded back
into continuous control. We retain this action representation without modification. The video VAE
remains frozen, and only the diffusion transformer is fine-tuned.
Our key idea is to extend this shared video space with geometry-related perception tasks. RGB
observations, metric depth, surface normals, functional role segmentation, and robot actions are
converted into RGB videos through deterministic codecs and encoded by the same frozen VAE. This
places appearance, geometry, semantics, and control in a common latent space without introducing
modality specific learned encoders or output heads.

RGB and action.
RGB observations use the identity mapping. Robot actions use the Action
Images codec (Zhen et al., 2026), which projects each 7-DoF end effector pose into the camera
views and renders the resulting trajectories as RGB videos. We use this representation unchanged.
Metric depth.
Raw metric depth has an unbounded and highly nonuniform value distribution,
making direct grayscale encoding inefficient. We first compress depth values with a monotonic
power transform and then map the resulting scalar values onto a continuous path through the RGB
cube. At decoding time, generated colors are projected back onto this path before the scalar trans-
form is inverted. This encoding distributes metric depth across an RGB path instead of a single
grayscale axis. Full definitions and decoding rules are provided in Appendix C.

Functional role segmentation.
We assign each functional role a fixed RGB color code shared
across tasks, episodes, camera views, and frames. The labels describe how a region participates
in the task, such as target object, goal region, robot, tool, fixture, distractor, or background, rather
than its semantic object category. This provides task relevant structural supervision while remaining
compatible with the same video representation.
Before training, each codec is evaluated both directly and after a frozen VAE round trip. A new
perception task can therefore be added through a deterministic codec, without introducing a learned
modality specific encoder, decoder, or output head.
3.3
TASK TEMPLATES AND CONDITIONING PLANS
Each training sample selects one perception modality m and forms the template introduced in Eq.
1. The four segments correspond to perception and action streams from two camera views. Their
order remains fixed across modalities. Sampling different templates changes the perception task
associated with action generation, while the model architecture remains unchanged.
A conditioning plan determines which parts of the packed sequence are observed. For each segment,
we use one of three states: F provides the full segment, I provides its initial frame and leaves the
rest to be generated, and 1 includes only the initial frame. This yields three settings:
ρjoint = (I, I, I, I),
ρpolicy = (1, I, 1, I),
ρinverse = (F, I, F, I).
(4)
The joint plan predicts future perception and actions together. The policy plan predicts future actions
from the current perception. The inverse dynamics plan predicts actions from a fully observed
perception sequence. The same plans apply to every perception modality.
During training, both the template and conditioning plan are sampled for each example. All tem-
plates use the same plan distribution. We also occasionally remove the action segments, allowing
the model to learn future perception generation from an initial frame alone. Sampling probabilities
are provided in the experimental setup.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate XGENACT on three questions: (i) how it compares with existing robot policies in
closed loop evaluation (§4.2); (ii) whether generating structured perception during training improves
the action policy, and how perception spaces affect the action performance(§4.3); and (iii) whether
generating future geometry and semantics is more accurate than generating RGB and applying a
frozen geometry perception model (§4.4).
4.1
SETUP
Training menus.
We name a training configuration by the perception templates in its menu:
RGB trains on video+action only, and D, N and S add depth+action, normal+action
and segmentation+action, respectively. The most inclusive menu, RGB+D+N+S, draws
video+action with probability 0.4 and each perception template with probability 0.2. Every
model is fine-tuned from the released Action Images checkpoint (Zhen et al., 2026) with the same
backbone, frozen VAE, objective, conditioning-plan distribution and optimizer (effective batch 4,
learning rate 5 × 10−7; Appendix E), and is evaluated at step 4,000.
Training groups.
We train three groups of models, one per question. Two training sources are
used: a self-generated RLBench tree (James et al., 2020) of 16 tasks and 3,960 episodes at 5122,
and a ManiSkill3 tree (Tao et al., 2025) of 7 tasks and 1,750 episodes (Appendix B). (i) For the
comparison with robot policies, XGENACT (RGB+D+N+S) and all baselines except the zero-shot
MolmoAct are fine-tuned on the same RLBench tree. (ii) For the menu ablation, eight models,
one per menu, are trained on RLBench and ManiSkill3 sampled at a 70/30 ratio. (iii) For the
perception study, a separate set of menu models is trained on the same 70/30 mixture; an RLBench-
only RGB+D+N+S model is included as unmatched context. All groups are evaluated on RLBench
tasks absent from training; ManiSkill3 serves only as a training source.
Evaluation.
Closed-loop control runs 20 trials per task with receding-horizon replanning under the
IIII plan, capped at 1.5× the demonstration length; success is RLBench’s task-success signal, and
methods run in our rollout harness are paired by scene seed. Future perception is scored against the
simulator state produced by each route’s own decoded actions: LPIPS for RGB (Zhang et al., 2018),
absolute relative error for depth, mean cosine similarity for normals, and mIoU for segmentation.

ject layout coincide with the ground-truth cloud, and the visible differences lie on the arm, whose
predicted pose runs ahead of or behind the demonstration.
The imagined future also matches what the robot then does. Appendix J.3 pairs generated RGB
frames with simulator frames reached by executing their decoded actions; the plan is carried out as
imagined rather than replaced by a different motion at execution time.

CONCLUSION
We presented XGENACT, which expresses robot actions, future RGB video, metric depth, surface
normals, and functional role segmentation through one video-generation interface. Deterministic
codecs let these tasks share a frozen video VAE, diffusion transformer, output head, and objective,
without modality-specific learned heads or auxiliary losses. On held-out RLBench tasks, structured
perception improves closed-loop success over RGB-only training, while direct generation predicts
future depth and segmentation more accurately than the evaluated RGB-to-expert pipelines. The best
training menu remains task-dependent, motivating broader evaluations and adaptive task balancing.

Template distribution.

camera of its view. The absolute extrinsics and intrinsics are used only to project and decode action
images.

Camera parameters.
The training tree randomizes camera poses per episode (Appendix B),
whereas rollouts use RLBench’s fixed default cameras. XGENACT receives the camera through
Pl¨ucker embeddings and its action projection (Appendix F). A baseline marked in Table 6 receives,
per view, the 3 × 4 extrinsic matrix and the intrinsics (fx, fy, cx, cy) rescaled to its input resolution,
16 values per view. The π0.5 and LaWAM rows are camera-free variants.
π0.5, LaWAM, Evo-1, and VLANeXt.
These four policies share one training budget: 20,500
steps at an effective batch size of 32 on the 16-task tree (3,960 episodes, 690,893 frames at 20 fps),
with 5122 renders resized to 2242. Each executes its action chunks as its official inference code
does. π0.5 predicts 50 SE(3) deltas relative to the current pose and executes 10. LaWAM and
VLANeXt predict 8 absolute poses and execute the whole chunk. VLANeXt conditions on an 8-
frame proprioceptive history, which its server rebuilds from the chunks it returned, because the client
state is the last commanded pose. We load its weights with zero missing or unexpected keys; it keeps
no EMA copy. Evo-1 executes the first 25 of 50 actions, as in both of its official clients. Its targets
are per-step deltas, since each state in the data equals the previous commanded action, so a chunk
is composed by accumulating the deltas. Its rotation deltas are computed by subtracting rotation
vectors, which wrap at ±π; 2.06% of training frames therefore carry a rotation delta near 2π. The
reported Evo-1 result consequently reflects this data-conversion defect and should not be interpreted
as an estimate of an otherwise correctly trained Evo-1 policy’s capability.
MolmoAct.
Following Zhen et al. (2026), we evaluate the released MolmoAct-7B-D checkpoint
zero-shot with the reasoning prompt from its model card and adapt its output to our controller as
follows. MolmoAct draws a 2D end-effector trace on its primary input image, so each replan queries
it twice, once with each view as the primary image. The two traces are resampled by arc length to 16
19


corresponding points, triangulated with the true camera matrices, and anchored to the current end-
effector position. Orientation is held at its current value; the gripper command is the mean of the
two predicted gripper values, thresholded at 0.5. All 16 waypoints are executed before replanning.
PAD-Depth.
PAD-Depth starts from the released Bridge-pretrained DiT-XL/2 (681M parameters).
It uses the frozen SD VAE and CLIP text encoder and is fine-tuned on the front view at 2562
together with metric depth and the front camera parameters. It predicts k = 3 future frames and
poses spaced 16 control steps apart, at global batch size 256 and learning rate 10−4. We evaluate
the step-2,560 checkpoint, which has seen 655,360 samples, the same exposure as the 20,500 × 32
budget above. We fixed three issues in the released training code. The depth loader referenced an
undefined attribute. The action loss was disabled for the first 50,000 steps; we enable it from step 0
with weight 1. The first future target was the current frame; we shift targets forward by one stride.
Sampling uses 75 DDIM steps, as in the paper. Following the paper, only the first predicted pose is
executed; it is linearly interpolated (orientation by slerp) into 16 waypoints to match the step budget
of other policies.
UWM.
UWM starts from the released DROID-pretrained checkpoint and is fine-tuned for 4,600
steps at batch size 36 and learning rate 10−4 with delta actions.
It observes the front and
overhead views at 1282 over two frames, together with a 42-dimensional state: a 10-dimensional
pose (position, 6D rotation, gripper) and 16 camera values per view. It predicts 16 actions and
executes 8.

VLA-JEPA
starts
from
the
released

(VLA-JEPA-Pretrain) and is fine-tuned with its LeRobot training code.
Our actions are
10-dimensional poses and our state is the 42-dimensional vector used for UWM, whereas the

encoder are re-initialized; all other weights, including the language backbone, are fine-tuned.
The latent world-model objective stays enabled with its default weight of 0.1. The front and
overhead views, at 2242, fill the two exterior-camera slots of the pretrained model. States are

our training tree. We train with AdamW (learning rate 10−4, β = (0.9, 0.95), gradient clipping at

evaluate the step-2,000 checkpoint. The policy predicts 7 absolute poses and executes the first 4
before replanning.
Closed-loop evaluation.
Each task is evaluated on variation 0 over 20 trials, and success is the
task-success signal reported by RLBench. MolmoAct, PAD-Depth, UWM, and VLA-JEPA run in
the same rollout harness as XGENACT, with scene seeds derived from (task, variation,
trial), 5122 renders, and RLBench’s planning-based end-effector controller. An episode ends at
success, at 1.5× the demonstration length, or after 20 consecutive inverse-kinematics failures; XGE-
NACT uses the same budget but ends after 20 inverse-kinematics failures in total. π0.5, LaWAM,
Evo-1, and VLANeXt are served behind a common WebSocket interface and driven by one model-
agnostic RLBench client; their episodes end at success, after 200 executed actions, or after 20
inverse-kinematics failures in total.
