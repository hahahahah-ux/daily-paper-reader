ABSTRACT
World action models jointly learn to forecast world dynamics and predict robot
actions, such that the learned internal world dynamics guide accurate actions. Ex-
isting approaches typically represent the world as RGB frames or latent counter-
parts while predicting actions as end-effector poses or joint angles, but they often
struggle to capture the 3D spatial structure and contact geometry central to dex-
terous manipulation. We introduce Point World Action Model (PointWAM), a 3D
world action model that decomposes the world into a scene (i.e., environment)
and hands (i.e., actor), and jointly forecasts both as 3D point trajectories within
a shared space-time coordinate frame. This explicit, disentangled representation
enables effective pre-training on large-scale human demonstration videos without
requiring any task-specific object or keypoint selection. Given a colored point
cloud and a language instruction, PointWAM predicts how the scene and hands
co-evolve in 3D space over time, then retargets the forecast hand motion to robot
actions. Pre-training on human videos improves average DexJoCo success by 56.9
percentage points, and scene-trajectory supervision adds 10.9 points over forecast-
ing the hands alone. With both, PointWAM surpasses the prior state of the art on
ten DexJoCo tasks by 11.7 points and outperforms strong VLAs on a real robot.

Dexterous robotic manipulation requires multiple fingers to precisely coordinate their 3D motion
relative to object surfaces (Salisbury & Craig, 1982; Okamura et al., 2000), enabling robots to per-

form a wide range of tasks from grasping objects of varied shapes to tool use (Bicchi, 2000; An et al.,
2026). Conventional Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) (Black et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al.,
2025) map observations to motor commands without explicitly forecasting how the scene evolves.
World Action Models (WAMs) have recently emerged as a promising alternative by treating world
and action modeling as deeply intertwined: they jointly learn to forecast how the world will change
and what actions cause those changes (Li et al., 2025a).
Many existing WAMs build on video generation backbones and represent the world as RGB
frames (Du et al., 2023) or their latent counterparts (Li et al., 2025a; Liao et al., 2026). These
representations are limited in their ability to capture key aspects of physical interaction, includ-
ing 3D spatial structure and causal relations between the actor and the environment. Point-based
policies (Haldar & Pinto, 2025; Kim et al., 2026a) address the 3D action prediction problem by
forecasting trajectories of 3D points, but do not model how the environment evolves. They also rely
on humans or external perception models to select task-relevant objects for their scene inputs. Con-
versely, action-conditioned 3D world models forecast dense 3D scene trajectories, yet treat robot
actions as inputs rather than quantities to be predicted (Huang et al., 2026).
In this work, we introduce Point World Action Model (PointWAM), a 3D world action model that
decomposes the world into a scene (i.e., environment) and hands (i.e., actor), and represents both
as three-dimensional point trajectories within a shared space-time coordinate frame (Fig. 1). This
explicit, disentangled world representation enables effective learning of cause-and-effect relations
between the environment and the actor by modeling how their points co-evolve in 3D space over
time. Given a colored point cloud and a language instruction, PointWAM forecasts trajectories for
every observed scene point and hand keypoint in a common coordinate frame. Its action retargeter
then converts the forecast hand trajectories into the corresponding robot actions.
The proposed approach offers three additional advantages. First, modeling both the scene and
hands in a shared 3D space enables effective pre-training on human demonstration videos from
EgoDex (Hoque et al., 2026) and VITRA (Li et al., 2026b). Second, no task-specific objects or
scene keypoints need to be selected in advance, so scene forecasting is not restricted to a predefined
set of target objects. Third, by decoupling hand-point trajectories from the scene, the forecast hand
motion is amenable to effective retargeting onto robot hands for action prediction.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows.
• We introduce PointWAM, which jointly forecasts the 3D trajectories of scene points and hand
keypoints in a shared space-time coordinate frame, unifying world and action modeling.
• We demonstrate that 3D world modeling benefits dexterous manipulation. Scene-trajectory su-
pervision improves average DexJoCo success by 10.9 percentage points over the same model
without it, and our 3D encoder outperforms lifted image features by 5.4 percentage points.
• We show that pre-training on human demonstrations from EgoDex and VITRA improves average
DexJoCo success by 56.9 percentage points over training from scratch. With this pre-training,
PointWAM surpasses the prior best multi-task success rate on DexJoCo by 11.7 percentage points
and outperforms robot-pre-trained VLAs on a real robot by up to 25 percentage points.

RELATED WORK
We discuss the closest work here and provide a broader review in Appendix A.
World action models (WAMs).
WAMs unify predictive world modeling with action prediction;
unlike conventional VLAs, which map a visual observation straight to a motor command, a WAM is
trained to forecast how the world will change and predict what actions the robot must perform (Du
et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026d; Li et al., 2025a). Existing WAMs
typically forecast future visual representations in the latent space of pretrained video models while
predicting robot actions as end-effector poses. These models must therefore learn how changes in
latent visual features correspond to robot motion, rather than explicitly modeling both scene and
hand motion as 3D trajectories. Closest to ours, EgoWAM (Li et al., 2026a), PointWorld (Huang
et al., 2026), and PointZero (Duisterhof et al., 2026) forecast dense 3D scene motion. EgoWAM
jointly learns scene forecasting and action prediction but maps human hand motion to the actions of

Human demonstration videos show hand
and scene motion from which trajectory supervision can be extracted without recorded robot actions.
Point-based policies can learn to forecast hand keypoint trajectories from these videos and retarget
the forecasts to robot actions. Point Policy (Haldar & Pinto, 2025) and Dexterous Point Policy (Kim
et al., 2026a), however, rely on humans or external perception models to select the objects in the
policy’s scene input, object keypoints specified by a human for Point Policy and object point clouds
segmented by external models for Dexterous Point Policy. Neither performs explicit world model-
ing through scene-motion forecasting. 3PoinTr (Hung et al., 2026) does forecast dense 3D scene
trajectories from human demonstration videos. Extracted video tracks supervise its forecasts, and a
separate policy trained on robot demonstrations predicts actions from the forecast tracks. PointWAM
instead forecasts trajectories for every observed scene point and hand keypoint with one backbone,
and a retargeter converts the hand trajectories into robot actions without a separate policy.

3.2
3D TRAJECTORY FORECASTER
Scene and hand features. We first encode the observed scene point cloud and hand keypoints.
A frozen Mosaic3D encoder (Lee et al., 2025), pre-trained to align 3D point features with text,
extracts a feature vector for each scene point that captures local geometry and semantics. For each
hand keypoint, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) maps a one-hot encoding of its index, concatenated
with a hand ID, to a feature of the same dimension as the scene features.

3.3
ACTION RETARGETER
The action retargeter maps forecast hand trajectories and the robot state to T robot actions; scene
forecasts supervise the shared backbone but do not enter the retargeter. To construct its input, we
encode the hand trajectories at each future step, add a step embedding, and append a state embedding
to form a context Ct for action prediction. A transformer decoder (Vaswani et al., 2017) then uses
this context and learnable action queries to predict the action chunk At:

3.4
LEARNING FROM HUMAN AND ROBOT DEMONSTRATIONS
We train the trajectory forecaster on human and robot demonstrations and the action retargeter on
robot demonstrations alone, since only they record the end-effector poses and finger joint commands
that the action retargeter learns to predict (Fig. 1, left).
Human demonstrations. Human demonstration videos lack recorded states, so we estimate both
trajectories from the videos themselves. Hand trajectories come from the 3D hand poses annotated
in EgoDex (Hoque et al., 2026) and reconstructed by VITRA (Li et al., 2026b), mapped to the robot
hand’s ten keypoints. Scene trajectories come from dense 2D point tracks of CoTracker3 (Karaev
et al., 2025), lifted to 3D with monocular depth from VGGT-Ω(Wang et al., 2026b) scaled to the an-
notated hands. The resulting 1.15M episodes share the trajectory format of the robot demonstrations,
so both supervise the same forecaster; Appendix B.1 details the preprocessing.
Robot demonstrations. Recorded robot states give hand trajectories through forward kinematics.
Scene trajectories come from replaying DexJoCo (Wang et al., 2026a) demonstrations, which move
each initial point with its object or rigid part, and on the real robot from CoTracker3 (Karaev et al.,
2025) tracks lifted with Fast-FoundationStereo (Wen et al., 2026) depth. We process the RoboDojo-
Precision demonstrations similarly to DexJoCo (Appendix B.2).
Training. We first pre-train the backbone and both trajectory heads from scratch on human demon-
strations using scene and hand trajectory supervision. On robot demonstrations, we then fine-tune
these components and train the retargeter from scratch with joint trajectory and action supervision:

EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we describe the experimental setup (Section 4.1) and answer three questions.
(1) Does PointWAM, pre-trained only on human demonstrations, outperform policies pre-trained
on robot or video corpora in dexterous and high-precision manipulation? (Section 4.2)
(2) Does human-demonstration pre-training transfer to robot control, and does it scale with the
number of human demonstrations and with model size? (Section 4.3)
(3) Does 3D world modeling enable more precise dexterous manipulation than 2D world modeling
or a model without it, and does a 3D encoder help beyond lifted 2D features? (Section 4.4)
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We pre-train PointWAM on 1.15M human demonstration episodes from EgoDex (Hoque et al.,
2026) and VITRA (Li et al., 2026b), which provide scene and hand trajectories but no robot actions,
and fine-tune it on the robot demonstrations of each benchmark and of the real robot.
4.1.1
BENCHMARKS AND PROTOCOLS

single-arm and five bimanual, on Franka Panda arms with 16-DoF Allegro hands. Its tasks require

placement setting, where no policy reported with the benchmark exceeds 5.3% success on bimanual
assembly. We exclude bimanual unlock iPad, since 42 of its 100 demonstrations fail when replayed
in the released environment; 959 of the other 1,000 demonstrations pass the replay check. We train
one policy across the ten tasks (Table 1) and one per task. Each task has one fixed instruction, which
tells the multi-task policy which task to perform. Following the official protocol, we run 50 closed-
loop episodes per task on each of three evaluation seeds and report the success rate (SR) averaged

RoboDojo-Precision. Although PointWAM is designed for dexterous hands, its point-trajectory
representation is not tied to one embodiment. We therefore test whether it transfers to parallel-

on these tasks alone. The tasks are hard; on the benchmark leaderboard, policies trained on all of its
tasks rarely complete them (Chen et al., 2026b). We therefore also report its partial-progress score,
which separates methods when success is rare. Appendices B.2 and C.3 detail the setup.
Real robot. To evaluate PointWAM’s manipulation performance in the real world, we use an Ope-
nArm humanoid with two 7-DoF arms, two 6-DoF Inspire RH56F1 hands, and a ZED 2i stereo
camera, as shown in Fig. 5a. We evaluate two tasks: banana pick-and-place (PnP) and square-peg
insertion. Banana PnP evaluates general manipulation ability using a single arm, while square-peg
insertion evaluates coordinated dexterous manipulation with both hands. We collect 54 demonstra-
tions for banana PnP and 64 for square-peg insertion, and evaluate each method over 24 trials per
task. For banana PnP, half of the trials use the banana seen in the demonstrations (in-domain), and
the other half use an unseen box whose color resembles the table (out-of-domain). For square-peg
insertion, we also report a progress score, the mean number of completed stages per trial out of
three, lifting each of the two parts and inserting the peg.

4.2
COMPARISON WITH PREVIOUS APPROACHES
DexJoCo. With one policy across the ten tasks, PointWAM achieves the highest success rate on eight
tasks and reaches 69.0% average SR (Table 1), outperforming the strongest baseline, GR00T N1.6,
by a large margin of 11.7 percentage points, and π0.5 by 19.0. Both pre-train on large-scale robot
demonstrations and web data, whereas PointWAM uses no robot demonstrations beyond DexJoCo.
PointWAM also ranks first with one policy per task, and its margin over the strongest baseline widens
from 5.3 to 22.2 percentage points under full randomization of camera pose, lighting, and texture
(Tables 5 and 6 in Appendix D). Figure 3 shows the scene and hand trajectories PointWAM predicts,
and Fig. 4 shows bimanual hanoi rollouts from one initial state, where PointWAM succeeds and
GR00T N1.6 times out; Appendix E shows more rollouts.
RoboDojo-Precision. With no robot data beyond the benchmark’s own demonstrations, PointWAM
reaches an average score of 8.8 and 4.8% SR (Table 2). Fast-WAM and PointACT also pre-train
on no robot data, only on videos, image-text pairs, or 3D scenes, and stay near zero (0.3, 0.2%
and 1.6, 0.0%), even though PointACT observes depth. GR00T N1.6, whose pre-training includes
large-scale robot data with grippers, reaches 6.2 and 2.3%, still below PointWAM, and leads on
three of the eight tasks. PointWAM, pre-trained on human hands alone, thus shows initial transfer
to grippers, and depth alone does not account for its lead over Fast-WAM and PointACT.
Real robot. Figure 5b summarizes the real-robot results. PointWAM outperforms both GR00T N1.6
and π0.5 over all trials of each task. On banana PnP, it performs comparably with the in-domain ba-
nana but reaches 58.3% SR with the out-of-domain unseen box, against 16.7% and 25.0%. Since the
box resembles the table in color, the RGB-based baselines likely struggle to find it, while PointWAM
can rely on its 3D shape. On square-peg insertion, PointWAM reaches 50.0% SR against 16.7% and
25.0%. All three methods usually lift both parts, but the baselines often fail at the insertion, which
needs precise bimanual placement; PointWAM inserts in 12 of 21 such trials, against 4 of 19 and 6
of 18. Figure 12 in Appendix E shows example rollouts.

COMPONENT ANALYSIS
We change one setting at a time from a common reference, a Volt-L12 backbone, named for halv-
ing the depth of Volt-L to 12 layers, pre-trained on EgoDex and fine-tuned for 60k iterations (Ta-
ble 3). Since the ablations require many training runs, the reference is smaller and uses fewer human
demonstration videos than the benchmark model, so its SR is lower than in Table 1. Appendix C
gives the training settings, and Appendix D.2 further varies the patch size and encoder fine-tuning.
Vision encoder (a). The 3D encoder Mosaic3D, which computes features on the point cloud,
reaches 64.4%. Lifting DINOv3 (Sim´eoni et al., 2026) image features onto the points, as in Point-
World (Huang et al., 2026), lowers SR to 59.0%. Lifted features carry 3D positions but are com-
puted from images. The gap suggests that encoding the 3D structure of the scene in the features
themselves, not only in their positions, matters for dexterous manipulation.
World modeling (b).
Removing scene-trajectory supervision from both pre-training and fine-
tuning, with the depth input, 3D backbone, and hand head unchanged, lowers SR from 64.4% to
53.5%. Forecasting the scene thus improves success beyond observing it in 3D, and Fig. 3 shows
the predicted scene moving with the hands. The 2D frame row uses the DINOv3 encoder of (a) and
forecasts the DINOv3 latents of the frame at t+T through learnable queries that read the backbone’s
output tokens, as in FLARE (Zheng et al., 2025) but with DINOv3 rather than SigLIP-2 latents, so
that its input and its target share one feature space. It reaches 60.1%, 6.6 percentage points above the
model without world modeling but 4.3 below the reference. Forecasting the 3D trajectories of scene
points from 3D features thus outperforms forecasting future image latents from image features.
Hand representation and voxel size (c–d). In (c), anatomical keypoints reach 64.4%, whereas
depth-sampled hand surface points reach 43.6% with 10 points per hand and 44.6% with 512. Key-
points likely help because they correspond between human and robot hands (Section 3.1). In (d),
1 cm voxels reach 64.4%, above both finer 0.5 cm voxels (59.9%) and coarser 2 cm voxels (52.2%).
Coarser voxels may blur the contact geometry, while finer ones add little detail beyond the 224×224
depth input. We therefore use 1 cm voxels in all experiments, including Table 1.
5
CONCLUSION
We have introduced PointWAM, a 3D world action model that decomposes the world into a scene
and hands. It jointly forecasts both as 3D point trajectories in a shared space-time coordinate frame
and retargets the forecast hand motion to robot actions. Since human videos provide the same
trajectories, PointWAM pre-trains on them without selecting task-specific points, raising average
DexJoCo success from 12.1% to 69.0% and surpassing the strongest baseline by 11.7 percentage
points. Observing the scene in 3D per se is not enough; forecasting the scene adds 10.9 percent-
age points over forecasting the hands alone. These results suggest that a shared 3D point-trajectory
representation lets robots learn dexterous manipulation largely from human videos. Since Point-
WAM sees the world only through points, it can miss details finer than the point spacing, such as
printed text, and surfaces that depth sensing cannot capture, such as transparent objects. Future work
could extend what each point carries, from finer appearance to physical properties such as mass and
friction, which govern how objects move under contact.

A
EXTENDED RELATED WORK
In this section, we extend Section 2 to further world action models, 3D representations for robot
policies, and other ways of learning from human demonstration videos.
Future targets of world action models.
Beyond the dense 3D scene motion forecast by EgoWAM
and PointWorld, world action models differ in what future they forecast and what they condition it
on. Several forecast geometric futures, such as object poses in DyWA (Lyu et al., 2025) and depth
in GAM (Han et al., 2026). 3D-VLA (Zhen et al., 2024) forecasts goal RGBD images or point
clouds, and WSA1 (Jiang et al., 2026) forecasts latent 3D scene states. Others keep an image future.
Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) and Cosmos 3 (NVIDIA et al., 2026) build on video generation
backbones, 4D-WAM (Yang et al., 2026) aligns its features with 3D trajectory fields during training,
and FLARE (Zheng et al., 2025) aligns future tokens of its policy with image features of a future
frame. X-WAM (Guo et al., 2026) forecasts multi-view RGB-D video alongside actions. In each of
these, the forecast future takes a different form from the motion that produces the action, whereas
PointWAM expresses the scene and the hands with the same 3D point trajectories and retargets only
the hands to actions. Dexterous World Model (Kim et al., 2026b) conditions its interaction video
on given hand trajectories, much as PointWorld conditions on future robot trajectories, so it also
requires a supplied hand trajectory rather than forecasting one itself for robot control.
3D representations for robot policies.
PointWAM observes the scene as a point cloud, which
gives a policy direct access to 3D geometry. DexPoint (Qin et al., 2022a) and DexArt (Bao et al.,
2023) combine observed geometry with kinematics-derived point clouds for dexterous reinforce-
ment learning, and DP3 (Ze et al., 2024) learns imitation policies from encoded point clouds. Even
the image-based SpatialVLA (Qu et al., 2025) augments its features with 3D positions from esti-
mated depth. In all of these, geometry serves only as input, and the control objective alone trains
the backbone. CordViP (Fu et al., 2025) adds contact and hand–arm correspondence objectives.
PointACT (Chen et al., 2026a) keeps a 2D vision–language backbone and admits pre-trained 3D
point features only in its action expert, through multi-scale point–action attention, so geometry con-
ditions the action but never the backbone’s representation. PointWAM also builds on pre-trained 3D
features but supervises the backbone with future hand and scene trajectories, which human demon-
stration videos supply without recorded robot actions.
Other ways of learning from human demonstration videos.
Beyond the point-based policies in
Section 2, methods differ in what they transfer from the video to the robot. Retargeting the human
hand pose (Qin et al., 2022b) transfers actions directly but sets no scene-forecasting objective, and
neither do the hand and object poses of FlowHOI (Zeng et al., 2026). DexWM (Goswami et al.,
2026) learns a latent world model from egocentric human videos with hand keypoints as actions,
so it forecasts the effect of given hand motion rather than the motion itself, and HarmoHOI (Dang
et al., 2026) co-generates hand–object interaction videos and 3D point tracks for synthesis rather
than robot control. Track-based methods (Wen et al., 2024; Bharadhwaj et al., 2024) forecast the
future image positions of arbitrary points, but the tracks and the features behind them stay in image
space. General Flow (Yuan et al., 2024) forecasts 3D flow only at query points sampled on the
manipulated object, MolmoMotion (Zhang et al., 2026) forecasts 3D trajectories of query points on
an object of interest, and µ0 (Lee et al., 2026) forecasts 3D traces of interaction keypoints selected
with a vision-language model. In all three, something outside the model decides which scene points
it supervises. PointWAM instead encodes the full point cloud and supervises the trajectory of every
observed point and of the hand, learned from human demonstration videos without robot actions.

metric scale from the hands, which ARKit annotates in EgoDex and VITRA reconstructs. For each
chunk of frames, we project the hand joints into the images, discard depth ratios zGT/zpred more than
a factor of two from their median, and take the median of the remaining ratios as the scale.
Scene tracks. We resize frames to 640×360, place queries on every second pixel of each clip’s first
frame, and track them with CoTracker3 (Karaev et al., 2025) in offline mode. Backward tracking
is unnecessary, since every query starts at the first frame. Each track reads the scaled depth at its
pixels and is lifted to the world frame with the camera intrinsics and poses. We keep points that are
visible with valid depth in the first frame, deduplicate them on a 1.5 cm grid, and subsample at most
12,000. Since queries restart in every clip, point correspondence holds only within a clip.
B.2
ROBOT-DEMONSTRATION PREPROCESSING
Point cloud and scene trajectories. In DexJoCo, robot fine-tuning and evaluation use 224 × 224
depth images, which we unproject with the camera calibration, reduce to one point per 1 cm voxel,
and crop to a box around the robot base, 1 m to each side and from 0.2 m below to 1 m above it. The
box adapts the bounds filter of PointWorld (Huang et al., 2026), a cube about the scene center, to
exclude the floor and walls. Since the DexJoCo dataset records object poses only at reset, we replay
each demonstration to recover them over time. For each successful replay, simulator segmentation
removes robot points from the initial point cloud and assigns every remaining point, background
included, to an object or rigid part, whose rigid motion defines the point’s trajectory.
Robot hand keypoints. Each robot hand has ten keypoints, six on the palm and four on the fin-
gertips. The fingertip keypoints lie on the contact pads, since points on the distal joint axes would
miss the motion of four finger joints during flexion. Separate kinematic chains compute the right
and left hands, which record their joints in different orders, and each hand’s measured pose places
its keypoints in the scene frame without mirroring one hand onto the other. Bimanual inputs list the
right hand before the left and give each hand its own ID.
RoboDojo-Precision. Its two ARX X5 arms carry parallel-jaw grippers, so we keep the pre-trained
interface and change only what the embodiment requires. Following Point Policy (Haldar & Pinto,
2025), which places robot points by a rigid transform about the gripper pose, each arm again has
ten keypoints, the six palm offsets placed on the gripper frame from forward kinematics and the
two fingertips, with the last point repeated to fill the ten slots. The point cloud fuses the depth of
the head and two wrist cameras and drops points within 5 cm of the arm and finger segments. The

C.2
DEXJOCO EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Inference timing. DexJoCo’s evaluator runs asynchronously. It requests a new chunk once fewer
than 24 of the 30 actions remain and holds the robot’s pose whenever the buffer empties, so inference
latency affects the executed behavior. On one B200 GPU, the Volt-L12 reference of Table 3 takes
55 ms per forward pass, 37 ms of it in the frozen encoder. Each simulator control step spans 20 ms,
so a 30-step chunk covers 0.6 s. Human clips carry the same 31 frames over 1 s (Appendix B.1), so
one predicted step spans about 33 ms in pre-training and 20 ms in fine-tuning.
Checkpoint selection. For the Volt-H multi-task model, we consider the 20k, 40k, and 60k fine-
tuning snapshots and select on the first seed’s ten-task average, which selects 60k; all component
ablations use 60k. Single-task policies are trained with the view augmentation of Appendix D for
20k iterations (30k for bimanual hanoi and microwave) and evaluated at that final snapshot.
C.3
ROBODOJO-PRECISION EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We use the official RoboDojo evaluator (Chen et al., 2026b). Unlike the DexJoCo evaluator, it
runs synchronously. The simulator waits for each query, executes the whole action chunk, and then
queries again, so inference latency does not affect the outcome. The evaluation seed selects a fixed
set of initial layouts, and we run 50 episodes per task on seed 0, taking the layouts in order. An
episode ends at success or at a task-specific step limit, from 300 steps for insert key and deposit coin
to 1,900 steps for fasten screws, that is, 12 to 76 s at the benchmark’s 25 Hz.
We report the success rate and the benchmark’s partial-progress score, each averaged over the eight
tasks with equal weight. PointWAM is fine-tuned once from the 60k pre-training checkpoint, and
we evaluate its 60k fine-tuning snapshot without further selection. The published benchmark instead
trains three seeds per policy and evaluates each for 50 trials; we use one training seed and one
evaluation seed for every method in Table 2. We leave out the public leaderboard entries, since they
are trained on all RoboDojo tasks rather than the Precision demonstrations alone.
C.4
REAL-WORLD EXPERIMENTS DETAILS
Banana PnP. This task evaluates basic single-arm pick-and-place and, more importantly, the ability
to rely on geometry when color cues are ambiguous. We collect 54 demonstrations in which the robot
picks up a banana from various positions on the table and places it in a basket at a fixed location.
We run 24 evaluation trials per method with positions sampled from those in the demonstrations, 12
with the banana and 12 with a box unseen in the demonstrations whose color closely matches the
tabletop. A trial succeeds if the object lies entirely inside the basket at the end of the episode.
Square-peg insertion. This task evaluates precise grasping and tight bimanual coordination: the
robot must lift a square peg and a blue hole, align them, and insert the peg into the hole. Because the
peg is slippery, an imprecise grasp allows it to tilt between the fingers, which causes the insertion
to fail. We collect 64 demonstrations with the peg and hole placed at various positions, and run 24
evaluation trials per method with positions sampled from those in the demonstrations. Since one
end of the hole is capped, a trial is successful only if the peg remains in the hole at the end of the
episode. To capture partial progress, we additionally score each trial on a scale from 0 to 3:
• 0: fails to lift the peg.
• 1: lifts the peg but fails to lift the hole.
• 2: lifts both objects but fails to insert the peg.
• 3: inserts the peg, which remains in the hole until the end of the episode (success).

BASELINES
C.5.1
GR00T N1.6
Following the official recipe,1 we fine-tune from the released GR00T N1.6-3B checkpoint (GEAR
Team, 2025). We use the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, an action horizon of
30, and input images at 224 × 224, with all remaining hyperparameters left at their defaults. With a
global batch size of 256, we train a separate model per task for 30K steps (single-arm) or 60K steps
(bimanual) in the DexJoCo single-task setting, and a single model across tasks for 60K steps in each
of the DexJoCo multi-task and RoboDojo settings. For evaluation, we follow the default protocols
of DexJoCo and RoboDojo-Precision. For the EgoDex (Hoque et al., 2026) pre-training experiment
in Fig. 6, we follow H-RDT (Bi et al., 2026) and convert the ARKit hand annotations of EgoDex
into a 44-D bimanual state per frame, consisting of the 3D position and quaternion orientation of
each wrist, which serves as the end-effector pose, and the 3D positions of the five fingertips of each
hand. Since human demonstrations carry no commanded actions, we define the action at step t as
the next state, so that the model directly regresses future hand states. We pre-train for 30K steps
with a global batch size of 192, the same budget as for the other baselines.
C.5.2
FAST-WAM
We use the official codebase2 with the pre-trained Wan2.2-5B model. We train for 100K steps with a
cosine learning-rate schedule initialized at 1×10−4, an action horizon of 32, and a global batch size
of 64. For DexJoCo, we concatenate three 224 × 224 camera views, while for RoboDojo-Precision,
we concatenate three 192 × 256 views. For evaluation, we follow the default protocols of DexJoCo
and RoboDojo-Precision. In Fig. 6, we pretrain Fast-WAM on EgoDex with the same hand-state
actions and budget as for GR00T N1.6, with single-view 224 × 224 videos as visual supervision.
C.5.3
POINTACT
Following the official implementation3, we initialize the VLM backbone from Qwen2.5-VL-3B-
Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) and keep it frozen, initialize the point encoder from the pretrained Con-
certo (Zhang et al., 2025) PTv3-Large (Wu et al., 2024) checkpoint, and train the remaining modules
from scratch. We use the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5 × 10−5 and a cosine learning
rate schedule, with random rotations of the point cloud about the gravity axis and image augmenta-
tions as in the official implementation. With a global batch size of 256, we train a separate model
per task for 30K steps in the DexJoCo single-task setting, and a single model across tasks for 60K
steps in each of the DexJoCo multi-task and RoboDojo settings. Depth maps for both benchmarks
are obtained in the same manner as for PointWAM. In Fig. 6, we pretrain PointACT on EgoDex with
the same hand-state actions and budget as for GR00T N1.6.
