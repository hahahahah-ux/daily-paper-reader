## ABSTRACT 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models aim to perform diverse manipulation tasks, but task success in existing rigid-body benchmarks does not indicate whether they preserve objects. We introduce M ANI PHYSICS ZOO , which consolidates literature-supported material properties, 3D meshes, and supporting references into reusable object assets. Using these assets, a solver-based assessment com-putes grasp-specific damage thresholds from object geometry, material properties, and recorded grasp conditions and compares them with recorded contact forces to assess potential deformation and fracture. Building on these components, M ANI -PHYSICS BENCH evaluates object preservation in LIBERO and SimplerEnv across three physics axes and three difficulty levels. Public VLA checkpoints show a substantial gap between task success and safe success, defined as task comple-tion while preserving the object. Their gripper commands concentrate near full opening and closure, with largely similar aggregate distributions across objects, consistent with binary gripper supervision. We examine how object-specific con-tinuous gripper labels change model behavior by retraining a VLA model. The retrained model shows more object-dependent gripping and higher safe success, but lower task success and limited generalization of object-preserving behavior. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models aim to perform a wide range of manipulation tasks from visual observations and language instructions. Their recent performance and generality across tasks and embodiments have made them a major paradigm in robot learning (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025b; Bjorck et al., 2025). This capability has been evaluated through manipulation tasks in simulation and in the real world, which serve as a standard protocol for VLA evaluation (Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b). However, task completion alone does not establish whether a policy handles objects without dam-aging them. In real-world deployment, VLAs may need to grasp and move objects with diverse physical properties, such as fragile eggs and rigid wooden boxes. Humans take these properties into account by holding fragile objects firmly enough to prevent slipping but gently enough to avoid damage, while using a stronger grip when handling rigid objects. In this sense, physical properties determine which actions are appropriate, and a genuinely generalist policy should be able to com-plete a task while preserving the object. We therefore ask: Can current VLAs account for objects’ physical properties to complete manipulation tasks without causing damage? 

The direct way to evaluate this ability is to conduct real-world experiments. However, fractured or permanently deformed objects cannot be reused under the same conditions, so repeated trials incur object replacement costs. Moreover, damaged objects and fragments may pose safety risks to the robot and its surroundings. Therefore, a simulation-based framework is needed to assess object preservation under repeatable conditions. Yet widely used simulation benchmarks do not account for object damage when evaluating task success (Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b). Because objects are modeled as rigid bodies, a policy can receive the same success score regardless of whether it handles the object carefully or uses gripping that would damage it in the real world. Adjacent lines of work, including physical understanding benchmarks, robot safety benchmarks, and tactile and force-aware VLAs, leave this gap open, as none of them judges a policy’s actions against a physically grounded criterion for object preservation (Chow et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Fan

These limitations highlight the need for a common evaluation protocol that assesses whether policies complete tasks while respecting each object’s physical constraints. Developing such an evaluation protocol requires defining preservation criteria for each object and reliably assessing how policy-applied forces affect its state during a rollout. This entails two chal-lenges. First, defining physically grounded preservation criteria requires object-specific material properties, geometric meshes, and supporting references, but these resources are not readily avail-able in a unified, verifiable form. Second, evaluating object preservation requires translating the forces applied during a rollout into predictions of deformation or fracture, but the rigid-body simu-lators used in standard manipulation benchmarks do not model these outcomes. To address the first challenge, we propose M ANI PHYSICS ZOO , an object library that consolidates object-specific material properties, 3D meshes, supporting references, structural information, and validation status into reusable assets. These assets can be reused for evaluating new policies, robots, and objects across simulation environments. The supporting references justify the material proper-ties and preservation criteria, making their physical basis traceable and verifiable. To address the second challenge, we introduce an assessment method that uses physics solvers to determine whether contact forces recorded during a manipulation rollout could cause object defor-mation or fracture under the corresponding grasp conditions. After each rollout is completed, the physics solver combines the recorded grasp information with the material properties and 3D mesh from M ANI PHYSICS ZOO to compute a damage threshold for each grasp. We compare the recorded contact forces with the corresponding thresholds and judge an interaction to be potentially damaging if its force exceeds the threshold. We justify each threshold using material properties and damage criteria supported by the literature, and document the modeling assumptions to make the resulting evaluation traceable and verifiable. Building on these two components, we construct M ANI PHYSICS BENCH , a physics-based bench-mark for evaluating object preservation during manipulation. MANI PHYSICS BENCH constructs manipulation tasks in LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) and SimplerEnv (Li et al., 2024b) using objects selected from our M ANI PHYSICS ZOO .Solver-based assessment enables object-preservation evaluation on these platforms, even though their simulators model objects as rigid bodies. The benchmark is organized into three physics axes: brittle fracture, soft object deformation, and thin-walled container deformation. Each axis includes high, medium, and low difficulty levels, with higher difficulty corresponding to a narrower range of gripping forces that allow the object to be lifted without damage. We evaluate public VLA checkpoints (Kim et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024a; Black et al., 2025a; Fang et al., 2026) on M ANI PHYSICS BENCH and observe a substantial gap between task success and safe success, defined as task completion while preserving the object. To investigate this gap, we analyze the policies’ gripper commands to examine whether they adapt their gripping behavior to objects’ physical properties. We find that these commands concentrate near fully open and fully closed states, with largely similar aggregate distributions across objects. This pattern motivates examining the role of gripper supervision. Standard VLA pipelines often binarize gripper labels into open and close, which may limit the learning of object-dependent gripper control (Walke et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). We therefore replace binary labels with continuous labels derived from object-specific physical constraints and evaluate how retraining with these labels affects gripper behavior and safe success. Retraining yields more object-dependent gripping and higher safe success. However, task success decreases, and the resulting object-preserving behavior shows limited generalization. Our contributions are summarized as follows. • MANI PHYSICS ZOO . ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Manipulation Benchmarks. Manipulation benchmarks evaluate policies across diverse tasks, ob-jects, and environments. LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) studies knowledge transfer across manipulation tasks, while SimplerEnv (Li et al., 2024b) provides simulation-based evaluation of real world robot policies. CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022) evaluates language-conditioned task sequences, and RL-Bench (James et al., 2020) provides a diverse task suite with demonstrations. Because these bench-marks model objects as rigid bodies and do not track whether an object is damaged, task success alone does not indicate object preservation.

Physical Understanding and Interaction. 

Physical understanding benchmarks evalu-ate VLMs’ knowledge of physical properties and interactions through question answering. PhysBench (Chow et al., 2025) assesses un-derstanding of object properties and physical dynamics, while ManipBench (Zhao et al., 2025) evaluates low-level manipulation rea-soning through multiple-choice questions on interaction keypoints and action trajectories. We share their interest in connecting physi-cal knowledge to manipulation. However, in these benchmarks’ question-answering evalu-ations, models answer questions without exe-cuting manipulation actions, so object preser-vation during task execution is not directly as-sessed. Even PhysBench’s force test relies on VLM-specified gripping forces, while its simulator treats all objects as rigid bodies and does not simulate grasp-induced damage. Our work complements these evaluations by assessing whether contact forces recorded during policy rollouts exceed physically grounded damage thresholds for each grasp. Deformable manipulation environments aim to simulate objects that change shape. SoftGym (Lin et al., 2021) provides tasks with cloth, rope, and fluids, while PlasticineLab (Huang et al., 2021) focuses on shaping elastoplastic materials. However, deformation is the goal of these tasks, so object preservation is neglected and the damage criterion is absent. DefGraspSim (Huang et al., 2022) computes the stress and deformation of an object under a grasp with the finite element method (FEM). However, its material parameters are assigned from a fixed set rather than sourced from the object’s own material, so the computed response is hypothetical. Moreover, in DefGraspSim a gripper is placed at candidate poses produced by a sampler and closes on the object, without an arm and a manipulation task. Therefore, grasping as part of manipulation falls outside its concern. 

Robot Safety Benchmarks. Robot safety benchmarks assess hazards and harmful interactions dur-ing policy execution. SafeVLA-Bench (Fan et al., 2026) scores rollouts on existing benchmarks against safety constraints specified for the robot, the scene, and how the object is carried. OopsieV-erse (Balaji et al., 2026) accumulates mechanical, thermal, and fluid damage as a health value that the authors define for each object. Both share our premise that task completion should be separate from safe completion when evaluating VLAs. However, SafeVLA-Bench centers on the safety of the scenario and does not evaluate object preservation. OopsieVerse evaluates object damage, in-cluding preservation, but from damage criteria that are annotated manually rather than derived from the object’s properties. The authors describe this model as an approximation adopted in place of accurate physical computation, and it judges preservation regardless of where the object is grasped. The physical rigor of these verdicts therefore cannot be ensured. 

Force and Tactile-Aware Manipulation. Force and tactile-aware methods such as Tactile-VLA, TaF-VLA, and DeliGrasp adapt contact behavior to object properties and feedback during execu-tion, and demonstrate this capability through real robot experiments (Xie et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; 2026). These methods share our view that appropriate manipulation requires adapting actions to objects’ physical properties and contact conditions. However, their focus is on improving models, and evaluation is conducted on dedicated task protocols and real robot setups, where object preser-3Preprint. vation is judged by visual inspection after each trial. Because each evaluation runs on real hardware, it is subject to cost and safety constraints, and a common criterion under which existing models could be compared remains outside their scope. 

Summary. As summarized in Table 1, M ANI PHYSICS BENCH brings together reusable object as-sets, grasp-specific damage assessment using physics solvers, and evaluation of public VLA check-points on existing manipulation platforms. Using the object geometry and material properties docu-mented in M ANI PHYSICS ZOO , physics solvers compute damage thresholds for the grasp conditions recorded during each rollout. We compare these thresholds with recorded contact forces to assess whether task completion also satisfies the object’s preservation criteria. Each judgment therefore depends on both the object’s physical properties and how the policy interacts with it. This post-rollout assessment supports a common evaluation protocol while retaining the platforms’ existing observation and action interfaces and rigid-body simulators. 

## 3 PRELIMINARIES 

We evaluate whether a manipulation policy completes a task while preserving the manipulated object throughout a simulated rollout. A task instance consists of an object o, a scene, and a language instruction on a given manipulation platform. At each time step t, a policy π receives an observation 

xt and produces an action at. Executing these actions in the platform’s rigid-body simulator yields a rollout τ of length T . We represent the grasps extracted from τ as G(τ ) = {gk}Kk=1 with gk =(pk, d k, f k), where G(τ ) denotes the collection of extracted grasps, gk denotes the k-th grasp, and 

K is the number of grasps, which varies across rollouts. The platforms we evaluate use parallel-jaw grippers, so we take a grasp to be an interval in which both jaws remain in contact with the object for at least 0.2 s. The contact position pk is the pair of points where the two jaws touch the object surface, expressed in the object’s coordinate frame, and the contact direction dk is the unit vector between them, along which the jaws compress the object. The contact force fk is the force transmitted through this pair of contacts under the position servo control we adopt, and we take it as the largest value sustained over the interval. We use position servo control for the gripper, which matches the opening and closure commands pro-duced by current VLAs and follows the host platforms’ settings (Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b). This controller drives the gripper toward a target opening and generates contact force when an ob-ject resists further closure, so policies modulate gripping force through their closing targets. For mechanical assessment, we assume that the grippers are fitted with standardized silicone pads (Kar-gar & Berselli, 2025; Achilli et al., 2022) and account for the reduction in position-servo force as the pads and object deform. App. C gives the rationale for this choice and the controller settings, App. C.3 the pad model, and App. D.5 how the servo coupling enters the damage threshold. 

## 4 PHYSICALLY GROUNDED PRESERVATION ASSESSMENT 

Our framework combines M ANI PHYSICS ZOO with solver-based assessment to evaluate object preservation during manipulation. We first describe the construction of the object library, then ex-plain how solver-based assessment uses its assets and the grasp information recorded during policy rollouts to assess potential object damage. Fig. 1 presents the overall framework. The upper left and upper right panels correspond to M ANI PHYSICS ZOO and to solver-based assessment, and the bottom panel shows M ANI PHYSICS BENCH in Sec. 5, which applies both. 4.1 MANI PHYSICS ZOO 

We introduce M ANI PHYSICS ZOO to provide unified and verifiable object assets for preservation assessment. Because the required meshes and material data are scattered across different sources, we collect and verify these resources for each object. We select objects from household, industrial and laboratory environments according to three criteria. First, the object’s size, mass, and graspable region must be compatible with common manipulators, since a task that no robot can perform can-not be evaluated. Second, gripping must be able to cause irreversible damage that compromises the object’s intended use. The material properties and structural information needed to assess this dam-age must be supported by the literature or product standards, providing a traceable physical basis for the damage criterion. This criterion excludes objects such as towels, whose deformation during ordinary gripping does not compromise their intended use. Third, the object must be recognizable in evaluation images, and its appearance must provide cues to the physical properties relevant to gripping. For example, recognizing an egg allows a policy to draw on prior knowledge of its fragile shell, while the visible thin walls of a container suggest that it may deform under gripping forces. 4Preprint. We curate the library to cover three types of mechanical response to gripping: brittle fracture, soft object deformation, and thin-walled container deformation. For each object, we first search public 3D mesh collections for a suitable geometry (Deitke et al., 2023). Since these meshes are designed primarily for visual representation, they may lack geometric details required for mechanical mod-eling, such as accurate dimensions or wall thickness. We therefore generate a new mesh when no suitable one is available, add missing structural features such as the wall thickness of hollow objects, and scale the geometry using published product specifications for manufactured objects or reported measurements for natural ones. We visually inspect the resulting meshes for shape, dimensions, and appearance, and retain their physical dimensions, structural information, and mass derived from volume and material density for subsequent mechanical modeling. With the geometry of each object fixed, we ground the material parameters in reported measure-ments, material datasheets, or calibration to published mechanical tests rather than choosing them arbitrarily. The objects range from produce to packaging and containers, so their materials include plant tissue, paperboard, plastics, and metals, and their properties are reported in different fields. Therefore we survey established journals in the corresponding field together with material datasheets and product standards for each object. However, these sources do not report the same kind of quan-tity. For example, a study of eggshell reports a tensile strength (Liu et al., 2024), a material constant that we can use directly, whereas a study of strawberry reports the force at which the fruit failed under compression at a given loading speed (An et al., 2020), an outcome of one test setup with its own contact geometry. We therefore record with each value the part of the object tested, its state, and the conditions of the test, and we determine which physical quantity the value represents. When a source reports a test outcome rather than a material constant, we reproduce that test with the physics solver and adjust the remaining material parameters until the reported outcome is recovered. We then document each parameter as directly reported, calibrated from a reported test, or transferred from a representative material. Where a separate failure test is available, we also check the resulting model against it without refitting; for example, the egg shell model predicts the measured breaking loads of half-eggshell tests within 8–21% (App. A.3). 5Preprint. We represent each object asset as o = ( n, M, P, R), where n is the object name and M contains the object’s mesh, physical dimensions, mass, and structural information. P contains the material properties and their values together with the measurement context and validation status. R contains the supporting references and links. This representation makes each asset traceable and supports reuse across policies, robots, and simulation environments. App. A.2 details the fields of each asset and illustrates them with an object card. 4.2 SOLVER -B ASED ASSESSMENT 

Because the rigid-body simulators used for policy rollout do not model deformation or fracture, we assess potential object damage after each rollout by combining the recorded grasp information with the object assets in M ANI PHYSICS ZOO . During the rollout, we record contact positions, directions, and forces from the simulator. We then extract the grasps G(τ ), retaining the contact position pk,contact direction dk, and contact force fk of each grasp as defined in Sec. 3. Each grasp is thereby associated with the corresponding region of the object’s mesh. For each grasp, we construct a mechanical model using the object’s geometry M and material prop-erties P from M ANI PHYSICS ZOO . The model incorporates the recorded contact configuration, the boundary conditions describing how the gripper compresses the object, and the standardized silicone pads described in Sec. 3. We then increase the applied load under the recorded grasp configuration and identify the smallest force at which the object-specific damage criterion is reached. This de-fines the damage threshold Fdmg (gk) = Solver( M, P, p k, d k), where the gripper geometry, pad properties, and boundary-condition convention are fixed across grasps. The threshold is converted for comparison with the recorded contact force, accounting for the reduction in position-servo load as the pads and object deform. We select a suitable solver and damage criterion according to the object’s dominant mechanical response (Belytschko et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2000) (App. D.1). We compare the recorded contact force fk with the corresponding damage threshold and flag a grasp as potentially damaging with damaged( gk) = 1[fk > F dmg (gk)] . This post-rollout assessment supplements the rigid-body simulation with a prediction of potential damage under the specified mechanical model. App. D details the modeling assumptions, servo coupling, and solver settings. App. A.3 discusses how the criteria account for model uncertainty.

The gap between the two captures task completions in which the object was not confirmed to be preserved. PR is the fraction of completed tasks in which the object was preserved, and is undefined when no episode succeeds; in that case, we report “–”. It isolates how carefully a policy handles the object from how often it completes the task, which allows comparison between policies with different success rates. We report these metrics for each physics axis and difficulty level to examine how policies respond to different physical constraints.

Object preservation depends on both the policy and the controller settings (e.g., the position gain kp; App. C.1). We therefore also examine the gripper commands di-rectly, without converting them into contact forces, to as-sess whether the policies adapt their gripping to objects’ physical properties. Figure 2 shows the distributions of gripper commands averaged across episodes and models for each platform. The commands concentrate at the two extremes, with largely similar distributions across the axis A objects despite their different damage thresholds. Al-though individual models exhibit some variation, concen-tration near the two extremes remains a common pattern across models and axes. Model-wise distributions across three axes are provided in Figs. 9 and 10, with details of aggregation and contact-conditioned analysis in App. F.1. This pattern closely matches the binary structure of grip-per supervision used in training. In standard VLA pipelines, the gripper channel is often binarized into open and close (Walke et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). Such la-bels supervise when to open or close the gripper, but do not provide different closing targets for objects with different physical constraints. When the clos-ing target is fixed, adapting contact forces to the object must rely on other aspects of the interaction, such as grasp location and orientation. Binary gripper supervision thus leaves the degree of closure without explicit object-dependent supervision. App. C.4 reports results under other kp values. 6.3 OBJECT -S PECIFIC GRIPPER SUPERVISION 

To investigate how relaxing binary gripper supervision affects gripping behavior, we introduce con-tinuous gripper labels that account for objects’ physical properties. We use GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 8Preprint. 2026) to identify objects that may be damaged by gripping in Bridge demonstrations (Walke et al., 2023), the training data used in SimplerEnv (Li et al., 2024b). However, the increase in safe success may simply result from weaker gripping after retraining. We therefore also evaluate both models on a low-friction ice cube task, where insufficient gripping force lets the object slip (App. H). They reach similar task success and both retain near-full closure during contact (Table 5). However, across the benchmark objects, the higher preservation rate among successful episodes is accompanied by lower task com-pletion, highlighting the need to balance object preservation with reliable manipulation. Moreover, object-specific supervision does not resolve all preservation failures. In axis C, the paper and portion cups still have no safe successes despite successful task completions. The Bridge annotations used for relabeling provide generic cup supervision, without explicitly distinguishing paper cups and plastic cups. These cases suggest limited generalization beyond the distinctions explicitly supplied in the supervision. Together with the lower task success observed above, these results show that object-specific gripper supervision alone does not ensure reliable object-preserving manipulation. Improving preservation across material and structural differences while maintaining task success remains an open challenge. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We present M ANI PHYSICS ZOO and M ANI PHYSICS BENCH , combining reusable object assets with solver-based assessment to evaluate whether manipulation policies complete tasks while preserving objects. Our evaluation of public VLA reveals a substantial gap between task success and safe success. We find that gripper commands concentrate fully open and fully closed states, with largely similar aggregate distributions across objects. Retraining with object-specific continuous gripper labels yields more object-dependent gripping and higher safe success, but also lower task success and limited generalization beyond the distinctions provided during training. These findings highlight the challenge of learning manipulation policies that preserve objects while completing tasks. 

Limitations and Future Work. Our assessment uses literature-supported material properties and damage criteria, but its damage predictions still require direct validation through physical robot experiments. It evaluates bilateral gripping after each rollout and does not capture damage from drops or impacts, or how deformation affects subsequent contacts and policy actions. Extending this evaluation to physical experiments and manipulation with deformation feedback is an important di-9Preprint. rection for future work. The documented object assets and assessment pipeline provide a foundation for these extensions, while M ANI PHYSICS BENCH offers a common setting for measuring progress toward reliable object-preserving manipulation. 10 Preprint. Evidence levels and parameter provenance. We distinguish three evidence levels for the spec-ified response and material region. Level 1 provides quantitative support through reproduction of, or comparison with, a published test response or its associated FE result. Level 2 provides support through two complementary routes: reported constitutive properties combined with object geom-etry for material-dominated responses, and material or constitutive-model transfer from analogous objects or tissues that share the relevant deformation or failure mechanisms. These routes provide a physical basis for FEM assessment without requiring a separate mechanical test for every object geometry. Level 3 identifies records with unresolved gaps in parameter provenance or test correspon-dence. We separately document whether each input is reported, transferred, or calibrated against a mechanical test. Where calibration is used, its target response and fitted parameters are recorded ex-plicitly. Quantitative comparisons distinguish calibration responses from additional test conditions, while numerical convergence and agreement with literature responses are assessed separately. 

Interpreting the benchmark evidence paths. Table 7 links geometry and constitutive inputs to the evidence supporting their use. Apart from the low-difficulty, high-strength controls in axes A and B (walnut and plum) and the material-dominated containers in axis C, all benchmark objects have Level 1 quantitative support for the specified mechanical responses. These are eggshell breaking loads at three loading positions, tomato compression reaction at an observed rupture displacement, potato compression stress at observed fracture strains, and reproduction of the strawberry bioyield calibration target. The tomato comparison uses a separate cultivar without refitting the material parameters, and the potato comparisons use additional years without refitting to their stresses. These records support the stated responses. The remaining benchmark assets in Table 7 are supported through complementary evidence paths. Plum skin has Level 1 support from bulge-test calibration and comparisons across culti-vars (Br¨ uggenwirth et al., 2014), while its core has Level 2 support from constitutive-model transfer from loquat flesh (Liu et al., 2025). For walnut, GHARIBZAHEDI et al. (2011) report shell-rupture onset loads as low as 40.17 N across their tested orientations and moisture conditions at 50 mm/min. These loads exceed the nominal quasi-static grip forces expected for our walnut geometry under the inherited position controllers (App. C.2). Because the legacy walnut shell parameters lack a traceable source, we also checked that the control verdicts do not depend on them by comparing the recorded forces directly with these reported loads. The largest per-jaw force recorded on the walnut in any successful episode was 16.0 N, 0.40 times the lowest reported rupture-onset load and 0.43 times the lowest directly computed walnut threshold of 37.7 N. The walnut therefore remains undamaged in the benchmark episodes even if its threshold were as low as the weakest reported shell. This comparison supports its role as a high-strength control; the provenance of the legacy shell parameters remains separately identified in the table. Together with the assessed load ranges, these evidence paths motivate walnut and plum as low-difficulty controls whose damage thresholds are difficult to reach under the evaluated settings. Axis C containers are modeled as thin-walled structures with one effective material law for each shell: paperboard, PP, or HIPS. Their response is governed by the material’s elastic and plastic be-havior together with wall thickness and geometry, providing a direct route from literature properties to structural FEM assessment. We use an isotropic reduction of the reported directional paperboard tensile branch and representative PP and HIPS constitutive laws (Alzweighi et al., 2023; Kasprzak, 2024; Fu et al., 2022). Numerical material checks support the implementation of these laws, and the object-specific FEM models compute bending, buckling, and plastic deformation with the corre-sponding geometry and thickness. This material–geometry route provides Level 2 physical support for container-preservation assessment. Below, we detail the evidence for all four High/Medium objects in axes A and B. 

Egg: axis A, High. We adopt E = 30 GPa, ν = 0 .3, and tensile strength σt = 19 .9 MPa from Liu et al. (2024). The source obtains the elastic modulus and tensile strength from naturally curved tensile specimens, combining their breaking loads with FE stress analysis, and adopts Poisson’s ratio from prior literature. These values are therefore independent of the source’s compression tests used below. Our assessment targets loss of shell integrity when maximum tensile principal stress reaches σt. Preserving an intact shell is the intended-use requirement for handling an egg for sale or presentation. Under the U.S. standards for shell eggs, only unbroken shells qualify for grades AA, A, and B, and an egg with a broken or cracked shell, including a very fine hairline crack, is classified as a check even when its membranes are intact and its contents do not leak; such eggs are 22 Preprint. to be diverted to immediate use or further processing (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2000). The criterion therefore counts crack initiation without requiring catastrophic breakage. The median benchmark damage reaction for the egg is 5.05 N per jaw (App. G), far below the external breaking load of 49 N reported by the source. We examined whether these two values are consistent and, more generally, whether the shell model can predict a failure load without being fitted to it. To this end, we reproduced the half-eggshell compression tests of Liu et al. (2024) at the sharp end, the blunt end, and the equator. A linear-elastic CalculiX shell model used the source egg profile and the benchmark shell properties, with the load applied through a uniform pressure patch of radius 0.2–1.0 mm and the cut edge resting on a roller support. No parameter was adjusted to these tests. Table 8 compares the load at which the outer surface reaches σt away from the load point, where the source locates the crack that causes breakage, with the measured breaking forces. The model gives 57.6, 55.8, and 37.8 N, within +8 to +21% of the measurements and within about 3% of the source FE predictions, and it reproduces the lower strength at the equator. These loads change by at most 6% across patch radii. At 49 N on the sharp end, the outer peak is 16.9 MPa, compared with 17.3 MPa in the source FE. Shell properties and strength obtained from separate tensile tests thus reproduce the failure load of the shell structure. This supports using them to evaluate cracking of the shell in the benchmark grasps, beyond matching a stress field at an imposed load. The same model also accounts for the low benchmark threshold. The inner surface beneath the load reaches σt much earlier, at 1.5–6.3 N depending on the patch radius, which corresponds to the source’s first crack that arrests before breakage. To confirm that the benchmark threshold refers to this event, we examined 25 randomly sampled benchmark grasp analyses. In all of them, the criterion was first reached on the inner surface beneath the pad, while the outer surface remained at 14–22% of σt. The median threshold of 5.05 N therefore lies within the crack-initiation range and does not contradict the breaking loads in Table 8. The benchmark combines the shell properties and this crack-initiation criterion with its recorded grasps and the deformable-pad contact model in App. C.3.

Boiled potato: axis B, High. Sadowska et al. (2005) report elastic moduli and compression-fracture stresses and strains for cooked Irga potato tissue. We used a homogeneous, free-lateral uniaxial CalculiX coupon to identify an effective elastoplastic response, rather than duplicating the cylinder–platen contact problem. For the 2000 data, the reported modulus of 620.82 kPa was mapped to an effective modulus of 493.83 kPa. With 

ν = 0 .4, the effective yield stress was fitted to 56.75 kPa so that the computed nominal stress at the reported fracture strain of 0.188 matched 68.3 kPa. This supplies an effective plasticity parameter that the reported modulus alone does not determine. We then fixed the modulus conversion factor (0.79545) and the yield-to-effective-modulus ratio (0.11492), and supplied the measured modulus and fracture strain for each of the 2001 and 2002 datasets without refitting to their stresses. At the reported strains of 0.172 and 0.220, the pre-dicted nominal stresses were 54.37 and 54.10 kPa, compared with 50.2 and 59.1 kPa in the literature (+8 .30% and −8.45% ). It supports the effective compression-response model across the reported conditions while keeping calibration and additional comparisons distinct. For benchmark assessment, the potato is intended to remain intact for serving, presentation, or sale, rather than to be mashed. We therefore assess preservation using an application-level tolerance: tissue with accumulated equivalent plastic strain PEEQ ≥ 0.002 must occupy less than 1% of the object’s initial volume. Reaching this fraction marks unacceptable modeled permanent tissue defor-mation. The 1% criterion quantifies the volume fraction of tissue affected by modeled irreversible deformation. We use this affected fraction as a proxy for potential degradation of tissue integrity and product quality when handling food intended to remain intact. The literature comparisons support the constitutive response used to calculate this tissue-level measure of preservation.

Damage verdicts under model uncertainty. Solver-based assessment inherits the limitations of its models. Material properties vary from one specimen to another, the models idealize contact and internal structure, and most of our evidence concerns material responses rather than the damage load of a whole object under a pad grasp. We are aware that a verdict can therefore differ from what a physical test of the same grasp would show, and we take this into account when setting the criteria. How we do so depends on whether the model has been checked against a measured failure. For the egg, the shell model predicts measured breaking loads that were not used to set its parameters (Table 8). Because a measured failure response has been reproduced, we apply the onset criterion as it is, and preservation fails when a crack initiates in the shell. For the cherry tomato, the soft foods, and the containers, the evidence reaches material or structural responses but not a measured failure load. For these objects, the most protective choice would be to declare damage at the earliest modeled event, such as first yield or the first microcrack. Without validation at the level of failure, however, the load at which such an event occurs carries modeling error. We therefore count damage only after limited irreversible deformation beyond the earliest event. Strawberries and boiled potatoes are counted as damaged only when tissue with PEEQ ≥ 0.002 occupies at least 1% of the initial volume, a tolerance we adopt for foods intended to remain intact. Thin-walled containers are counted as damaged when the maximum shell PEEQ reaches 0.002, and the cherry-tomato tissue-strength criteria do not count earlier cuticular microcracks. These allowances serve as a margin for modeling error. A small overestimate of stress or plastic strain does not by itself produce a damage verdict, and a grasp is judged damaged only after it has exceeded the modeled onset by this margin. Under the same material model and loading history, these criteria therefore favor preservation success over the most protective choice. Because the solver reports the affected volume fraction continuously, a different tolerance can be applied without new analyses. We also examined how much the verdicts depend on the threshold values themselves. Each verdict compares fk with Fdmg (gk), so scaling every threshold by a common factor is equivalent, below actuator saturation, to scaling the recorded forces by its inverse. The gain analysis in App. C.4 there-fore also covers a twofold error in the thresholds. For the High objects, the median ratio of recorded force to threshold among damaged episodes ranges from 2.0 to 6.0. The success–preservation gap reported in Sec. 6.1 persists under these criteria. 25 Preprint.

## C GRIPPER CONTROL 

At deployment, gripper control interfaces can accept pulse-width modulation (PWM), current, target-force or position commands (ROBOTIS, n.d.; Franka Robotics, n.d.; Trossen Robotics, n.d.). A generalist VLA must accommodate different gripping requirements across objects, using a gentle grip to avoid damaging fragile objects and a stronger grip when needed to hold heavier, rigid ob-jects securely. In general, no single fixed force or current setting can be assumed to satisfy these requirements across the open-ended range of objects a generalist robot may handle, so the policy itself must modulate its grip. Current VLAs do so through gripper opening or closure commands rather than PWM signals or target forces; we therefore use position servo control to preserve this action interface. The rest of this section describes the position-servo relation and pad–object contact model. In the host simulator, the position servo acts together with joint dynamics, damping, friction, actuator limits, and the contact model to determine the force transmitted to the object. We record the resulting contact forces during rollout and use them for damage assessment. We do not assume that these simulated contact forces are identical to those produced by a particular physical gripper. The gripping forces applied during data collection are also not reported for the datasets on which the evaluated policies are trained. In the public data-collection code released with BridgeData V2 (Walke et al., 2023), for example, the gripper action is converted into a jaw position target, and the gripper motor is driven by a bounded PWM command proportional to the position error. Under the current action interface, a policy thus specifies a gripper opening or closure rather than a force, and any regulation of the gripping force passes through a position-based controller. For generalist policies that are developed and evaluated in simulation before deployment, we therefore consider it a reasonable choice to adopt the simulator’s command-to-force response as the common reference. Simulated and physical responses can be brought into closer agreement through cali-bration, either by identifying simulation parameters from hardware measurements or by tuning a physical controller toward the simulated response within its operating limits. If a policy preserves objects under our evaluation conditions, such alignment can help carry this behavior over to physical deployment. Controller gains and actuation limits are configurable choices. We inherit the host platforms’ set-tings to preserve their existing control interfaces and provide consistent conditions for policy com-parison (Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b). The verdicts we report depend on this setting, since a different gain or force limit changes the contact force that a given closing command produces, and therefore changes which grasps exceed their thresholds. App. C.4 examines this dependence by halving and doubling kp. As expected, Safe SR is higher at the lower gain and lower at the higher gain, but it remains well below SR at both gains. The gripper commands are unchanged in this anal-ysis, because the policies output closure commands rather than forces, and they concentrate near full closure across objects with different physical properties (Sec. 6.2). Our main findings therefore remain consistent within this range. We also recognize that policies may behave differently under a substantially larger or smaller gain. What the setting does not change is the criterion against which the force is compared. The un-derlying FEM damage load is computed from the object’s geometry and material properties at the recorded contact position and direction, so it is a property of the object and the grasp rather than of the controller; App. D.5 gives the conversion from this load to the comparison threshold. The MANI PHYSICS ZOO assets and the solver-based assessment can therefore be reused under other controller settings. For a different gain or force limit, the difficulty levels can be recomputed from the same assets (App. G), and object preservation can be assessed with the corresponding contact loads and grasp geometry. The same applies to pipelines in which a policy outputs target forces, since the assessment takes the recorded contact loads and grasp geometry as its inputs. We adopt the position-servo setting because it matches the action interface of current VLAs, and the gap we report is a finding under this setting. Because both the controller setting and the damage criteria determine the verdicts, comparing object preservation across policies requires evaluating them under the same setting and criteria. This shared setting can serve as a basis for a common criterion in evaluating object preservation across policies. C.1 CONTROLLER SETTINGS

## D FEM I MPLEMENTATION AND SOLVER CONFIGURATION 

D.1 OBJECT MECHANICS AND PHYSICS SOLVERS 

Object preservation depends on material properties and geometry, which determine how an object responds to contact. Young’s modulus describes elastic stiffness, while yield behavior and frac-ture strength govern irreversible deformation and fracture (Bower, 2025). For brittle shells such as eggshells, fracture depends on material strength as well as shell thickness and curvature. Elastic and plastic properties describe the compression of fruit flesh and the deformation that remains after release. Thin-walled containers can undergo permanent indentation or buckling depending on their material properties and wall geometry. These responses also depend on where and in which direc-tion the object is grasped. A physics solver is software that numerically solves physical equations to predict such responses. The finite element method (FEM) is a widely used numerical method for analyzing deformation and fracture in solids. It divides a solid into small elements and com-putes deformation, stress and strain from material properties under specified contact and boundary conditions (Bower, 2025). Accordingly, we use CalculiX with implicit structural FEM for brittle ob-jects, where damage occurs when stress reaches the fracture strength (Bower, 2025; Dhondt, 2025); FEBio with nonlinear constitutive models for soft objects, where damage corresponds to irreversible deformation beyond an application-level volume tolerance (Holzapfel, 2000; Maas et al., 2012); and OpenRadioss with explicit dynamics for thin-walled containers, where damage corresponds to per-manent indentation or buckling (Sun et al., 2000; OpenRadioss Contributors, 2022). The numerical settings of each solver are given in App. D.4. D.2 GEOMETRY AND MESHING 

Using the pad geometry and placement defined in App. C.3, we discretize the pad–object contact problem with local refinement around the recorded contact regions. The standard target edge lengths are 2 mm near contact and 4 mm away from contact; the 2 oz paper cup uses a 3 mm object refine-ment target. Thin structures use shell elements, volumetric objects use quadratic tetrahedra, and skin–core models share boundary nodes between the two components. D.3 CONTACT AND LOADING CONDITIONS 

For each recorded grasp, we solve a coupled contact problem comprising the object and the two deformable pads. Prescribed motion of the pad backings progressively compresses the object. As the pads and object deform, the contact solver determines the evolving contact region and traction distribution, accounting for load redistribution rather than prescribing a fixed pressure footprint. Weak centering springs regularize initially free rigid-body modes; they do not represent the robot’s position-servo gain. The solver-specific contact algorithms and loading schedules are summarized below. D.4 NUMERICAL SOLVERS 

CalculiX (Dhondt, 2025) provides the implicit shell and solid contact route; FEBio (Maas et al., 2012) provides the nonlinear soft-tissue route; and OpenRadioss (OpenRadioss Contributors, 2022) provides the explicit thin-walled-container route. Table 12 summarizes their discretization and nu-merical settings. In the OpenRadioss conversion, each quadratic triangular shell is subdivided into four DKT18 triangles, with five integration points through the thickness. Shell stresses and plastic strains are read across the thickness rather than only at the midsurface. The implicit static route adapts its increments as contact and material nonlinearities evolve. For dynamic routes, a gradual displacement ramp reduces transient inertial effects; slower ramps are used when the decision interval fails the prescribed numerical checks. The FEBio ramp scale and its evaluated fraction jointly determine the loaded duration, while the OpenRadioss displacement factor changes the imposed closure amplitude. These parameters must therefore be distinguished from an allowable plastic-strain or damaged-volume fraction. A crossing between saved states retains a threshold interval, and later force reduction does not undo an earlier damage event. When a FEM analysis fails to provide a usable damage threshold due to nonconvergence, we esti-mate the threshold using the median of the directly computed thresholds from the five nearest grasps of the same object. ## G DIFFICULTY LEVELS 

Each object can be lifted without damage only within a range of gripping forces. We characterize this range using the minimum lifting force Flift , the pad–object damage reaction Rdmg (g), and a nominal reference ceiling Fceil (g) = min {kpw(g)/2, F ref }. Here w(g) is the contact-point distance for grasp g, kp = 1000 N/m, and Fref = 20 N. The lifting force follows the reference asset’s mass, friction, and center-of-mass offset. This index uses the mechanical reaction at damage, before the servo-equivalent threshold conversion in App. D.5; its reference ceiling is not a measured maximum contact force.
