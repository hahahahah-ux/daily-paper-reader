# PatternDex: Learning Interaction Patterns to Guide Reinforcement Learning of Bimanual Dexterous Manipulation of Articulated Objects 

David Minkwan Kim, Runfa Blark Li, Beckham Po-Ju Lee, Nikolay Atanasov, Truong Nguyen University of California, San Diego 

Fig. 1: Overview of PatternDex. Given a human demonstration, PatternDex learns the interaction pattern that describes the relationship between the object trajectory and the wrist motions in the object frame. Since this pattern is dictated by the object rather than the hands, it is agnostic to hand embodiments. With this pattern as guidance, PatternDex trains a policy that manipulates articulated objects with the target robot hand in both simulation and the real world. The target robot is a bimanual setup of Allegro hands mounted on xArm6 arms. 

Abstract — In this paper, we develop a method that enables bimanual dexterous hands to manipulate articulated objects with a high success rate without suffering from an embodiment gap. We observe that the correlation between hand motions and object motions is dictated by the object rather than the hands and can be learned from human-object demonstrations. Based on this observation, we propose PatternDex, a method that learns this correlation and represents it as a token sequence, which we call an interaction pattern. From this pattern, Pattern-Dex estimates the wrist motions and contact points that fit the target robot, and then trains a reinforcement learning policy that exploits these estimates as guidance. Since the guidance fits the target embodiment, the policy explores only the actions that the target robot can execute and thus achieves high success rates. PatternDex also requires only simple fine-tuning to train a new robot, since it can reuse the learned interaction pattern. We evaluate PatternDex with bimanual dexterous hands on human demonstrations from the ARCTIC dataset. PatternDex achieves, on average, a 92.8% success rate with Allegro hands, while the state-of-the-art baseline achieves 52.2%. Also, it achieves success rates above 70% with three other robot hands after fine-tuning alone. Furthermore, we verify that the learned policy transfers well to a real-world task of opening a microwave. Videos and additional results are available at 

https://patterndex.github.io/PatternDex/ .

I. INTRODUCTION Dexterous manipulation of articulated objects, such as opening a microwave door or a laptop lid, is a challenging problem because it requires coordinating and synchronizing high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) hands. Successful approaches for these tasks adopt imitation learning, which trains areinforcement learning policy to reproduce the motions ob-served in human demonstrations [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. A straightforward method is to train a policy with robot execution data collected through teleoperation, where a human operator controls the robot hands directly [1], [2]. However, collecting teleoperation data is expensive and time-consuming. Another approach is to use kinematic retarget-ing that maps the human finger joints to the robot finger joints [3], [4], [5], [6], but this method becomes unreliable when the embodiment gap between the human and the robot hands is large. A more recent approach [7] observes that the human wrist motion is less sensitive to the embodiment gap than the finger motion, so it imitates only the human wrist motion and learns the finger actions with reinforcement learning. However, this method addresses the embodiment gap with respect to the fingers but not with respect to the hand size and structure. When the robot wrist is placed at the human wrist pose, a larger robot hand exceeds the manipulation workspace, and a smaller hand fails to reach the object. Thus, imitating the human wrist alone is still insufficient to eliminate the embodiment gap. We observe that in articulated manipulation tasks, the correlation between the hand and object motions is dictated by the desired object trajectory, rather than the hands. The key idea of our work is to learn this relationship and to represent it as a sequence of tokens in a latent space. We refer to such a sequence as an interaction pattern .Since the interaction pattern depends on the object rather than the hands, it is agnostic to the hand embodiment and can be mapped not only to human hands but also to various robot hands. We develop a model that learns arXiv:2610.04765v1 [cs.RO] 3 Oct 2026 the interaction Pattern from human-object demonstrations and decodes it into training guidance for a reinforcement learning (RL) policy to achieve Dex terous manipulation of articulated objects. We refer to our method as PatternDex .By encoding an interaction pattern and combining it with a robot hand description, PatternDex estimates wrist motions and contact points that fit the target robot hand and uses them as guidance to drive the policy’s actions. Fig. 1 gives an overview of PatternDex, where the interaction pattern is learned from human demonstrations and reproduced with bimanual dexterous robot hands in simulation and the real world. Our contributions are summarized as follows.  

> •

We learn a latent representation of bimanual object manipulation in the form of a token sequence, which we call an interaction pattern. This representation cap-tures the hand-object correlation dictated by the desired object trajectory and is agnostic to specific hand em-bodiments.  

> •

We design a guidance predictor that combines an inter-action pattern with a robot hand description to predict the wrist motions and contact points for the desired manipulation task. Since the interaction pattern transfers across robot hands, the predictor is trained once and needs only simple fine-tuning for a new robot hand.  

> •

We train an RL policy with the wrist motions and contact points from the predictor as guidance. Since the guidance fits the target robot hand, the policy learns actions within the robot’s capabilities rather than imitating human motions that are not feasible for the robot. As a result, our guided policy achieves higher success rates than policies trained directly from human hand motions. II. RELATED WORK 

Dexterous Manipulation with Imitation Learning. Re-inforcement learning has solved dexterous manipulation tasks by designing a reward that specifies what to achieve in each task [8], [9], [10] or by using a pretrained controller that moves the fingers so that the policy only gives high-level commands [11]. However, such methods are hard to design manually for long-horizon tasks such as manipu-lating an articulated object. Many works therefore learn a policy by imitating human hand-object demonstrations, which show how the human hand moves and where it touches the object [4], [5], [3], [6], [7]. ManipTrans [4] and DexMachina [5] kinematically retarget the human hand motion by matching a fixed set of keypoints on the human hand with the corresponding keypoints on the robot hand. ReGRIND [3] and TopoRetarget [6] formulate retargeting as an interaction-mesh optimization that preserves the spatial relationship between the hand and the object. Since these methods map each human finger to a robot finger, the retargeted motion becomes infeasible when a robot hand such as the large four-fingered Allegro hand follows the motion of the smaller five-fingered human hand. ObjDex [7] avoids this finger mapping by using only the human wrist trajectory as a reference, but the robot wrist is still placed at the human wrist pose. Therefore, a robot hand of a different size either exceeds the manipulation workspace or fails to reach the object. In contrast, we solve this problem by estimating wrist motions that fit the robot embodiment instead of using the human wrists directly. We further add contact points estimated for the same embodiment, so the policy is guided on where the wrist should go and what the fingers should touch. 

Hand-Object Motion Correlation. A line of work in computer graphics generates human hand motion from ob-ject motion. OMOMO [12] takes object motion alone and predicts where the hands should be to achieve it, and then generates a body pose from the predicted hands. CAMS [13] uses the object shape and a sequence of object poses to predict the hand pose for each frame in a space defined with respect to the object and the contact. Both works show that a consistent correlation exists between object motion and the hand motion that achieves it, and that this correlation can be learned from human motion data. However, these methods generate the motion of human hands and have not been applied to a robot hand of a different embodiment. Accordingly, we learn this correlation as an embodiment-independent token sequence and apply it to robot hands by decoding the wrist motions and contact points for a given target hand. III. PROBLEM STATEMENT Given human hand-object demonstrations together with a description of the target robot hand, we address the problem of learning a control policy for bimanual dexterous manipu-lation of articulated objects. Each demonstration provides a sequence of object states and human hand states. The object state includes the pose, the articulation angle of the object, and the object shape (e.g., mesh or point cloud). The human hand states include the wrist poses and the shape of the human hands. This problem poses two challenges. The human and robot hands differ in size and structure, so direct transfer of the demonstrated hand states to the robot is infeasible. Also, since manipulation of articulated objects is contact-rich with a large action space, reinforcement learning with a reward alone is unreliable. Thus, we decompose the problem into two stages to address these challenges: the prediction stage (Sec. III-A) and the guided policy learning stage (Sec. III-B).

EVALUATION This section evaluates PatternDex on how accurately the guidance predictor G estimates the wrist motions ˆW and the contact points ˆC that fit the target robot hand, and how successfully the guided policy reproduces the demonstrated manipulation in both simulation and the real world. Pattern-Dex is trained on 229 human hand-object demonstrations from the ARCTIC dataset [18] and evaluated on 6 unseen demonstrations. All policies are trained in the Isaac Gym simulator [15]. A. Wrist Motion and Contact Point Prediction 

Table I reports the interaction pattern consistency, the wrist offset, and the contact precision for four structurally different robot hands. The pattern consistency is calculated as the Pearson correlation [21] between the frame-to-frame displacements of the human wrist and the predicted robot wrist along each of the x, y, z axes in the object frame. Shadow, which is structurally closest to the human hand, shows the highest consistency of 99.1%, and even Allegro, which differs the most, reaches 96.5%. All four hands thus exceed 96%. The wrist offset is the distance between the human wrist and the estimated robot wrist. Allegro and Paxini are placed about 5 cm behind the human wrist, while Shadow and XHand are placed about 2.8 cm behind. These offsets reflect the structure of each hand rather than its overall size. Shadow and XHand have both the palm and the fingers about 1–2 cm longer than the human hand, and their wrists are placed about 2.8 cm behind, which roughly matches this uniform increase in length. Allegro has fingers about 6 cm longer than the human fingers but a palm of similar length, while Paxini has a palm about 5 cm longer than the human palm but fingers only about 2 cm longer. Their wrists are placed about 5 cm behind, which matches the more elongated part of each hand rather than the sum of the two. The contact precision is the overlap between the estimated contact points and the human contact points, and all four hands reach 74–80%. These results show that the predic-tor G maintains the demonstrated interaction pattern while adapting the wrist motions and contact points to each robot embodiment. 

B. Guided Policy Evaluation 

We compare three types of guidance on six demonstrations with two Allegro hands mounted on xArm6 arms. Each episode starts with both hands about 22 cm away from the object. The retargeting methods [3], [4], [5], [6] instead reset the hand to a state on the retargeted reference within the workspace, so the wrist placement is fixed by the reset rather than by learning. We therefore compare with ObjDex [7], which starts from the same poses and guides the wrist with the human wrist motions. PatternDex guides the policy with the estimated wrist motions alone or with both the wrist motions and the contact points. Each episode follows a 500-step reference object trajectory and counts as a success if the object stays within 10 cm in position, 30 ◦ in rotation, and 10 ◦ in joint angle of the reference without collision between the hands and arms. Table II reports the success rate and the errors averaged over the steps before each episode ends. ObjDex achieves a mean success rate of 52.2% across the six demonstrations. With the estimated wrist motions alone, PatternDex reaches 58.1%, 5.9% higher than ObjDex. PatternDex guided by both the wrist motions and the contact points achieves the highest success rate of 92.8%. D. Sim-to-Real Experiment 

We deploy PatternDex on a real robot to verify that the policy trained in simulation transfers to the real world. Since PatternDex has already learned the interaction pattern from the ARCTIC dataset, it needs only a single human demonstration video to let the target robot imitate a new task without collecting robot demonstrations. Our example task is opening a real microwave with an Allegro hand mounted on an xArm6 arm. From the video demonstration of a human opening the microwave, CoTracker3 [23] tracks the position and the hinge angle of the microwave, and HaMeR [24] tracks the human wrist pose. The tracked trajectories are fed into the guidance predictor G, and the guided policy is then trained in the Isaac Gym simulator with the estimated wrist motions and contact points.
