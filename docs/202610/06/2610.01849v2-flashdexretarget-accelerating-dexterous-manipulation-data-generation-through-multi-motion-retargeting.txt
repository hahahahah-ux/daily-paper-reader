Abstract— Human hand-object demonstrations provide a
scalable source of data for dexterous robot learning, but trans-
ferring them across embodiments requires physically feasible
retargeting. Existing physics-based methods typically optimize
each demonstration independently, leading to either limited
success under finite simulation budgets or training costs that
grow with dataset size. We introduce FlashDexRetarget, an RL
framework for multi-reference dexterous retargeting. We for-
mulate retargeting as multi-reference tracking, jointly learning
a single policy across many demonstrations with off-policy RL
and geometric supervision of the demonstrated interactions.
This shared training formulation amortizes optimization across
references while enabling the policy to track diverse hand-object
interactions. On a 50-motion benchmark from TACO, OakInk2,
and HOT3D using XHand and Sharpa Wave Hand as target
embodiments, FlashDexRetarget retargets 90% of demonstra-
tions using about 30 GPU-hours, compared with about 46% at
about 3,000 GPU-hours for CHORD. This corresponds to about
100 times lower training compute and a 44-percentage-point
improvement in retargeting success. Ablations examine the key
design choices, while experiments with up to 1,000 motions
and real-world replay further demonstrate the scalability and
practical applicability of our method.
I. INTRODUCTION
Learning dexterous manipulation requires large amounts
of robot interaction data. Human hand-object demonstra-
tions [2–4] provide a promising source of such data, as
diverse manipulation behaviors can be collected at scale
∗Equal contribution. †Corresponding author.
without directly operating a robot. To use these demonstra-
tions for robot learning, however, human motions must be
translated into actions executable by a target robot hand.
Physics-based retargeting addresses this issue by adapting
human demonstrations to the morphology and actuation of
the robot in simulation, while leveraging physical contact
to reproduce the demonstrated object motion. To make
large human motion datasets practical sources of robot data,
retargeting must achieve high success at low compute cost.
Existing physics-based retargeting methods adopt a single-
reference formulation, optimizing each demonstration in-
dependently. Sampling-based methods [5, 6] search directly
over action sequences through simulated rollouts, but high-
dimensional actions and long contact sequences make this
search challenging under a finite simulation budget. RL-
based methods [7–9] instead learn closed-loop policies to
track the demonstrated hand-object interaction, but train
a separate policy for each reference. Despite their differ-
ent optimization strategies, both approaches retarget each
demonstration independently, causing computational cost to
grow linearly with the number of demonstrations.
To avoid this repeated optimization, we study multi-
reference tracking, where a single reference-conditioned
policy is trained jointly across many demonstrations. By
sharing learning across references, this formulation has the
potential to amortize training cost over an entire dataset
while retaining accurate tracking. Multi-reference tracking
arXiv:2610.01849v2  [cs.RO]  4 Oct 2026


has been effective for humanoid whole-body motion [10],
but dexterous manipulation introduces additional challenges.
First, object shapes and hand-object configurations vary
across demonstrations, requiring the policy to distinguish
interaction geometries. Second, reconstructed human demon-
strations, even when captured using motion-capture equip-
ment, contain contact errors, making exact contact matching
unreliable. Third, multi-reference training broadens the state
distribution, increasing the need for model capacity and
effective experience reuse. Finally, for bimanual motions,
the two hands assume distinct roles, such as primary and
supporting roles, requiring asymmetric learning signals.
To address these challenges, we introduce FlashDexRe-
target, an RL framework for multi-reference dexterous retar-
geting. First, we condition the policy on object geometry and
K future reference frames to distinguish diverse interactions
across references. Second, we replace exact contact matching
with distance-based interaction rewards to provide dense
hand-object supervision despite imperfect and sparse recon-
structed contact information. Third, we adopt the off-policy
learner FlashSAC [11] and increase both the replay buffer
size and critic capacity to support learning across a broader
multi-reference distribution. Finally, we use separate left- and
right-hand actor-critic networks to handle the distinct roles
and learning signals of the two hands.
We evaluate FlashDexRetarget on 50 hand-object motions
from TACO, OakInk2, and HOT3D [2–4] using XHand
and Sharpa Wave Hand as target embodiments. Against
sampling-based and RL-based baselines, we evaluate re-
targeting success, measured as the fraction of successfully
retargeted demonstrations, together with the total training
compute required. A single FlashDexRetarget policy retar-
gets 90% of the benchmark using about 30 GPU-hours,
compared with about 46% success at about 3,000 GPU-hours
for CHORD [7]. This corresponds to approximately 100×
lower training compute and a 44-percentage-point improve-
ment in retargeting success. To understand these gains, we
ablate how conditioning on K future reference frames, hand-
object interaction objectives, off-policy training and capacity
scaling, and separate left- and right-hand networks contribute
to performance in the multi-reference setting. We further
show that the number of successfully retargeted trajectories
continues to grow as the dataset scales. Finally, we validate
the retargeted trajectories through real-world replay.
Our main contributions are:
(i) We introduce FlashDexRetarget, an RL framework
that jointly retargets human hand-object demonstrations
through multi-reference tracking with a single policy.
(ii) We combine object point-cloud observations, hand-
object interaction objectives, off-policy learning with
larger replay and model capacities, and left- and right-
hand actor-critic networks for joint dexterous tracking.
(iii) We evaluate on 50 motions and two robot embodiments,
achieving approximately 100× lower training compute
and 44 percentage points higher success than CHORD,
with ablations and real-world replay.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Human Hand-Object Interaction Data
Human hand-object datasets cover diverse objects and
manipulation behaviors. DexYCB provides annotated hand-
object poses [12], while GRAB and OakInk capture grasping
configurations and interaction geometry [13, 14]. HOI4D and
HOT3D extend this coverage to egocentric interactions [4,
15], and TACO, OakInk2, and ARCTIC capture coordinated
bimanual manipulation [2, 3, 16]. GigaHands offers large-
scale recordings of activities such as pouring, assembling
objects, and playing cards [17]. These datasets provide
diverse manipulation experience for robot learning.
However, these recordings capture human hands, which
a robot cannot execute directly. Kinematic retargeting is a
common first step to close this embodiment gap. UniDex [18]
converts human hand-object trajectories into robot trajecto-
ries by solving fingertip inverse kinematics and manually
adjusting the hand base so that the retargeted fingers still
appear to touch the object. VideoManip [19] retargets hu-
man hand-object trajectories through keypoint matching and
repairs the resulting contacts with contact optimization. In
both, contact is imposed geometrically after the fact rather
than produced by the robot hand acting on the object, so it is
not guaranteed that executing the trajectory reproduces the
demonstrated object motion. Retargeting must therefore be
physics-based, producing trajectories with verified contacts.
B. Dexterous Retargeting from Human Demonstrations
Kinematic and planning-based pipelines adapt human
demonstrations to robot motions [20–24], while physics-
based optimization additionally accounts for hand-object
contact dynamics in simulation [25, 26]. Within this physics-
based setting, sampling-based methods search over simulated
control rollouts [27–29] to support dexterous planning [30]
and demonstration refinement [31]. For demonstration re-
targeting, SPIDER [6] introduces temporary virtual forces
between robot fingers and the object to guide annealed
sampling toward trajectories that reproduce the demonstrated
contacts. Building on this approach, Do as I Do [5] in-
corporates hand-object reconstruction and physics-based re-
finement to retarget video demonstrations. Although these
methods avoid training a tracking policy, they require a sep-
arate trajectory search for each reference, with effectiveness
dependent on initialization and reference quality.
Rather than searching trajectories directly, RL-based meth-
ods learn dexterous manipulation policies from human mo-
tion references and task objectives [7–9, 32–37]. Manip-
Trans [8] learns residual corrections to a pretrained hand-
motion imitator, while DexMachina [9] progressively reduces
object assistance during training. CHORD [7] instead uses
contact-wrench guidance to align human and robot object-
motion capabilities. Although these methods replace trajec-
tory search with policy learning, they still require separate
training or adaptation for each demonstration. In contrast,
FlashDexRetarget amortizes this cost by jointly learning
hand-object tracking across demonstrations.

D. Off-Policy Learning
On-policy algorithms such as PPO [1] discard transitions
after each policy update, which is data-inefficient, whereas
off-policy algorithms reuse past transitions stored in a replay
buffer. We therefore adopt FlashSAC [11], an off-policy
RL algorithm with high sample efficiency. Unlike single-
reference training, however, multi-reference training involves
diverse objects as well as hand motions, which yields a much
broader state distribution. To accommodate this, we enlarge
the replay buffer from the default 10M to 50M transitions
to retain experience across references, and increase the critic
hidden dimension from the default 256 to 1024 to better
estimate values over such diverse data.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate FlashDexRetarget in terms of retargeting
success, computational efficiency, scalability, and real-world
executability through four research questions:
RQ1: How does FlashDexRetarget compare with prior meth-
ods in retargeting success and computational efficiency?
RQ2: Which design components improve multi-reference
retargeting performance and learning efficiency?
RQ3: Does multi-reference retargeting remain effective as
the number of reference motions increases?
RQ4: Do retargeted trajectories transfer to real-world robot
execution?
A. Experimental Setup
a) Datasets and preprocessing: We use human hand-
object demonstrations from TACO [2], OakInk2 [3], and
HOT3D [4], following the preprocessing procedure of
CHORD [7]. Our collection includes single-object interac-
tions from HOT3D and two-object interactions from TACO
and OakInk2. To avoid trivial successes, we exclude inactive
clips in which neither hand performs manipulation and the
objects remain stationary, since such clips can satisfy the
tracking criteria without meaningful robot action.
b) Evaluation protocol: For the main comparison, we
select 50 reference motions comprising 25 single-object
and 25 two-object interactions. We evaluate retargeting to
two robot embodiments, XHand and Sharpa Wave Hand,
and compare FlashDexRetarget with Do as I Do [5], Dex-
Machina [9], and CHORD [7]. We use the released imple-
mentations of Do as I Do and CHORD, where CHORD is
trained with PPO [1], and modify DexMachina to initialize
from random states along the reference trajectory rather than
always from the motion start. For the scaling experiments,
we use reference collections of 200, 500, and 1,000 motions,
maintaining approximately balanced proportions of single-
and two-object interactions. All simulation experiments are
conducted in Isaac Sim on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs.
c) Metrics: We report three success rates: SRMT-Obj,
SRMT, and SRSPIDER. The ManipTrans metric SRMT [8] uses
object position and rotation error thresholds of 3cm and
30◦, together with fingertip and hand-joint position error
thresholds of 6cm and 8cm, respectively. Its object-only
variant, SRMT-Obj, retains the object-tracking criteria while
omitting the hand-tracking requirements. For comparison
with prior baselines, we also report SRSPIDER [6], which
applies object position and rotation error thresholds of 10cm
and 0.5rad to object errors averaged over the active hands
rather than requiring each hand’s object to satisfy them.
This averaging can overestimate success when one object
remains nearly stationary in the reference. We therefore use
SRMT-Obj for component-wise ablations, as it measures object
retargeting without cross-object averaging, while retaining
SRSPIDER for the main comparison with prior methods. To
evaluate the quality of successfully retargeted trajectories, we
additionally report the mean object position error (MOPE)
and mean object rotation error (MORE) among trajectories
that satisfy the SRSPIDER criterion.
d) Computational cost: We report computational cost
in total GPU-hours. For single-reference baselines, we peri-
odically evaluate each per-motion run, stop it once a success-
ful rollout is obtained and stored, and record the total GPU-
hours consumed up to that point. We sum these costs over
all attempted motions, including the full computational cost
of unsuccessful runs. For multi-reference training, we use
a fixed training-step budget for each experiment and report
the total GPU-hours required to reach that budget. Multi-
reference success is measured cumulatively over training: a
reference is counted as successful once a rollout satisfying
the SRMT-Obj criterion is stored. The training budgets used for
each experiment are specified in the corresponding sections.

C. Key Components for Multi-Reference Retargeting
We ablate the observation design, rewards, actor-critic
architecture, and training configuration of FlashDexRetarget.
All variants are trained for 300 million environment steps.

geting success measured by SRMT-Obj.

portance of K future reference conditioning, we remove the
future trajectory encoding while retaining the next-step ref-

the immediate target helps the policy anticipate subsequent
motion, improving hand pose and object rotation tracking.

cumulative retargeting success. These results support training
separate actor-critic pairs with hand-specific rewards to help
each hand perform its role in bimanual manipulation.
d) Off-policy learning and capacity scaling: We eval-
uate the effects of off-policy learning and capacity scaling
using PPO, default FlashSAC, FlashSAC with a larger re-
play buffer, and our full configuration with both a larger
replay buffer and critic. Default FlashSAC uses a replay-
buffer capacity of 10 million transitions and a critic hidden
dimension of 256. The larger-buffer variant increases the
capacity to 50 million, while our full configuration further
increases the critic hidden dimension to 1024. This ablation
shows the most pronounced performance gap among our
design choices. Under the same budget of 300 million envi-
ronment steps, PPO achieves near-zero cumulative success,
whereas default FlashSAC converts approximately 34% of
the reference collection. Increasing the replay-buffer capacity
raises this to approximately 60%, and further scaling the
critic reaches 86% with our full configuration. The large and
consistent gains indicate that both effective experience reuse
and sufficient critic capacity are particularly important for
learning over the broad state distribution induced by many
reference trajectories.

D. Scaling to Larger Reference Collections
To examine scalability beyond the 50-motion setting,
we train shared policies on collections of 200, 500, and
1,000 reference motions. We use a budget of 600 million
environment steps for all three settings, providing sufficient
interaction as the number of reference motions increases.
To compare their computational efficiency on a common
scale, Figure 6 shows the cumulative number of successful
conversions over the first 60 GPU-hours of training. Larger
reference collections consistently produce more successful
conversions within the same compute budget, with the gap
increasing over the course of training. This indicates that
exposing a shared policy to more reference motions allows


the computation spent on training to benefit a larger set
of demonstrations. Overall, these results demonstrate that
multi-reference training improves conversion throughput as
the reference collection grows.
E. Real-World Validation
To test whether simulation-retargeted trajectories remain
executable under real-world contact dynamics, we replay
successful trajectories on the physical robot. As shown in
Figure 5, the robot completes several manipulation tasks, in-
cluding wiping a board, pouring into a pan, and closing a lid.
These tasks involve different interaction requirements, from
sustained surface contact during wiping to controlled object
tilting during pouring and contact-guided closure. Successful
execution across these tasks demonstrates that the retargeted
trajectories preserve task-relevant hand-object interactions
and remain physically executable beyond simulation.
V. CONCLUSION
We introduced FlashDexRetarget, an RL framework that
efficiently converts human hand-object demonstrations into
dexterous robot data through multi-reference tracking. The
framework combines geometry- and interaction-aware obser-
vations, future reference conditioning, hand-object interac-
tion rewards, and dedicated hand-wise actor-critic networks
with off-policy learning and increased replay-buffer and
critic capacity. These components enable experience reuse
across demonstrations, achieving higher retargeting success
at substantially lower computational cost than the evaluated
baselines on XHand and Sharpa Wave Hand. Ablations
demonstrate the contributions of individual components,
while scaling experiments show higher conversion through-
put with larger reference collections under the same compute
budget. Together, these results support joint retargeting as an
efficient approach to generating reusable robot data at scale.
Nevertheless, our framework relies on relatively clean hu-
man hand-object trajectories, accurate object geometry, and
privileged simulator information, which may limit robustness
and real-world deployment. Future work will explore robust
reference processing from human videos, generalization to
unseen motions and embodiments, task-aware evaluation be-
yond pose-based metrics, and teacher-student policy distilla-
tion to enable deployment with only real-world observations.
