# VICON: Visual-Inertial-Contact based Hand-Object Tracking for Manipulation Datasets 

Yubin Jeon 1, Uiseong Shin 1, Hwanchul La 1, Jaeseong Kang 1, Hyelim Choi 2 and Yongseok Lee †

Abstract — Learning dexterous manipulation benefits from human demonstration datasets that capture diverse and natural hand-object interactions. In particular, contact points and forces provide supervision on where and how strongly to interact, which cannot be fully captured by motion trajectories alone. However, methods for jointly capturing hand and object motion, contact points, and forces remain limited. Moreover, severe occlusion from hand-object interaction challenges accurate tracking of both hands and objects. To address these limitations, we present a Visual-Inertial-CONtact based hand-object tracking (VICON) framework. It holistically captures both hand and object motion along with contact information during manipulation, even under severe occlusion. First, we adopt a visual-inertial glove [1] and an RGB-D camera for accurate hand tracking, and redesign the glove to incorporate contact sensing. Specifically, force-sensitive resistors (FSRs) are placed on the glove based on human grasp frequency to synchronously record contact states and calibrated normal forces. Second, without requiring pre-existing CAD models, we estimate object poses using RGB-D images and a mesh reconstructed from a monocular video. We propose factor-graph-based object trajectory estimation that fuses object-pose estimates weighted by visibility under hand-object occlusion, FSR measurements, and a hand-motion prior. Across 40 motion-capture sessions with five objects, VICON achieves a 2.5% failed-frame rate compared with 50.9–64.6% for the baselines, with median errors of 3.9 mm and 3.0 ◦ under occlusion. Using VICON, we construct a dataset containing synchronized hand-object motion, contact points, and normal forces, and will publicly release an expanded dataset covering 10 object categories at 

https://github.com/VICON-dataset/dataset .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Dexterous manipulation is a fundamental capability for humanoid robots operating in human environments, where everyday tasks require coordinated hand motion and continu-ous physical interaction with objects. Learning such intricate behaviors heavily relies on rich demonstrations. However, collecting demonstrations via teleoperation is challenging; differences in kinematics and workspace between human and robot hands make real-time control unintuitive, severely limit-ing data collection efficiency and degrading motion quality. In contrast, directly capturing human demonstrations bypasses these difficulties, naturally preserving human dexterity and task variability across diverse objects and environments [2], [3]. Yet, to fully realize this potential, hand-object interactions must be measured reliably. Rich demonstration data requires

more than hand-object motion alone, since successful dexter-ous manipulation fundamentally depends on where contact occurs and how much force is applied. Such information is particularly important for learning contact-rich manipulation policies [4] and emerging force-aware vision-language-action models [5]. Demonstration data should therefore capture contact points and forces alongside hand and object motion. However, many existing datasets provide detailed hand-object geometry and contact annotations without directly measuring the applied forces [6], [7]. Although recent tactile datasets and sensing systems provide richer contact information [8]– [10], simultaneously capturing hand-object poses along with contact locations and forces remains challenging within a compact setup. In particular, achieving accurate ground truth often requires multi-view camera systems, restricting data collection to dedicated environments. With a compact setup, occlusion presents another major challenge for reliable hand-object tracking. Under severe occlusion, even state-of-the-art vision-based pose estimators can become unreliable or fail entirely. Although existing works have attempted to mitigate occlusion in hand pose estimation [11] and hand-object tracking [12]–[14], these approaches still rely heavily on visual cues, which can become unreliable under severe or prolonged occlusion. Recent work has explored using hand motion as an additional constraint for object tracking [15], but maintaining robust tracking when visual evidence is largely unavailable remains challenging. Wearable hand tracking directly captures hand kinemat-ics, providing reliable motion estimates where vision-only arXiv:2610.05180v1 [cs.RO] 4 Oct 2026 estimation fails. In particular, visual-inertial skeletal tracking (VIST) [1] combines inertial measurements with visual marker observations to robustly track hand motion under severe occlusions. Because hand and object trajectories are tightly coupled during manipulation, this robust hand motion can serve as a prior to guide object pose estimation when visual object cues become unreliable. However, this kinematic constraint is physically valid only when the hand and object are in direct contact. Contact sensing therefore serves as a crucial gate to determine when the hand-motion prior should be applied. To address this, we integrate force-sensitive resistors (FSRs) directly onto the glove, providing occlusion-invariant contact detection while simultaneously yielding calibrated normal force measurements. Building on these components, we present VICON (Fig. 1), a multimodal tracking framework built on the VIST glove [1] integrated with eight FSRs, together with a single RGB-D camera. An offline factor graph fuses object-pose estimates from FoundationPose [16], weighted by object visibility, with a contact-gated hand-motion prior to refine object trajectories under occlusion. Using the refined trajectories, contact points are located on the object surface, which are paired with calibrated FSR forces. The main contributions of our work are: 1) Visual-inertial hand tracking system with contact sensing. 

We integrate eight FSRs into the VIST glove [1], posi-tioned according to human grasp contact frequency [17]. Together with a single RGB-D camera, the resulting 

VICON-glove synchronously records hand motion, contact states, and calibrated normal contact forces. 2) Robust hand-object tracking algorithm under severe occlusion. 

We fuse a contact-gated hand-motion prior with visibility-weighted object pose estimates to refine object trajectories and maintain robust tracking even under severe occlusion. 3) Scalable contact-rich dataset collection for custom objects. 

We present a pipeline that reconstructs object geometry from monocular video and captures synchronized hand motion, object pose, object-frame contact points, and calibrated normal forces using only a VICON-glove and a single RGB-D camera. We will publicly release an expanded dataset covering 10 object categories. II. R ELATED WORKS 

A. Vision-Based Hand and Object Pose Estimation 

Vision-based methods have been widely studied for recov-ering hand and object poses during manipulation from RGB or RGB-D observations. For object pose estimation, Mega-Pose [18] estimates the 6-DoF pose of novel objects using rendered CAD views for coarse pose selection and iterative refinement. A second line of work jointly estimates hand and object poses by aligning hand-object models with RGB-D observations using proximity-based contact and occlusion constraints [12]. HOLD [14] reconstructs both from monocular video using neural implicit surfaces and contact constraints without an object template. More recently, ComPose [15] learns from monocular RGB video to weight hand joints and combine hand- and object-derived motion cues for object tracking. Despite these advances, image-derived geometry and motion consistency may not reliably distinguish near-contact state from actual contact at an occluded surface, and none of these methods directly measures contact forces. 

B. Hand-Tracking Gloves and Contact Sensing 

Sensorized gloves have been developed to capture hand motion and tactile interactions during manipulation. VIST [1] fuses glove-mounted IMUs with visual markers under hand kinematic constraints, using inertial propagation and visual corrections to maintain hand tracking through occlusion. A markerless approach uses inertial pose priors to infer image keypoints and their uncertainty and combines the learned keypoint likelihoods with inertial observations in a factor graph [19]. FSGlove [20] uses IMUs placed on the glove, whose alignment is calibrated from reference poses and fits a personalized hand shape using predefined finger contact constraints. However, an external dorsal tracker is additionally used to recover the hand’s global pose. For contact sensing, STAG [8] records spatial patterns of normal force using 548 piezoresistive sensors distributed over the hand, and learns to identify objects and estimate their weights from these tactile signatures. However, neither kinematic tracking nor tactile sensing is sufficient on its own; kinematic tracking cannot measure applied force, while tactile sensing requires hand-object kinematics to locate contacts on the object surface. Despite this clear complementarity, prior works rarely integrate both modalities in a single wearable system. 

C. Hand-Object Interaction Datasets for Manipulation 

Table I compares existing datasets in terms of pose annotations, contact and force signals, and recording setups. EgoDex [2] pairs large-scale egocentric video with hand poses but has no object pose, contact, or force. ContactPose [6] maps thermal imprints after static grasps onto object meshes alongside hand and object poses. ARCTIC [7] derives contact from reconstructed hand-object geometry during bimanual manipulation of articulated objects, requiring a costly multi-camera motion-capture studio. Tactile datasets pair measured pressure with hand motion. OpenTouch [9] combines full-hand pressure, hand tracking, and egocentric video, but provides no object poses. More recently, TactiDex [10] combines dense tactile gloves with hand-object motion capture, using pressure-gated proximity and tactile-constrained optimization, but requires a motion-capture workspace. Force-aware robot datasets record external loads for contact-rich policy learning. ForceVLA [5] synchronizes images, proprioception, and estimated six-axis tool-center-point (TCP) wrenches in ForceVLA-Data across five contact-rich tasks and fuses them in a vision-language-action policy. However, these wrenches describe net end-effector loads without resolving individual human hand-object contacts. In contrast to these systems, VICON enables scalable collection of contact-rich demonstrations. Specifically, it is low-cost and wearable, since the VICON-glove builds on the VIST glove, whose material cost is about $100 and weight is 52–55 g [1], and adds only eight FSRs. Moreover, it requires only one RGB-D camera and no motion-capture studio, so recording is not restricted to a dedicated capture space. In addition, it provides accurate data even under severe occlusion, as the glove tracks the hand robustly and the FSRs measure contact directly (Sec. V-C). Finally, it pairs object-frame contact points with calibrated normal forces, a feature absent from prior datasets in Table I. As the volume of demonstration data becomes increasingly important for learning manipulation, this combination of low acquisition cost and high accuracy is a key advantage over costly studio-based systems such as ARCTIC [7]. III. H ARDWARE SYSTEM 

The hardware consists of the VICON-glove (Fig. 2) and one Intel RealSense D435i camera. The glove is built on VIST [1], which incorporates IMUs and visual markers, and is augmented with eight FSRs. The camera is positioned beside the subject at roughly eye level, providing a side view of the workspace; depth is computed from the infrared pair with Fast-FoundationStereo [21] at about 30 Hz.

B. FSR Placement 

We determine the FSR placement based on a contact-frequency analysis of human grasps(Fig. 2(c)) [17]. Their study classifies grasps into power, precision, and intermediate grasps and analyzes contact frequencies during mechanic and housemaid tasks. The results show that the fingertips exhibit the highest contact frequencies, while frequent contact is also observed at the thenar eminence and distal palm. Based on these observations, we place a sensor on each of the five fingertips, one on the thenar eminence, and two on the distal palm over the second- and fifth-metacarpal regions (Fig. 2(d)). For the ulnar-side distal-palm sensor, the analysis reports contact frequency as a single region spanning the third to fifth metacarpals, without distinguishing the areas beneath the ring and little fingers [17]. A. Recording Setup and Object Models 

Four subjects, each wearing a single glove, recorded 71 sessions with five rigid objects: a block, a mouse, a mug, a mustard bottle, and a phone (Fig. 4(b)), and each session consisted of approach, grasp, slow transport, and placement. Forty sessions (i.e., eight per object) were captured in a motion-capture room for ground truth (Fig. 4(a)); the other 31 were recorded without motion capture. The object meshes were obtained from monocular video via COLMAP [23], 3DGS [24], and SuGaR [25], with a ChArUco board setting the metric scale and unobserved surfaces cleaned away; all evaluated object pose trackers use the same meshes. Validating on YCB objects [26], the reconstructions deviate from the scans by only about 1 mm on average and yield closely matching FoundationPose trajectories, so we use the reconstructions throughout.

D. Component Ablations 

Table IV ablates the second (final) refinement on the same frames, with the hand poses and the contact states of Sec. IV-C fixed, so the variants differ only in how the refinement uses the visual and contact evidence. Each variant removes one component and retains the other two. 

Visibility weighting. The acceptance gates, hand mask, and outlier checks are removed and the observation deviations fixed to their vd = 0.5 values, so unreliable FoundationPose candidates enter the graph at that fixed weight. 

Hand-motion prior. We disable hand-motion initialization, propagation, the contact-gated prior, the grasp links ( A, G), and the synthetic poses, leaving occluded frames dependent on visual evidence that is itself degraded. The occluded-frame medians include stationary periods and therefore do not isolate the contribution during transport. 

Tactile contact timing. The contact gate is disabled, so the hand-motion prior is applied to every usable hand increment, including intervals in which the hand moves without the object. Removing any component increases the failed-frame rate from 2.5% to 18.1–28.9%. Paired comparisons of the per-session occluded median translation errors favor full VICON over each ablation (40 sessions; two-sided sign tests, Holm-corrected p < 0.0005). 

E. Dataset and Contact-Force Annotations 

The current dataset consists of all 71 sessions (including 40 motion-capture sessions): 81,307 RGB-D frames, meshes, hand skeleton trajectories and refined object trajectories, and the contact records of Sec. IV-E. The calibrated normal forces have a median of 10.9 N and a 90th percentile of 27.0 N; 83% of them fall between 0 and 20 N, the range for which the calibration error is reported in Sec. IV-E. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We present VICON, a visual-inertial-contact based hand-object tracking framework that is robust to occlusion and enables scalable collection of contact-rich demonstrations. Using a single RGB-D camera and the VICON-glove, our system synchronously captures hand-object motion, contact points, and contact forces. Furthermore, our offline refinement recovers robust object trajectories even under severe occlusion by weighting visual estimates by visibility and admitting hand motion constraints only under measured contact. Using our framework, the failed-frame rate falls from 50.9% (FoundationPose) to 2.5%. The current dataset covers single-hand sequences with five rigid objects. Future work will add subjects, object classes, and faster motions, while relaxing the fixed-grasp prior to support full in-hand manipulation, and evaluate these synchronized kinematic and tactile records in policy learning.
