TacOT: Learning Contact-Rich Dexterous Manipulation from
Human Demonstrations via Tactile-Guided Optimal Transport
Xingting Li1∗, Yifan Han2∗, Zijian Lin1∗, Wei Hou1, Chuqiao Lyu3, Shoujie Li4†, Wenbo Ding1†
Abstract— Learning
contact-rich
dexterous
manipulation
from human demonstrations provides a scalable source of
interaction data, yet transferring such skills to robots remains
challenging due to unreliable human–robot correspondence.
Existing human-to-robot transfer methods typically rely on
visual appearance or motion similarity, which may associate
similar motions with different contact states and force patterns.
Tactile dynamics provide interaction-aware cues to distinguish
manipulation processes with similar motions but different
contact states. We introduce tactile-guided optimal transport
(TacOT), a framework for human-to-robot contact-rich manip-
ulation. TacOT leverages action–tactile dynamic time warping
to identify human–robot demonstration correspondences with
consistent interaction dynamics and uses these correspondences
to guide soft optimal transport alignment in a shared policy rep-
resentation space. This enables human demonstrations to pro-
vide contact-rich supervision for robot policy learning without
requiring predefined frame-level human–robot pairing. Across
four real-world dexterous manipulation tasks, TacOT improves
closed-loop success rates over action-guided OT by up to 17
points on in-distribution tasks and 20 points under targeted
human-to-robot out-of-distribution transfer. Further analyses
show that tactile-guided correspondence selects demonstration
pairs with more consistent contact dynamics and produces
latent representations that better reflect interaction-state evo-
lution. These results demonstrate that tactile dynamics provide
an effective semantic signal for establishing reliable human-to-
robot correspondence in contact-rich dexterous manipulation.
I. INTRODUCTION
Learning contact-rich skills with dexterous robot hands
remains constrained by the scarcity of robot demonstrations.
Collecting multi-finger interactions on physical robots is
slow, labor-intensive, and difficult to scale across objects,
scenes, and contact conditions. Automated demonstration
generation and visuo-tactile pretraining improve data ef-
ficiency [1], [2], while large multi-embodiment datasets
broaden the coverage of robot learning [3]. Yet dexterous
manipulation remains underrepresented because its critical
variations lie not only in appearance and motion, but also in
how contact is established, maintained, and released.
Human demonstrations offer a scalable source of this miss-
ing interaction diversity. Wearable and in-the-wild systems
can capture natural hand motion, visual observations, and

These cues are insufficient for contact-rich dexterous manip-

morphologically different human and robot motions may
realize the same physical behavior. Consequently, demonstra-
tions can appear aligned in vision or kinematics while dif-
fering substantially in finger contacts and force distributions.
We therefore cast human-to-robot imitation as identifying
interaction-consistent behavior rather than merely closing
appearance- or motion-domain gaps. The relevant unit of cor-
respondence is a temporally extended action–contact pattern,
not an individual visually similar frame. Tactile trajectories
disambiguate interaction phases that vision and kinematics
leave uncertain. Here, tactile information serves not only
as a policy observation but also as a supervision signal for
matching human and robot demonstrations with consistent
interaction dynamics. Although prior work has demonstrated
the value of touch for policy conditioning, tactile representa-
tion learning, and cross-sensor transfer [7], [10], [11], [12],

The same interaction may exhibit temporal variations be-
tween human and robot demonstrations, and multiple human
segments may correspond to similar robot behaviors. There-
fore, effective correspondence requires flexible alignment
beyond frame-wise matching. Dynamic time warping (DTW)
addresses the temporal requirement through order-preserving
sequence alignment [15]. To support soft correspondence,
we adopt entropy-regularized optimal transport (OT), which
couples human and robot policy representations without pre-
defined one-to-one pairing [16], [17]. The key challenge is to
define a transport cost that reflects interaction compatibility
between human and robot behaviors. We use joint action–
tactile dynamics to guide this cost, favoring correspondences
consistent in both motion and contact.
We present tactile-guided optimal transport (TacOT),
a framework for contact-rich human-to-robot manipula-
tion. TacOT combines DTW distances computed separately
over action and tactile trajectories to identify interaction-
consistent human–robot demonstration pairs. These pairs
guide OT by reducing their latent transport costs, favoring
compatible interactions while retaining soft coupling across
all pairs. Optimized jointly with a multimodal flow-matching
objective, this alignment allows contact-rich human demon-
strations to supervise a shared policy space anchored by robot
data. Our contributions are as follows:
• We formulate human-to-robot imitation for contact-rich
dexterous manipulation as an interaction-consistent cor-
respondence learning problem, using joint action–tactile
dynamics to guide soft optimal transport alignment.
• We develop a matched-sensing human–robot platform
and a multimodal flow-matching policy for joint learn-
ing from visual, motion, and tactile demonstration data
from human and robot systems.
• We evaluate TacOT on four real-world dexterous manip-
ulation tasks. Compared with action-guided OT, TacOT
improves success rates by up to 17 percentage points
in-distribution and 20 percentage points under targeted
human-to-robot transfer.

III. SYSTEM AND METHOD
TacOT learns human-to-robot dexterous manipulation by
jointly leveraging human and robot demonstrations with
synchronized visual, motion, and tactile observations. We
first construct matched human and robot demonstration sys-
tems for multimodal data collection (Fig. 2). The collected
observations are then converted into unified representations
for multimodal policy learning and action–tactile-guided
latent alignment, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The following
sections describe the hardware design and data collection
(Sec. III-A), human–robot representation alignment (Sec. III-
B), multimodal flow policy learning (Sec. III-C), and action–
tactile-guided latent alignment (Sec. III-D).

A. Hardware Design and Data Collection
To support human-to-robot transfer, we construct two
matched-sensing demonstration systems for multimodal data
collection. The human demonstration system records natural
hand–object interactions, whereas the robot demonstration
system maps human motion to executable arm–hand com-
mands through teleoperation. Both systems collect visual,
motion, and tactile observations for human–robot represen-
tation learning. The human and robot datasets are collected
independently and are not linked by shared timestamps,
episode identifiers, or recorded cross-embodiment pairings.
Fig. 2 summarizes their hardware configurations and sensing
modalities.
Human
demonstration
system. The human system
records hand motion and fingertip contact forces using a
Manus data glove, a Vive Tracker with base stations, and
five three-axis Paxini force sensors. Each sensor is fixed to a
Manus fingertip using a 3D-printed mount, providing repeat-
able placement across demonstrations. Two Intel RealSense
D435 cameras record synchronized RGB observations at
a resolution of 640 × 480, providing wrist-view and third-
person views of the interaction.
Robot demonstration system. The robot system consists
of a UR5 manipulator equipped with an XHand dexterous
hand. During teleoperation, the human wrist trajectory is
mapped to the UR5 end effector (EEF), while glove signals
are converted online into XHand joint commands. The glove
and tracker signals used for teleoperation are not stored and
are not used for training. The robot system records only
robot-side EEF and hand trajectories, fingertip contact forces
measured by tactile sensors integrated into the XHand hand,
and dual-view RGB observations using two D435 cameras.
B. Human-Robot Representation Alignment
Although both human and robot systems collect visual,
motion, and tactile observations, their raw representations
differ due to embodiment-specific kinematics. We therefore
convert human and robot demonstrations into unified visual,
motion, and tactile representations for downstream learning.
Each system records synchronized multimodal observations
at 30 Hz, including wrist-view and third-person RGB images.
Spatial trajectory conversion. To establish a common
spatial reference across embodiments, human wrist and
XHand poses are expressed in the third-person camera frame
C as TH

Joint and action conversion. To bridge the morphological
differences between the human hand and XHand, we select
12 semantically corresponding Manus joints, map their angu-
lar ranges to those of the XHand, and arrange them according
to the XHand joint order. This yields a unified 12-DoF
hand representation. End-effector actions are represented as
camera-frame position offsets and wrapped roll–pitch–yaw
increments relative to the current pose, while hand actions
are represented as absolute joint angles in the XHand joint
space. Each target action chunk contains K = 64 future
steps. Robot chunks use 64 recorded frames, whereas human
chunks are linearly resampled from 32 frames to 64 steps.
The same interpolation weights are applied to action and
tactile sequences to preserve their temporal correspondence.
Tactile conversion. Both systems measure three-axis
forces at five fingertips, yielding 15 force channels. Sensor-
frame forces are transformed into a shared canonical frame
and arranged in a consistent finger and axis order. The action
and tactile signals are independently normalized to reduce
residual scale differences between embodiments.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate TacOT on four real-world dexterous manip-
ulation tasks using closed-loop success rates as the primary
metric. The evaluation includes in-distribution comparisons
with representative baselines, targeted human-to-robot trans-
fer to configurations unseen during robot training but repre-
sented in human demonstrations, analyses of action–tactile
correspondence and latent representations, and experiments
with varying numbers of human and robot demonstrations.
A. Experimental Setup
Tasks and success criteria. We evaluate TacOT on four
real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks: Tissue, Wiping,
Egg, and Block. These tasks cover diverse contact-rich
manipulation challenges, including flexible-object handling,
sustained surface interaction, fragile-object manipulation,
and geometry-sensitive insertion. All methods use the same
UR5–XHand platform and closed-loop control protocol.
We use task-specific binary success criteria. A trial is
successful if the tissue is placed in the designated region, the
target marks are removed during wiping, the egg is placed
intact inside the container, or the block is fully inserted
into the corresponding slot. The same criteria are applied
to all methods under both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-
distribution (OOD) conditions.
Data splits and evaluation protocol. We construct
episode-level training and evaluation splits to measure both
ID performance and human-to-robot transfer. For each task,
all human-augmented methods use the same 60 robot ID
episodes and 200 human demonstration episodes, including
100 ID and 100 OOD episodes. Robot-only uses the same 60
robot ID episodes without human demonstrations. The meth-
ods differ only in their training objectives, correspondence
mechanisms, and tactile observation inputs, as indicated
in Table I. OOD is defined relative to the robot training
domain: each task uses one configuration represented in the
human demonstrations but excluded from robot training. We
collect 100 human demonstrations for this configuration and
evaluate each method with 15 robot trials under the same
setting; the resulting success rates are reported in Table II.
The OOD changes are different tissue-package appearances
for Tissue, a black square changed to a red triangle for
Wiping, an orange egg changed to cyan for Egg, and a
rectangular block changed to circular for Block.
All methods are evaluated in the same scenes and initial
poses under closed-loop control. We perform 30 rollouts per
method–task pair for ID evaluation and 15 rollouts per pair
for OOD evaluation. Control runs at 30 Hz, and complete-
task binary success rate is used as the primary metric.

with identical human candidate sets for TacOT and action-
guided OT, aggregate ρq, and report the relative improvement
with 95% confidence intervals estimated by a robot-episode-
level clustered bootstrap. As shown in Fig. 8(a), TacOT
improves latent–contact-evolution rank alignment on all four
tasks, with relative gains of 27.1%, 41.2%, 52.0%, and 68.8%
on Tissue, Wiping, Egg, and Block, respectively. These
results indicate that tactile dynamics improve the ability of
the latent space to preserve interaction dynamics.
Finally, we evaluate whether the learned latent organi-
zation improves consistency during successive replanning.
For two temporally adjacent query windows q and q′, we
compare their generated hand-action sequences over the
shared prediction horizon and compute the overlap action-
plan MSE. Lower MSE indicates more consistent action
prediction. As shown in Fig. 8(b), TacOT reduces the overlap
action-plan MSE by 13.5%, 50.0%, 23.4%, and 43.5% on
Tissue, Wiping, Egg, and Block, respectively.


D. Demonstration Efficiency Analysis
The demonstration-efficiency results in Fig. 8(c)–(f) use
only ID demonstrations. We vary the number of robot
demonstrations with 100 human demonstrations fixed, and
vary the number of human demonstrations with 60 robot
demonstrations fixed. Increasing robot demonstrations con-
sistently improves performance across all tasks. Additional
human demonstrations are most beneficial when robot data
is limited: with 60 robot demonstrations, adding human
data raises Block success from 47% to 73%. These results
suggest that robot demonstrations provide stronger task-
specific execution supervision, while human demonstrations
provide complementary behavioral information.
When robot demonstrations are fixed, increasing human
demonstrations also improves joint-training performance. For
example, increasing human demonstrations from 30 to 100
raises success from 47% to 80% on Wiping and from 53%
to 73% on Block. However, with more robot demonstrations,
the marginal benefit of additional human data becomes
smaller.
V. CONCLUSION
We presented TacOT, a framework that uses action–
tactile correspondence to guide optimal transport alignment
for learning dexterous manipulation from human demonstra-
tions. Across four real-world tasks, TacOT achieves the best
or tied-best in-distribution success rates among the evalu-
ated methods and improves transfer to human-demonstrated
configurations unseen in robot training. Compared with
action-guided OT, TacOT incorporates tactile dynamics into
correspondence selection, yielding more contact-consistent
matches, stronger alignment between latent similarity and
interaction contact dynamics, and more consistent action pre-
dictions across successive replanning steps. Together, these
results support the use of tactile information as correspon-
dence supervision for human-to-robot policy learning.
The current evaluation focuses on human-to-robot transfer
with a fixed robot embodiment and relies on RGB observa-
tions without explicit 3D geometry. Future work will extend
TacOT to heterogeneous robot morphologies and incorporate
depth and multi-view geometric representations to improve
correspondence robustness under more general human-to-
robot transfer settings.
