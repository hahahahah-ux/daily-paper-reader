Fig. 1. Overall configuration of the proposed Digital-Twin-based Augmented Reality (AR) teleoperation interface for bimanual multi-fingered robotic hands.
Abstract—Teleoperation is becoming increasingly important
for collecting high-quality demonstrations to teach robots dex-
terous manipulation skills. For dexterous manipulation, bare-
hand tracking provides a practical way to control robotic hands
and demonstrate coordinated finger movements without gloves
or exoskeletons. However, this type of teleoperation faces two key
feedback limitations: a lack of force feedback and visual feedback
of occluded region. The absence of force feedback hinders precise
and safe manipulation, as operators must infer contact force
visually rather than feel them directly. Occlusion by objects
or other robot parts impairs the assessment of hand positions,
approach distances, and pre-grasp configurations suited to the
object’s shape. To address these limitations, we propose a digital-
twin-based augmented reality (AR) teleoperation interface that
integrates bare-hand tracking, bimanual robotic hands, and vir-
tual representations of the robot and task objects. The interface
renders tactile measurements on robot hand meshes as visuo-
force feedback. Furthermore, it offers a user-controlled virtual
view panel or occlusion-aware transparency rendering to provide
occlusion-mitigating visual feedback. We evaluated the interface
through user studies involving Task 1 and Task 2, examining how
force visualization and visual assistance support demonstration
collection in tasks requiring careful force regulation and manip-
ulation under occlusion.

I. INTRODUCTION
Teleoperation has become a primary means of collect-
ing high-fidelity demonstration data for robot learning by
recording robot actions and observations during human-guided
manipulation. Specifically, for dexterous robot hand manipu-
lation, human demonstrations provide high-dimensional joint
trajectories during interactions with diverse objects for robot
learning. Vision-based hand tracking and motion retarget-
ing enable operators to control robotic hands through bare-
hand movements across different robot configurations [1],
[2]. By eliminating the need for gloves or exoskeletons for
motion input, bare-hand interfaces provide a practical means
of demonstrating coordinated finger movements.
Despite these advantages, bare-hand teleoperation with typi-
cal visual feedback (i.e., bare-eyes or camera) provides limited
information about hand–object interactions. In a headset-
based setup, operators control the robot through tracked hand
movements while observing the physical workspace through
passthrough. Without force feedback, they must infer finger
contact and contact forces from visual cues, hindering efforts
to prevent slippage or excessive grasp forces. Moreover, occlu-
sion by objects or other robot parts obscures finger placement
and approach distances, hindering grasp formation. Collec-
tively, these limitations impede the monitoring and correction
of manipulation errors during demonstration collection.
Prior work has addressed the lack of force information
through wearable haptic devices or visual feedback. Vibro-
tactile, cutaneous or exoskeleton haptic devices on Glove can

convey contact forces, but require hand-worn hardware that
can restrict finger motion and complicate deployment across
operators [3]–[5]. As a visual alternative, TactAR [6] renders a
tactile sensor’s deformation field in AR and reports improved
demonstration quality relative to teleoperation without this
feedback. However, how to represent per-finger force informa-
tion for dexterous hand teleoperation remains underexplored,
particularly the relative benefits of force heatmap and force
arrow representations.
To address occlusion, previous studies position or move
physical cameras to obtain alternative views [7] or render the
robot or selected links transparent to reveal hidden regions [8],
[9]. However, these studies neither directly compare the two
approaches in dexterous hand teleoperation nor establish their
interaction with force visualization.
We propose digital-twin-based AR interfaces combining
visuo-force feedback with occlusion-mitigating visual feed-
back for bare-hand bimanual dexterous teleoperation. Digital-
twins of the robot and task objects are updated according
to robot states and tracked object poses to visualize contact
forces and occluded spatial relationships. We design two
force representations: (1) a force heatmap encoding tactile
signal magnitude through pad-level color and (2) force arrows
conveying direction and magnitude through fingertip-anchored
arrows (Fig. 2). For occlusion handling, we introduce (1) a
user-controlled virtual view panel, which displays an operator-
controlled virtual camera view alongside passthrough, and (2)
occlusion-aware transparency rendering, which reveals robot
fingers through semi-transparent objects in the digital-twin
layout (Fig. 4). These interfaces support force regulation and
grasp formation without hand-worn feedback devices.
We evaluated the interfaces through a user study assessing
task performance and perceived workload. The study com-
pared force heatmap, force arrow, and no visuo-force feedback
in Task 1, which requires careful force regulation with unob-
structed contact regions. In Task 2, we combined the force
visualization evaluated in the Task 1 user study with the user-
controlled virtual view panel and occlusion-aware transparency
rendering. We analyzed and evaluated its interaction with each
occlusion-assistance technique during bimanual lifting with
occluded grasp openings. In Task 1, both force visualizations
significantly reduce perceived workload relative to no visuo-
force feedback. The cup deformation rate is 13.3% with force
arrow feedback, compared with 44.4% without visuo-force
feedback. In Task 2, occlusion-aware transparency rendering
achieves the highest observed success rate (82.1%) when
combined with force arrow feedback.

II. RELATED WORKS
A. Robot Hand Teleoperation
AnyTeleop [1] supports vision-based arm–hand teleopera-
tion across different robot arm, hand, and camera configura-
tions, emphasizing adaptability across hardware setups. Open
Teach [2] combines HMD hand tracking with close-up vi-
sual feedback for multi-fingered and bimanual control. Open-
TeleVision [10] couples arm and hand motion transfer with
active stereoscopic feedback, integrating motion input with im-
mersive workspace observation. Alongside these vision-based
approaches, wearable gloves provide an alternative means of
tracking hand motion for teleoperation [11], [12].
While these approaches support motion input and task
observation, hand tracking alone does not convey robot contact
forces, and scene views may leave hand–object interactions
occluded. Our bare-hand AR interfaces therefore combine
visuo-force feedback with occlusion-mitigating visual feed-
back to support perception of forces and hand–object spatial
relationships during bimanual manipulation.
B. Tactile Feedback for Human Operators
Wearable haptic devices convey contact information through
vibration [4] or combine hand tracking with force feedback for
robotic manipulation [3] and virtual manipulation [5]. Visual
feedback studies compare color-based force representations in
robot-assisted surgery [13] and display forces as arrows at
the end effector [14]. TactAR [6] presents tactile and force
information as an AR deformation field registered to the robot
end-effector.
Wearable approaches require hand-worn hardware, while
the cited visual approaches examine surgical interaction, end-
effector force display, or parallel-jaw grasping rather than a
common dexterous hand setting. Their evaluations therefore
do not directly establish how force heatmap and force arrow
representations compare in bimanual dexterous teleoperation.
We compare these representations with a baseline without
force visualization on a task requiring precise grasp force
regulation.
C. Occlusion-Mitigating Visual Feedback
Prior systems employ an assistant robot arm to adjust
camera viewpoints [7] or a drone to provide task-relevant
views [15]. VICARIOS [16] enables viewpoint changes within
a virtual environment. Other approaches reveal occluded re-
gions by rendering the robot transparent in diminished re-
ality [8] or rendering selected digital-twin robot links semi-
transparent during task demonstration [9].
Although these studies explore viewpoint adjustment and
transparency, they do not directly compare the two approaches
within a common dexterous hand teleoperation setting. These
studies also do not establish the collaboration effect be-
tween force feedback and occlusion-mitigating feedback. We
therefore compare a user-controlled virtual view panel in a


3
passthrough interface with occlusion-aware transparency ren-
dering in a digital-twin interface, alongside a baseline without
occlusion handling. Each interface condition is evaluated with
and without visuo-force feedback during bimanual manipula-
tion under occlusion.
III. INTERFACE DESIGN
A. Object Reconstruction for the Digital-Twin
We adopt 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [17] to reconstruct
the physical task objects for the digital-twin environment
because it supports photorealistic novel-view rendering in real
time. HIL-GS [18] highlights the value of interactive 3D visu-
alization for providing spatial context beyond the limited per-
spectives of live camera streams in robotic teleoperation. In our
interface, this capability motivates the adoption of 3DGS to
preserve object appearance while allowing operators to inspect
hand–object spatial relationships from viewpoints beyond their
direct view. We construct each object’s representation from
images captured around it, enabling virtual viewpoint changes
without placing or moving a physical camera at the selected
viewing location.
To obtain a surface representation alongside Gaussian splat-
ting, we deploy the reconstruction and refinement pipeline of
RORA [19], a refined version of GS2Mesh [20] which can
reconstruct more refined meshes than GS2Mesh. The pipeline
builds on GS2Mesh for initial reconstruction and refines the
resulting mesh by removing isolated fragments, reconstructing
the surface, remeshing, and filling holes to obtain watertight
geometry. These steps address missing surfaces and recon-
struction artifacts in the initial mesh. The resulting Gaussian
splatting and mesh assets are integrated into the virtual task
scene.
During teleoperation, the virtual objects are positioned ac-
cording to the tracked object state so that they can be displayed
together with the virtual robot. The reconstructed scene sup-
ports the user-controlled virtual view panel in the passthrough
interface and occlusion-aware transparency rendering in the
digital-twin interface, as described in the following subsec-
tions.
B. Visuo-Force Feedback
We design the visuo-force feedback interface to be inde-
pendent of a specific robot platform, with sensor-to-visual
mappings that can be adapted to different robotic hands. The
interface employs fingertip tactile measurements to provide
contact information through two visual representations: force
heatmap and force arrow. Figure 2 presents both representa-
tions on the left and right robotic hands.
1) Force Heatmap: Drawing on force-to-color mappings
employed in robot-assisted surgery [13], we apply color-based
force feedback to individual fingers of a multi-fingered robotic
hand. Each fingertip sensor pad is uniformly colored according
to its tactile signal magnitude to indicate contact and its
intensity.

C. Occlusion Handling
We design two approaches to provide occlusion-mitigating
visual feedback: (1) a user-controlled virtual view panel in
the passthrough layout and (2) occlusion-aware transparency
rendering in the digital-twin layout. Both approaches utilize a
virtual scene implemented in Unreal Engine 5.3 and displayed
through the operator’s Meta Quest 3 headset.
1) User-Controlled Virtual View Panel: Inspired by camera-
viewpoint adjustment in telemanipulation [7], [22], the user-
controlled virtual view panel allows the operator to control the
pose of the virtual camera within the reconstructed digital-twin
task scene. The display panel renders the reconstructed virtual
scene, allowing operators to select a virtual viewpoint without
placing or moving a physical camera at the selected location.
The rendered view is displayed alongside the passthrough view
of the physical workspace.
The virtual robot is initially aligned with the physical
robot in passthrough, after which the virtual scene is updated
according to robot states and the tracked object pose. During
the approach to the task object, the interface monitors the
distance between the virtual robot and the virtual object.
When this distance falls at or below a predefined threshold,
teleoperation is automatically paused. Visual references of the
operator’s tracked hands are retained at their poses at the
moment of pausing, and the display panel and camera-control


4
buttons automatically appear in the operator’s field of view
(Fig. 4).
The operator translates and rotates the virtual camera
through the buttons and selects a viewpoint for the subsequent
manipulation. Pressing the confirmation button hides the but-
tons and places the panel at the center of the operator’s field of
view. Teleoperation remains paused until the operator aligns
their tracked hands with the retained hand references, at which
point control resumes immediately. The panel then provides
the selected virtual view during task execution. The interface
does not support reselecting the viewpoint after confirmation
.
2) Occlusion-Aware Transparency Rendering: Occlusion
by the task object obscures finger placement and hand–object
contact regions, hindering grasp formation. We therefore
present the virtual robot hands and task object in front of the
operator, preserving their relative positions and orientations to
support egocentric observation of hand–object spatial relation-
ships. The operator’s hand movements are mapped to relative
changes in position and orientation within this configuration.
The virtual scene is updated according to wrist poses received
from the robot and the tracked object pose.
When the fingers are sufficiently far from the object, the
object is rendered opaque to convey its shape and preserve
depth-ordering cues. As a fingertip approaches, the interface
locally reduces object opacity around the viewing ray from the
operator to that fingertip, with stronger transparency at shorter
distances. This reveals otherwise occluded fingers and regions
near contact while retaining the surrounding object surface.
The transparent regions follow changes in fingertip positions
and the operator’s viewpoint, with multiple regions applied
when several fingers approach the object. A semi-transparent
object silhouette is displayed together with the local trans-
parency to retain a visual reference for the object’s overall
shape and spatial extent. Both effects are removed as the
fingers move away.
To provide additional spatial cues, red markers indicate the
object surface points closest to reference points on the virtual
fingertips and palms. Identifying contact locations enables
the operator to assess which parts of the object each finger
supports and adjust hand position and orientation during grasp
formation. The markers support this assessment by visualizing
the correspondence between occluded hand regions and the
object surface. They are updated as the hands move and remain
visible through the object and hands.
The transparency rendering updates automatically during
manipulation without requiring viewpoint selection or confir-
mation. When visuo-force feedback is enabled, force arrows
display tactile measurements at the corresponding virtual fin-
gers. The operator can therefore observe finger placement rel-
ative to the object together with the force magnitude measured
at each finger. Local transparency, the object silhouette, and the
surface markers remain available when visuo-force feedback
is disabled.

To accommodate the different tactile sensor layouts, the

virtual object’s position and orientation, allowing the virtual

IV. EXPERIMENT DESIGN
A. Participants
A total of 15 participants (13 male, 2 female) were recruited
from a university. Their mean age was 25.0 years (SD = 2.0,
range: 21–28). All 15 participants took part in Task 1. Of these,
14 also participated in Task 2; one participant was unable
to attend Task 2. Regarding prior experience, 2 participants
(13.3) had previously operated a teleoperated robot, and 2
participants (13.3) had prior experience with a VR head-
mounted display.
B. Tasks
Participants performed Task 1 and Task 2, involving precise
grasp force regulation and object-induced occlusion, respec-
tively. Figure 4 presents representative views of both tasks
across the feedback and interface conditions.

a paper cup (190 ml) with the fingertips of both hands, and
transported it 450 mm to a target location, where it had to
be placed upright. A paper cup is a highly deformable object;

could not visually confirm how many fingers were engaged
with the box through each opening; visuo-force feedback
provided this per-finger contact information, which directly
determined task success. A trial was considered a success if
at least three of the four non-thumb fingers of each hand
were inserted into the openings, the box was lifted at least
10 cm above the ground, and it was held in that position for
4 seconds. A trial was considered a failure if two or fewer
non-thumb fingers of either hand were inserted when the box
was lifted, or if the box was dropped before the 4-second hold
was completed.

B. Task 2: Box Lifting
Task 2 evaluates performance in the box lifting task using
success rate and insertion retries. Trial outcomes were clas-
sified according to the success and failure criteria described
in the Task 2 procedure. Insertion retries were recorded by
an on-site supervisor and defined as additional attempts after
the initial insertion attempt; zero retries therefore indicates
successful insertion on the first attempt. The analysis included
14 participants and 28 trials per condition.
Success rates were calculated over all trials in each con-
dition. Insertion retries were summarized only for successful
trials.
Across the 168 trials, occlusion-aware transparency ren-
dering with force arrow feedback yields the highest task
success rate (82.1%, 23/28), compared with the user-controlled
virtual view panel (71.4%, 20/28) and the baseline without
occlusion handling (Baseline; 57.1%, 16/28) under the same
feedback condition. Without visuo-force feedback, occlusion-
aware transparency rendering and the user-controlled virtual
view panel both achieve a success rate of 78.6% (22/28),
compared with 60.7% (17/28) for the baseline.
Occlusion-aware transparency rendering also shows the
fewest insertion retries among successful trials in both feed-
back conditions. Without visuo-force feedback, the mean
number of retries is 0.59 with occlusion-aware transparency


7
rendering, compared with 1.18 with the user-controlled virtual
view panel and 1.47 with the baseline. With force arrow
feedback, the corresponding means are 0.70, 1.50, and 0.81.
The variability observed in the Baseline condition appeared
to reflect individual differences in performing insertion without
occlusion assistance. Some participants succeeded on the first
attempt, whereas others frequently failed or required multiple
retries even in successful trials.
The higher success rates of both proposed occlusion han-
dling interfaces relative to the baseline under both feedback
conditions suggest that visualizing otherwise occluded hand–
object spatial relationships supports grasp formation during
box lifting. In particular, occlusion-aware transparency ren-
dering combines high success rates with the fewest insertion
retries among successful trials. These descriptive results sug-
gest that occlusion-aware transparency rendering can support
manipulation with occluded grasp openings.
VI. CONCLUSION
This study examined how force feedback and spatial visual-
ization support teleoperation in deformation-sensitive manip-
ulation and insertion under occlusion. In Task 1, force arrow
feedback yielded the lowest observed deformation rate, sug-
gesting its potential for deformation-sensitive manipulation.
However, the results did not establish a statistically signifi-
cant improvement in overall task completion. The observed
failure pattern suggests a possible trade-off between reducing
deformation and maintaining grasp stability, motivating further
work on grasp retention. Building on these findings, Task 2
examined force arrow feedback in combination with visual
techniques designed to improve spatial awareness under oc-
clusion.
In Task 2, occlusion-aware transparency rendering yielded
the lowest mean number of insertion retries among successful
trials, both with and without visuo-force feedback. When
combined with force arrow feedback, it also achieved the
highest observed success rate. Red surface markers and local
object transparency provided visual references for fingertip
approach locations and revealed otherwise occluded finger–
opening relationships during insertion. Force arrows addi-
tionally conveyed per-finger contact information and force
magnitude, allowing users to interpret these signals in relation
to finger position. This combination may have supported both
insertion adjustments and contact-state assessment, suggesting
a potential synergy between spatial visualization and visuo-
force feedback.
In the user-controlled virtual view panel condition, partici-
pants frequently selected viewpoints that did not adequately
reveal the insertion state, and some reported high mental
demand when using the panel. We interpret these observations
as reflecting the panel’s dependence on the suitability of the
selected viewpoint and the additional demands that viewpoint
selection and interpretation may place on some operators.
Together, these findings highlight the importance of presenting
spatial and force information in a shared visual context while
reducing the effort required to obtain a task-relevant view
during teleoperation under occlusion.
VII. LIMITATIONS
This study evaluated the interfaces with a limited set of
objects and controlled task configurations. The current im-
plementation requires prior object reconstruction and marker-
based pose tracking, which limits immediate use with unfamil-
iar objects or in unprepared environments. Further evaluation
is therefore needed across a broader range of object geome-
tries, occlusion conditions, and workspace configurations.
The spatial information presented by the digital twin also
depends on reconstruction accuracy and the alignment and
synchronization of robot and object states. Further investiga-
tion is needed to characterize how reconstruction errors, reg-
istration errors, and update latency affect users’ interpretation
of the rendered scene and their task performance.
