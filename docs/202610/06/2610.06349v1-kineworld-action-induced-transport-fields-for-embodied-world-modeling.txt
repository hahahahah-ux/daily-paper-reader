ABSTRACT
Embodied world models predict the visual consequences of candidate actions be-
fore execution. However, existing action-conditioned world models often adopt
uniformly weighted visual generation objectives that can be misaligned with em-
bodied prediction needs. Even with explicit motion conditioning, these objectives
can underemphasize spatially sparse changes that are critical to interaction. We
propose KineWorld, a transport-aware world-modeling framework that extends
robot kinematics from motion conditioning to the spatial allocation of generative
supervision. Kinematic Transport Lifting (KTL) constructs renderer-derived,
camera-aligned transport fields from commanded robot motion.
Transport-
Aware World Diffusion (TAWD) calibrates their motion support on the video-
latent grid and reweights future-RGB flow matching through a normalized mix-
ture of uniform and transport-focused distributions. We train KineWorld using
ALOHA–AgileX bimanual manipulation data from RoboTwin 2.0. KineWorld
achieves an EWMScore-P of 68.95 in single-view evaluation and a TWB-Score
of 54.82 in multi-view evaluation. These results support a shift from appearance
fitting toward action-consequence modeling for embodied decision-making.

INTRODUCTION
World Action Models (WAMs) enable robots to anticipate how the visual world will evolve un-
der candidate actions before executing them in the physical environment. By generating action-
conditioned future observations, these models can serve as predictive simulators for policy learning,
action evaluation, planning, and synthetic experience generation. Recent advances in large-scale
video generation further provide strong appearance and dynamics priors, making it possible to con-
struct visually realistic robot rollouts from limited embodied data Zhu et al. (2024); Guo et al.
(2025); Liao et al. (2025).
Existing world action models (WAMs) improve action responsiveness through numerical action to-
kens, learned latent codes, and spatially aligned conditions such as embodiment masks, action im-
ages, optical flow, and projected kinematic fields Zhu et al. (2024); Guo et al. (2025); Wang et al.
(2026); Liao et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2026b). Yet motion conditioning does not itself alter the spa-
tial weighting of the primary RGB-generation objective. When action-responsive regions occupy
only a small part of the frame, uniform token averaging can bias optimization toward static appear-
ance. Motion-sensitive auxiliary objectives and flow-branch reweighting address related concerns
Zhu et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2026b). We further use robot kinematics to guide future-RGB super-
vision, aligning the model’s learning priorities with action consequences. This raises a key question:
how can robot kinematics guide the learning of action consequences?
Our key insight is that action conditioning and supervision allocation serve different purposes: the
former specifies what motion to generate, whereas the latter determines which prediction errors

transport, while Transport-Aware World Diffusion (TAWD) calibrates its motion support on the
video latent grid and converts the resulting support into a normalized mixture distribution for the
RGB flow-matching objective. The resulting objective redistributes supervision while preserving the
aggregate token weight, without adding inference parameters or sampling steps. This extends kine-
matic priors from generation conditions to learning guidance for interaction- and decision-oriented
world prediction. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Action-Consequence-Oriented Learning. We distinguish visual extent from interaction
importance, motivating kinematics-guided learning of interaction-critical local changes.
• KineWorld Framework. We propose KineWorld, a transport-aware framework with KTL
and TAWD. KTL constructs camera-aligned transport from the commanded rollout, while
TAWD reweights RGB flow matching via a normalized mixture on the latent grid, preserv-
ing token weight and sampling.
• Benchmark Evaluation. Trained on RoboTwin 2.0 ALOHA–AgileX bimanual manipula-
tion data (Chen et al., 2025), KineWorld achieves an EWMScore-P of 68.95 and a TWB-
Score of 54.82 in single-view and multi-view evaluation, respectively. Our framework and
evaluations provide the community with a reference for action-consequence-oriented world
modeling.

RELATED WORK
World Action Models.
World Action Models (WAMs) increasingly leverage pretrained video
generators to connect visual prediction with robot control (Guo et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025). Exist-
ing approaches include action-conditioned video prediction (Zhu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025), joint
video-action generation (Zhou et al., 2026), and action decoding from visual latent representations
(Liao et al., 2025). Recent systems further broaden this paradigm. RealWonder connects physi-
cal actions to video generation through simulated RGB and flow (Liu et al., 2026), while CLAP
integrates heterogeneous action representations for cross-embodiment learning (Liu & Shorinwa,
2


2026). However, advances in generative architectures and action interfaces do not inherently pri-
oritize interaction-critical changes in the primary RGB objective. When these changes occupy few
visual tokens, uniform weighting can underemphasize their contribution. KineWorld addresses this
complementary problem through kinematics-guided allocation of future-RGB supervision.
Action Representations for WAMs.
Existing WAMs encode robot actions as numerical tokens,
learned latent codes, or spatially grounded visual cues (Zhu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026a; Zhou
et al., 2026). Recent methods align actions with the visual domain through embodiment masks
(Chen et al., 2026c), multi-view action videos (Zhen et al., 2026), optical flow (Chen et al., 2026b),
and camera-aligned kinematic fields (Yang et al., 2026). Beyond conditioning, FlowWAM reweights
its flow-branch loss using reference-relative latent deviations (Chen et al., 2026b), while EA-WM
uses frame-difference supervision to guide cross-stream fusion (Yang et al., 2026). In contrast,
KineWorld uses kinematic transport to directly reweight future-RGB flow matching without intro-
ducing auxiliary objectives.

KINEWORLD
4.1
OVERVIEW
KineWorld is a latent video world model that predicts future RGB observations from the current ob-
servation, a language instruction, and a candidate action sequence. A causal video VAE represents
visual states in latent space, while a conditional flow model learns their action-conditioned dynam-
ics. When projected robot motion occupies only a small part of the video, uniform token averaging
assigns this region a correspondingly small share of the total token weight. KineWorld uses com-
manded kinematics to guide both generation and learning: camera-aligned transport conditions the
predicted motion, while its spatial support directs a fixed share of future-RGB supervision toward
regions of projected robot motion. KineWorld comprises two components. Kinematic Transport
Lifting (KTL) constructs camera-aligned transport and injects its latent representation zu into the
video generator as a clean structural condition. Transport-Aware World Diffusion (TAWD) de-
rives calibrated support d from the transport fields and constructs normalized token weights for
the flow-matching objective. KTL supplies motion conditions during both training and inference,
whereas TAWD modifies only training-time supervision.

Training and Inference.
During training, TAWD derives the calibrated support d from the
renderer-derived transport and converts it into ρtr and w for reweighting the flow-matching ob-
jective. At inference time, the support calibration and loss-weighting branch is not required. The
clean transport latent zu remains as a structural condition, while the learned vector field is integrated
from τ = 1 to τ = 0 to obtain future RGB latents, which are decoded into future video frames.

CONCLUSION
We presented KineWorld, a kinematics-guided framework for action-conditioned video prediction.
KTL constructs camera-aligned transport, and TAWD calibrates its support and uses it to redistribute
future-RGB supervision between action-responsive regions and the full scene. The objective pre-
serves the total token weight and recovers uniform supervision under zero motion, without adding
inference parameters or denoising steps. Trained on ALOHA–AgileX bimanual manipulation data
from RoboTwin 2.0, KineWorld achieves an EWMScore-P of 68.95 in single-view evaluation and a
TWB-Score of 54.82 in multi-view evaluation. These results highlight the potential of kinematics-
guided supervision to advance world models from appearance fitting toward action-consequence
modeling for embodied decision-making.

KineWorld relies on calibrated robot and camera models, and
its transport support does not explicitly capture object motion or contact dynamics. Future work
will investigate calibration-robust transport estimation, object-aware supervision, and contact-aware
world modeling.

A.1
BROADER IMPACTS
KineWorld may improve the action responsiveness of embodied world models by directing learning
toward image regions that are causally associated with commanded robot motion. More faithful
action-conditioned rollouts could support simulation, policy evaluation, and data generation while
reducing unnecessary physical interaction. At the same time, generated videos remain predictions
rather than certified physical trajectories: errors in calibration, contact modeling, or long-horizon
generation can produce plausible but unsafe outcomes. KineWorld should therefore be used together
with independent feasibility checks, collision detection, controller limits, and validation on the target
robot.
A.2
CONTRIBUTIONS
Problem Discovery.
We distinguish action conditioning from supervision allocation.
Action-
induced motion can occupy only a small fraction of video-latent tokens, so uniform averaging of
the RGB-generation objective can underemphasize prediction errors in action-responsive regions.
Explicit motion conditions alone do not correct this weighting imbalance.
KineWorld Framework.
We introduce KineWorld, a transport-aware embodied world-modeling
framework that promotes robot kinematics from an auxiliary condition to a signal for allocating gen-
erative supervision. Given the initial embodiment configuration and commanded action sequence,
KineWorld constructs camera-aligned transport support and redistributes token-wise supervision be-
tween global scene modeling and action-responsive regions while preserving the total token weight.
Core Modules.
Kinematic Transport Lifting (KTL) rolls out the commanded configurations, ren-
ders the embodiment from the observation camera, and estimates source-supported inter-frame trans-
port. Transport-Aware World Diffusion (TAWD) calibrates this activity on the video-latent grid,
converts the calibrated support into a normalized mixture distribution, and uses mean-one weights
to reallocate the conditional flow-matching objective, while preserving uniform supervision under
zero motion and introducing no additional denoising steps.
Experiments.
Our evaluation distinguishes retained system profiles, local generation diagnostics,
and an executed matched comparison. In the matched comparison, two 50-update continuations
start from the same step-315 checkpoint, keep KTL conditioning fixed, and differ only in the future-
RGB weighting parameter β. Changing β = 0 to β = 1 improves EWMScore-P from 66.10 to
67.85 (∆= +1.75), with additional gains of 5.60 points in Trajectory Accuracy and 4.20 points in
Interaction Quality.

A.3
ROBOTWIN 2.0 DATA, LOCAL PROTOCOLS, AND RETAINED PROFILES
Single-View Local Protocol.
A single-view example comprises an initial head-camera observa-
tion, language instruction, initial robot configuration, and action trajectory. Future RGB frames
serve as references for reference-based metrics. Following the action definition in Section 3.1, each
arm is controlled by an absolute 6-DoF end-effector pose target and one gripper command, yielding
a 14-D bimanual action vector. The reported 640 × 480, 24-fps videos use a pipeline-specific export
convention. Resolution and playback rate do not specify the dataset split or the physical control
frequency.
Data Partition.
Clean-50 contains 2,500 episodes across 50 tasks. Within each task, episodes 0–
35, 36–39, and 40–49 define the development-training, validation, and held-out partitions, respec-
tively (Table A.1). These partitions are episode-disjoint but share the same tasks. The combined
2,000-episode training set includes development training and validation; the 200-episode valida-
tion partition is therefore not unseen for a model trained on this combined set. Held-out denotes
exclusion from training, not the number of completed model evaluations.
Evaluation Collections and Result Tables.
Single-view reference profiles appear in Tables 1
and A.2, local single-view diagnostics in Tables 2 and A.3, and multi-view reference profiles in
Table 3. The KTL diagnostic uses episodes 1–100 of a separately released three-view validation
collection, with 300 videos per variant. The interpolation diagnostic in Table A.4 uses episodes
17–32 of the single-view generation collection: 16 videos and 3,770 frames at 640 × 480 and 24
fps. These collection-local identifiers are not Clean-50 task–episode identifiers; reference profiles
and diagnostics are not reassigned to the Clean-50 held-out partition. Table 6 reports the executed
matched TAWD comparison over M = 1000 paired single-view episodes. This comparison is
interpreted separately from the retained system profiles and local diagnostic collections.
Counting and Filtering.
An episode is counted once regardless of camera views or temporal
clips. All 2,500 Clean-50 episodes passed data-preparation checks for required observations, videos,
instructions, trajectories, and camera calibration; missing inputs trigger a failure rather than silent
filtering. Screening against the separate 1,000-episode single-view test collection found no exact
initial-image or action-array matches, so it excluded no additional episodes. The executed matched
TAWD comparison uses this separate 1,000-episode single-view test collection; all M = 1000
episode pairs are retained for the paired analysis. This screen tests exact duplication, not task-level
or semantic independence.
Training Scope.
The FlowWAM checkpoint used in the interpolation diagnostic was trained on
the combined 2,000-episode Clean-50 set. This training-set assignment is specific to that checkpoint,
rather than a shared setting for all reference profiles. External method scores retain their original
training conditions.
Three-View Local Protocol.
The three-view protocol uses synchronized head, left-wrist, and
right-wrist observations together with an initial robot state, instruction, and action sequence. Each
view uses its own camera calibration and transport condition, including time-dependent extrin-
sics for moving wrist cameras. The reported inference convention generates 17 uniformly spaced
keyframes and expands them to the episode length. This convention describes temporal sampling
and does not define episode membership. Reference-based metrics use the corresponding future
observations from each camera.

Scope of Reported Results.
The appendix separates four evidence layers: externally reported
system profiles, the retained KineWorld candidate profile, local generation diagnostics, and the ex-
ecuted matched TAWD comparison in Table 6. The first three layers retain their original evaluation
scope and do not provide common-training causal estimates. The matched comparison fixes initial-
ization, training budget, KTL conditioning, data order, and evaluation, and changes only β. The
retained KineWorld profile has EWMScore-P 68.95, whereas the matched β = 1 continuation in
Table 6 has EWMScore-P 67.85. These values correspond to different training scopes and are not
intended as two measurements of the same run. EWMScore-P follows the 15-component percep-
tual scoring convention defined in Appendix A.3.1; TWB-Score follows TriWorldBench’s separate
19-component multi-view convention in Appendix A.3.2. Neither is a newly proposed metric or a
RoboTwin 2.0 task success rate. Method citations identify the underlying approaches; they do not
establish common evaluation conditions. The objective-label interpretation is specified separately in
Appendix A.8.2.

Support Interpretation.
The transport field is a renderer-derived, camera-aligned estimate of pro-
jected robot motion, not a sufficient statistic for the full action. Gripper semantics, contact forces,
object state, and unobserved dynamics may not be recoverable from image-plane transport alone.
KineWorld uses this estimate to decide where supervision is concentrated while language, the initial
scene, and the generative model retain responsibility for the remaining dynamics. Figure A.3 con-
ceptually illustrates the relation between projected robot motion and normalized transport support.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Training Sequence.
A reproducible KineWorld update consists of the following operations:
1. integrate the control sequence from the initial embodiment configuration and sample K =
9 visual states at stride r = 4;
2. render robot-only frames with the training camera and obtain source-grid forward flow
between successive rendered states;
3. mask flow by source-frame robot occupancy, clip displacement at Mpx = 25, and construct
the three-channel transport video;
4. encode RGB and transport clips with the video-VAE interfaces, keeping the initial RGB
and transport streams clean;
5. downsample source-supported activity to the future-RGB latent grid and normalize it ac-
cording to Equation 44;
6. predict the conditional flow field with the dual-stream denoiser and apply Equation 45; and
7. update the optional action expert through detached generator features and add λactLact to
the scalar training objective.

A.8.3
FRAME-LEVEL DIAGNOSTIC DEFINITIONS
The measurements in Table A.6 are complementary local diagnostics rather than newly proposed
or official benchmark metrics. Let Yt ∈[0, 1]H×W denote the luma channel of frame t. A-MAD
averages the pixel-wise absolute difference between adjacent Yt; CPF reports the fraction of pix-
els whose adjacent-frame change exceeds the evaluator’s fixed threshold; and FL-MAD applies the
same absolute-difference statistic to the first and last frames. NDP is the fraction of adjacent pairs
classified as near duplicates by a fixed frame-difference test. Frame differencing and cross-frame
mean absolute differences have long been used in video analysis, including temporal-flicker evalua-
tion (Huang et al., 2024).
For motion diagnostics, we estimate dense flow between adjacent frames with Farneb¨ack’s two-
frame method (Farneb¨ack, 2003). FB Mean and FB P95 are the mean and 95th percentile of the
resulting flow magnitudes, while Jerk summarizes temporal variation across successive flow fields
as a heuristic smoothness measure. Related differential-flow and jerk-based analyses precede this
work (Shen et al., 2020; Takeda et al., 2018). These statistics characterize different failure modes:
adjacent differences are sensitive to both motion and flicker, first-to-last difference measures end-
point change but not path quality, near-duplicate rate exposes repetition, and flow magnitude or jerk

A.10
LIMITATIONS AND REPRODUCIBILITY
Limitations.
KineWorld relies on calibrated robot and camera models, and its transport support is
sensitive to calibration errors, flow estimation errors, and occlusion. The support captures projected
robot motion but does not explicitly model object motion or contact dynamics. Autoregressive roll-
out may accumulate appearance drift, while transport-focused supervision may trade visual fidelity
for motion quality. The matched study evaluates TAWD over a 50-update continuation from a shared
checkpoint; convergence and stability across training seeds remain to be assessed. Future work will
investigate object-aware transport, calibration-robust conditioning, and cross-embodiment transfer.
Safety.
Generated rollouts are predictive media rather than certified robot trajectories. They must
not be used as evidence of collision-free or hardware-safe execution. Deployment requires indepen-
dent state estimation, feasibility and collision checking, controller limits, emergency handling, and
validation in the target simulator and robot system.
Reproducibility Specification.
Reproducing the controlled comparison requires:
• source code for rendering, support construction, training, sampling, and evaluation;
• pinned environment and dependency versions, including renderer, RAFT, and VAE revi-
sions;
• data-split and episode manifests with checksums and license notes;
• parent and trained checkpoint hashes plus complete run configurations;
• external-source records and local or matched per-episode evaluations with bootstrap scripts;
• generated-video manifests linking each output to its input, seed, and checkpoint; and
• timing, memory, seed-variation, ablation, qualitative-selection, and failure-case reports.
Scope of Conclusions.
The normalization and zero-motion properties follow from the objective
definition. The reported system profiles and local diagnostics characterize their respective evaluation
scopes; their interpretation is distinct from the causal effect of changing future-RGB supervision
(Appendix A.8.2).
