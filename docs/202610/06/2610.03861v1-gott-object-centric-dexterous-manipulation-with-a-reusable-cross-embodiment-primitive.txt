Abstract. Foundation models and large-scale human data provide rich sources of manipulation intent, but translat-ing this intent into multi-fingered robot behavior remains difficult. ## 1. Introduction 

Foundation models [16, 39] and large-scale human data [3, 60] provide rich sources of manipulation intent. For parallel-jaw grippers, such intent can be translated into robot actions through mature low-level primitives such as grasping, placing, and trajectory following [23, 24, 31]. However, the same approach does not directly extend to dexterous hands, which lack a comparable set of general-purpose low-level skills. These skills must establish complex contact configurations, coordinate high-dimensional actions, and adapt to different hand morphologies. Existing dexterous manipulation methods usually address these challenges through task-specific sim-to-real learning [40] or hand-motion retargeting [30]. However, these approaches are not well suited to converting high-level intent into robot behavior. Task-specific policies [9, 19, 44, 45] require retraining for each new behavior, making them difficult to scale. Retargeting recovers kinematic targets from demonstrations [37], often without incorporating perceptual feedback [41]. This lack of feedback makes contact-rich tasks difficult to execute in the real world. This raises a key question: what is the right interface for translating high-level manipulation intent into low-level dexterous execution? 

Object-centric motion representations provide a promising answer to this question. Prior work has shown that many rigid-object manipulation tasks can be described by the desired object motion [20, 27, 43], abstracting away embodiment-specific robot motion. However, an object trajectory specifies how the object should move , but not where and how the hand should establish contact . This distinction is critical for functional manipulation: a geometrically stable grasp may still be unsuitable for pouring, sweeping, or tool use. Prior methods address this gap by supplying a pre-grasp or hand reference [10, 32], or by learning contact acquisition jointly with trajectory execution [26, 28]. While effective, these designs do not expose contact acquisition as a reusable skill across high-level sources and robot embodiments. We propose General Object Trajectory Tracking (GOTT ), a framework for executing diverse object motions across tasks and robot embodiments using a shared contact-acquisition primitive. We represent high-level manipulation intent using a desired object trajectory and a reach specification , which specifies how to bring the hand near a task-relevant contact region. These components may come from different sources. For example, the object trajectory may be extracted from a video, while the reach specification may be generated through affordance-aware planning or obtained by retargeting a human trajectory. 

GOTT implements this interface through a reach, acquire, and move decomposition. During reach , the robot follows the reach specification toward the selected contact region. During acquire , a learned closed-loop contact-acquisition primitive establishes stable contact from this approximate initialization. During move ,an object-pose-conditioned controller tracks the desired object trajectory. The primitive uses only local hand-object geometry and proprioceptive history, while a canonical per-link representation allows the same policy to be shared across hands. Reach specifications can come from VLM-guided, future-aware planning, external models, or demonstrations, while the contact-acquisition primitive remains unchanged. We evaluate GOTT in simulation and the real world. First, we test whether the contact-acquisition primitive can robustly grasp diverse objects across different hands and transfer zero-shot to unseen hand morphologies. We then evaluate the full pipeline on object-trajectory tracking tasks, including trash cleanup, pouring, whiteboard erasing, and tool use. We compare against end-to-end trajectory learning, showing that our decomposition enables robustness to workspace shifts and reuse across robot arms. We also show that the same primitive supports high-level specifications from generated videos, keyframes, human demonstrations, and hand-object interaction datasets. Finally, we conduct ablations on the primitive’s observation and reward design and the system’s planning modules.

## 2. Related Work 

High-level task interfaces. Foundation models and human demonstrations can specify robot manipula-tion tasks through language, visual constraints [24], code [31], or generated videos [43]. ManipGen [12] combines reusable local policies with foundation models and motion planning. Another line of work learns from human videos by extracting intermediate motion representations, such as point trajectories, object flow, or object-centric motion plans [4, 20, 29, 56, 58, 65]. SPOT [21] learns object-pose trajectory plans from human demonstrations and uses them to condition a robot policy. VLM-Scaffold [13] adapts a similar pipeline to dexterous hands but relies on task-specific residual RL, making it challenging to scale across tasks and embodiments. LUCID [18] learns embodiment-agnostic intent from unstructured human videos and connects it to embodiment-specific sensorimotor policies through object flow and a rough palm pose. In contrast, GOTT represents high-level intent using an object trajectory and a flexible reach specification, and connects these inputs to robot execution through a reusable dexterous contact-acquisition primitive. 

Trajectory-based dexterous manipulation. D-Grasp [11] combines reference-conditioned grasp control with global motion synthesis to move objects to target poses. Methods that start from a reference trajectory typically follow one of two strategies. The first performs trajectory-specific transfer, converting a human or object trajectory into robot motion through kinematic optimization, sampling [41], or reinforcement learning [30, 37]. Human2Sim2Robot [35] uses a single object trajectory and a pre-grasp to learn a task-specific robot policy. ManipTrans [30] and REGRIND [15] transfer hand-object interaction trajectories through residual learning, while SPIDER [41] performs fast physics-informed retargeting. These transfer methods can reproduce a given demonstration but are often tied to a specific trajectory or embodiment. The second strategy trains policies conditioned on multiple reference trajectories. ObjDex [10] and DexTrack [32] condition policies on object or hand trajectories. AdaDexTrack [1] adds online modulation to improve tracking of noisy references. OmniGrasp [36] learns to grasp diverse objects and follow object trajectories with a simulated humanoid. SimToolReal [26] and Dex4D [28] learn general object-centric policies conditioned on target object poses or point tracks, jointly handling contact acquisition and motion execution. Grasp to Act [17] combines human-guided grasp optimization with residual RL for online finger adaptation. In contrast, GOTT factors execution into reach, acquire, and move : high-level inputs specify the desired object motion and a task-relevant reach, while a trajectory-agnostic primitive establishes contact before trajectory tracking begins. This decomposition enables the same contact-acquisition primitive to be reused across trajectories, objects, and robot embodiments. 

Dexterous grasping. Existing learning-based methods develop generalizable tabletop grasping poli-cies [34, 50, 59, 63]. Grasp synthesis and evaluation methods instead generate or refine grasp tar-gets [7, 8, 14, 33, 51, 53]. GraspXL [62] and ConGraspXL [64] condition grasping motions on approach, contact-region, or pose constraints. Functional grasping methods use affordances, human grasp references, or language to select task-relevant grasps [2, 22, 52]. Cross-embodiment methods further share grasping policies or representations across hands: CrossDex [61] uses a unified observation space and retargeted eigengrasp actions, while OHRA [54] uses canonical hand representations and UniMorphGrasp [57] conditions grasp generation on morphology. GET-Zero [42] studies zero-shot transfer to unseen hand structures for in-hand rotation. These works advance grasping and cross-hand transfer, while primarily focusing on grasp acquisition itself. In contrast, GOTT treats contact acquisition as a reusable interme-diate primitive that refines an approximate, task-relevant reach into stable contact before downstream object-trajectory tracking. This enables the same primitive to be reused across objects, tasks, trajectories, and hand embodiments.

## 3. Dexterous Manipulation via General Object Trajectory Tracking 

We propose GOTT , a reach-acquire-move framework (Section 3.1) for executing rigid-object motions with dexterous hands. Its high-level interface consists of a desired object trajectory 𝜏 𝑜 and a reach specification 𝜌 that brings the hand near a task-relevant contact region. At its core, a reusable cross-embodiment contact-acquisition primitive grounds this interface by turning an approximate reach into stable contact (Section 3.2). In the object-centric setting, a future-aware planner constructs 𝜌 from the object mesh, desired trajectory, and task description (Section 3.3). Alternatively, external models or human demonstrations can provide 𝜌 (Section 3.4), while the primitive and tracking backend remain unchanged (Section 3.5). 

3.1. Reach-Acquire-Move Decomposition 

Given a desired object trajectory 𝜏 𝑜 = {𝑇 𝑂,𝑡 }𝑇 𝑡 =0 where 𝑇 𝑂,𝑡 denotes the object pose at timestep 𝑡 , and a reach specification 𝜌 = ( 𝜉 app , 𝑔 init ), GOTT decomposes dexterous execution into three stages. During 

reach , the robot follows the approach trajectory 𝜉 app to an approximate initial configuration 𝑔 init . During 

acquire , the contact-acquisition primitive uses closed-loop feedback to establish stable contact. During 

move , the pose-conditioned tracker moves the object along 𝜏 𝑜 .This decomposition separates high-level reasoning about functional contact selection, collision-free approach, and long-horizon reachability from local contact-rich control. Consequently, the high-level source needs to provide only a feasible approach to an approximate initial configuration, rather than an exactly executable final grasp. 

3.2. General-Purpose Contact Acquisition Primitive 

Establishing stable contact from an initial hand configuration near the object remains challenging. State-of-the-art grasp-generation methods either execute grasps open loop [7, 53], do not account for the functional suitability of the selected initial grasp [34, 63], or do not generalize across embodiments. We therefore train a local, closed-loop contact-acquisition policy using reinforcement learning in simulation and transfer it to the real world. The policy uses local hand-object geometry and proprioceptive history, while a canonical representation enables sharing across hands. This design allows the same primitive to be reused across tasks, high-level sources, objects, and hand embodiments. We first describe the single-hand control policy and then its extension to cross-embodiment control. 

Environment setup. We select 16 YCB objects [5] as our training set and collect several stable resting poses for each object. For each object–pose pair, we use BODex to generate 4,000 initial hand configurations of the type described in Section 3.3 and shown in Figure 2A. Each episode starts from one of these initial hand configurations. The policy must establish stable contact and lift the object. 

Observations and actions. The observation contains three components: (1) the link-distance represen-tation from [63], in which each hand link is represented by its displacement to the nearest point in the object point cloud; (2) hand-link positions; and (3) joint positions and previous joint targets. Components (2) and (3) are stacked over the five most recent timesteps. The policy outputs a 6D wrist-delta command and a vector of hand-joint deltas: 𝑎 𝑡 = 𝜋 𝜃 (𝑜 𝑡 ).

The four terms encourage link-object proximity, opposing contact between the thumb and another finger, lifting after contact, and object stability, respectively. Additional definitions and weights are provided in the appendix.

Unified cross-embodiment representation. For link-level observations, we map each hand onto a 21-keypoint topology consisting of one palm keypoint and four keypoints per finger. This representation allows one policy to operate across hands with different joint counts and kinematic structures without hand-specific encoder or decoder networks.

3.4. Reach Specifications from Human Guidance and External Models 

Section 3.3 constructs the reach specification 𝜌 from object-side information. Alternatively, human demonstrations or external models can supply contact regions, initial hand configurations, or approach trajectories directly, replacing the corresponding planning steps (Figure 4). The resulting reach specifica-tion is executed by the same contact-acquisition primitive and trajectory tracker. 

Contact regions or initial configurations. External models or human demonstrations can provide a task-relevant contact region or an initial hand configuration. Such a contact region replaces VLM-based affordance reasoning, while an initial configuration also bypasses candidate generation. The remaining planning steps construct a feasible approach and check downstream arm feasibility to form 𝜌 .

3.5. Execution from High-Level Specifications 

Regardless of the high-level source, the execution backend receives a desired object trajectory 𝜏 𝑜 and a reach specification 𝜌 . During real-world deployment, we use SAM2 [46] to segment the target object and FoundationPose [55] to estimate and track its pose. GOTT executes the task in three stages: reach, acquire, and move .

Reach. The robot follows the approach trajectory specified by 𝜌 . The system switches to the contact-acquisition primitive once the minimum distance between the hand links and the object surface falls below 𝜖 approach .

Contact acquisition. At this transition, the contact-acquisition primitive takes control of the wrist and fingers. It locally adjusts the hand to compensate for object pose errors, geometry mismatch, and contact uncertainty. During this phase, we run the proprioception-based contact predictor described in Section 3.2. Once the predictor detects stable contact, the system proceeds to the move stage.

4.1. Is It a Reliable General-Purpose Contact Primitive? 

We isolate the low-level contact-acquisition primitive by evaluating object lifting in simulation and the real world, without full-arm planning. 

4.1.1. Simulation grasping evaluation 

We use a floating-hand tabletop setup and evaluate on DexGraspNet objects [51], following the pre-processing protocol of BODex [7]. After filtering out flat objects and rescaling the remaining objects to four sizes, the evaluation set contains 9,588 assets and 54,149 object-scale-pose instances. We run simulations in IsaacLab with the friction coefficient set to 1.5 and the object mass set to 100 g. All physics parameters are fixed across evaluations. 

Baselines. We compare against two representative grasp synthesis methods. BODex [7] optimizes grasp poses for each robot-object pair using bilevel optimization. DRO [53] is a cross-embodiment grasp prediction network, for which we use the authors’ released checkpoints. Since both methods output grasp targets rather than closed-loop acquisition policies, we execute their outputs open-loop using their original execution controllers. 

Evaluation protocol. For each object-scale-pose instance, we generate 20 initial poses using BODex’s first stage (Section 3.3) and share them across methods. BODex refines them via bilevel optimization, DRO uses them to specify initial approach directions, and our policy starts directly from them for closed-loop contact acquisition. In the same tabletop setup, BODex and DRO execute open-loop from predicted pre-grasps, effectively assuming successful motion planning to these poses. Our method additionally handles the approach from the raw initial poses. 

Metrics. We report lifting success rates across hand embodiments, objects, and initial object poses. A trial is successful if the hand establishes contact, lifts the object by at least 5 cm, and keeps it stable for the full evaluation horizon. 

Results. The results are shown in Table 1. GOTT consistently outperforms BODex and DRO in lifting success across all three hands. Notably, although the policy is trained on only 16 YCB objects, it generalizes to the full evaluation set of 9,588 DexGraspNet assets. The success rates for BODex and DRO are lower than those reported in their original papers due to our stricter tabletop protocol. Prior work initializes objects without gravity and applies gravity only after contact is established, whereas we place objects on a table with gravity enabled from the start and execute directly. Appendix E details the baseline implementations and evaluation protocols and presents controlled comparisons. 

4.1.2. Real-world grasping experiments 

We evaluate the contact-acquisition primitive using the same checkpoint on three arm-hand platforms: Franka-Sharpa, Franka-XHand, and Tianji-XHand. This tests both cross-hand generalization and transfer across arm platforms through the end-effector control interface. The evaluation setup is shown in Figure 5. We run 5 trials on each of 10 objects, for 50 rollouts per platform. A trial is successful if the hand establishes contact, lifts the object by at least 5 cm, and keeps it stable for at least 3 seconds. GOTT 

achieves success rates of 90%, 90%, and 96% on Franka-Sharpa, Franka-XHand, and Tianji-XHand, respectively. The slightly lower success rates on Franka are mainly due to the more constrained reachable workspace in our Franka setup.

4.2. Does It Generalize across and beyond Training Hands? 

4.2.1. Cross-hand evaluation and representation comparison 

The lower portion of Table 1 compares GOTT with CrossDex [61], an RL baseline that uses a shared action space through human-hand retargeting. To isolate the effect of the observation–action representation, we reimplement CrossDex within our framework. We adopt the observation and action design from its released code while keeping the reward, PPO hyperparameters, and training and evaluation protocol the same as those used for GOTT . Specialist policies are trained separately for each hand, while unified policies are trained jointly on XHand, Sharpa, and Allegro. Our unified policy with the per-link canonical representation achieves 78.7% average success versus 51.7% for CrossDex. Joint training also improves average success over our specialist policies, supporting positive transfer across morphologies. 

4.2.2. Zero-shot transfer to unseen Allegro variants 

To further probe cross-embodiment generalization, we test unseen Allegro variants generated by removing finger links. We evaluate our three-hand unified policy, trained on XHand, Sharpa, and the original Allegro hand, zero-shot on these variants. We compare it with (i) per-variant oracle policies trained directly on each variant and (ii) a policy trained only on the original Allegro hand. As shown in Table 2, our three-hand policy averages 72.1% success across the three variants, matching the average of the per-variant oracle policies (72.2%), whereas the Allegro-only policy achieves 17.2%. Transfer therefore comes from multi-morphology training, not the canonical representation alone. Figure 6 further shows that the policy produces coordinated grasp configurations on these unseen variants. 

4.3. Does the Primitive Improve End-to-End Task Success? 

We evaluate whether the contact-acquisition primitive improves end-to-end execution on four real-world manipulation tasks using the Franka-Sharpa platform. For each task, the robot must approach the object, acquire stable contact, and move the object along the specified trajectory. We compare GOTT with two matched open-loop variants. Each replaces our region-conditioned candidate generation with the full grasp generation procedure of BODex [7] or DRO [53] and executes the predicted grasp open-loop. All other components, including perception, VLM-based affordance reasoning, future-aware IK selection, and trajectory tracking, are kept fixed, isolating the contribution of closed-loop contact acquisition. 

Real-world tasks. We evaluate Trash Cleanup (grasp a brush and sweep trash), Mustard Pouring (grasp a mustard bottle and rotate it to a pouring orientation), Whiteboard Erasing (grasp an eraser and sweep across the board), and Drill Use (grasp a power drill and rotate it to a working orientation), as illustrated in Figure 7. These tasks require both translation and rotation, and some require functional contacts, such as grasping the drill near its handle. Each task is repeated 10 times across varied initial poses.

4.6.1. Contact primitive design 

We vary one observation or reward setting at a time, holding all others fixed (Table 4). Removing link-position observations degrades success across hands. Reducing the proprioceptive history to one timestep removes temporal contact cues, lowering average success by 15.7 percentage points. We also compare with an OHRA-style [54] observation representation. In its original form, OHRA encodes only the object point cloud with a heavyweight point-cloud encoder, concatenates the resulting feature with a morphology VAE embedding, and uses the combined representation to predict grasps offline. This encoder is too large to train jointly with PPO at our training scale, so we adapt the representation to RL by (i) replacing the encoder with a lighter PointNet and (ii) adding the hand point cloud as input, while preserving OHRA’s morphology VAE conditioning. Even after this adaptation, average success drops to 25.7%, suggesting that per-link contact geometry provides a better representation for a closed-loop primitive. Removing either 𝑟 contact or 𝑟 lift reduces average success to below 2%, confirming their necessity. While 

𝑟 dist modestly improves lifting on training hands on average, it suppresses finger inversion and distorted joint bending (Figure 6). Under morphology shift, removing this regularization lowers average success on three unseen Allegro variants from 72.1% to 55.1%.

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented GOTT , a reach-acquire-move framework for dexterous manipulation from object trajectories and flexible reach specifications. At its core, a cross-embodiment contact-acquisition primitive establishes stable contact from approximate reach specifications, decoupling local contact control from arm-specific reaching and downstream tracking. Simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate the primitive’s reuse across objects, embodiments, tasks, workspaces, and high-level input sources. 

Limitations and future work. First, high-level planning and contact acquisition operate sequentially. Tighter feedback between them could enable online recovery from poor reach specifications. Second, trajectory tracking relies on visual object pose estimation, which can fail under severe hand-object occlusion. Incorporating tactile sensing or visuotactile state estimation is a natural next step. Finally, we treat the hand-object transform as approximately fixed after grasping. Extending GOTT to handle changes in this transform would enable more precise in-hand manipulation tasks, such as unscrewing.

A.4. Policy Action Action space. The action is 𝑎 = [ 𝑎 EE (6) , 𝑎 hand (𝐽 can ) ] , i.e. 6+ 26 = 32 dimensions. Range, scaling, and clipping. Raw policy actions are clipped to [−1, 1] before being rescaled into physical commands. For the hand, per-joint deltas are rescaled using each joint’s upper and lower limits so that an action of ±1 corresponds to a full-range increment. The end-effector deltas are scaled to ±0.008 m per step for translation and 

±0.052 rad per step for rotation, then composed with the previous target pose. 

Control frequency. Simulation uses a timestep of dt = 1 /120 s. With decimation = 12 , each action is held for 12 physics substeps, giving a control rate of 10 Hz.

## B. Future-Aware Planning Details 

At deployment, the planner identifies a task-relevant contact region and then generates candidate initial-izations within this region. A vision-language model (VLM) provides the task-aware affordance selection, while candidate generation and trajectory selection are performed geometrically and kinematically. 

Task-aware region selection. We render the object at its observed pose on the table from four side views (Figure 12 (a)). The views use textured rendering with a low camera elevation and are overlaid with a light pixel grid for image-coordinate reference. The VLM is prompted to identify the object’s functional parts, mark each visible part in each view, and designate the single part to be grasped for the specified task (Figure 12 (b)). For the designated part, the VLM additionally predicts multiple points spanning its extent. All predicted points are back-projected onto the object mesh by ray casting. For each functional part, its annotations across views are clustered in the object frame, and the mean of the largest cluster is used as the part location. Predicted grasp points whose back-projected locations are inconsistent with the designated part are discarded. We define the task-relevant contact region as the union of 3 cm surface neighborhoods around the remaining grasp points (Figure 12 (c)). 

Candidate generation. Following the initialization stage of BODex [7], we sample candidate hand poses and joint configurations from surface points within the selected contact region. We include the table in the collision world and reject candidate initializations that collide with the table or other scene geometry, as well as ill-posed poses. We retain 𝑁 = 10 valid candidate initializations by default. These candidates serve as approximate initial configurations for the contact-acquisition primitive rather than final grasps. 

Trajectory expansion and selection. For each remaining candidate, we plan a collision-free approach from the robot’s initial configuration to the initial grasp pose with cuRobo [49]. We then assume a rigid hand-object attachment and compute the hand trajectory along the desired object motion 𝜏 𝑜 , solving IK along this trajectory to recover the full arm-hand motion. Candidates whose approach or tracking

E.1. Protocol Comparison with BODex 

BODex’s original evaluation places each scaled object in a floating state without a table . Ten grasps are synthesized per (object, scale, pose) instance. The hand is initialized at the pre-grasp pose x𝑝 and driven to the squeezed pose x𝑠 . Gravity is then applied along the positive and negative directions of each of the three coordinate axes. A grasp counts as successful only if the object’s translation and rotation deviations remain within 5 cm and 15 ∘ for at least 3 s under all six directions. In contrast, our protocol places objects on a tabletop under gravity from the start of the trial. Both seed-pose initialization (used identically across all methods) and grasp execution must avoid hand-table collisions throughout the trial. This setting is closer to real-world deployment, where objects rest on a workspace surface and the robot must operate within the resulting constraints. In summary, the two protocols differ in two respects: • Generation phase. BODex synthesizes grasps without considering a table. Our setup adds hand-table collision constraints to both initial-pose sampling and the bilevel optimization. • Evaluation phase. BODex evaluates grasps in a floating-hand state with gravity applied after squeezing along all six directions. Our setup places the object on a table under −𝑧 gravity from the start of the trial.

E.4. Protocol Comparison with DRO 

DRO [53] evaluates its grasps in Isaac Gym, where a controller closes the hand around the object. The grasp is then perturbed by sequential forces along the positive and negative directions of each coordinate axis, with a magnitude corresponding to an acceleration of 0.5 m /s2 (i.e., 𝐹 = 𝑚 obj (0 .5 m /s2)). A grasp is successful if the object’s displacement stays below 2 cm. Our protocol differs in two ways. First, we require the grasp to lift the object against gravity rather than resist a brief perturbation. The gravitational load 𝐹 𝑔 = 𝑚 obj (9 .8 m /s2) is roughly 20 × the perturbation force 𝐹 used in DRO’s protocol and is sustained throughout the trial. Second, our protocol places the object on a table from the start, whereas DRO’s grasp synthesis does not account for table collisions, which limits its performance under our protocol. 

E.5. DRO Baseline Variants 

Our main table (Table 1) uses DRO’s [53] released checkpoint. DRO is trained across embodiments and transfers zero-shot to new hands given their URDFs, but XHand and Sharpa Wave are not part of its training set. For a fair comparison, we additionally fine-tune or retrain DRO on our BODex-generated YCB grasp set. Specifically, we run BODex’s tabletop-aware bilevel optimization from the same initial hand configurations used by GOTT and keep the 4,000 grasps that pass BODex’s own validity check as the training set. All variants are evaluated under our tabletop protocol (Table 13). All fine-tuning and retraining variants underperform the released checkpoint on average across hands. We attribute this to our smaller 16-object training set with tabletop-constrained grasps, which differs from DRO’s original training distribution. We therefore keep the released checkpoint in the main table as the strongest DRO variant overall. Notably, even on the natively supported Allegro hand, DRO reaches 43.2% versus our 87.1%, indicating that the gap primarily reflects the benefit of closed-loop contact acquisition over open-loop grasp execution under the same tabletop protocol. Evaluation protocol. At each control step, is_grasping is set according to the per-finger contact predictions. We compare this signal against the simulator’s ground-truth grasp state. A false positive is a step at which is_grasping is true while the simulator indicates no grasp, and a false negative is the reverse. At the episode level, a switch is considered correct if the predictor triggers tracking while the hand is actually holding the object. 

Results. At a contact-probability threshold of 𝜏 𝑐 = 0 .5, the per-step false-positive rate is 0.066–0.125 and the false-negative rate is 0.036–0.047 across the three hands. Per-episode switch accuracy is 0.953–0.960, with a median lag of at most one step relative to the simulator’s switch. Switch decisions are insensitive to the threshold: switch accuracy remains 0.94–0.98 for 𝜏 𝑐 ∈ [0 .4, 0.8] and degrades only for 𝜏 𝑐 ≤ 0.3.

Robustness to observation noise. We train a separate proprioception-only predictor because it is more robust to observation noise than a contact head coupled to the policy. To verify this, we compare against two alternatives: a head inside the policy network trained jointly with PPO from the full policy observation, and per-embodiment proprioceptive predictors trained alongside the policy instead of after it. All three are evaluated offline on the same recorded rollouts using their respective inputs and the same ground-truth labels.
