# AgenticTactileVLA: Contact-Guided Execution-Time Supervision for Generalizable Dexterous Manipulation without VLA Retraining 

Elizaveta Semenyakina 1,2, Ivan Snegirev 1, Mikhail Kiselev 1, Miguel Altamirano Cabrera 1,2,Artem Lykov 1,2, Hajira Amjad 1, and Dzmitry Tsetserukou 1

Abstract — Vision-language-action policies may predict atransferable manipulation strategy yet fail to realize it reliably on the encountered object: objects compatible with the same grasp differ in geometry and compliance, and visual feedback degrades under closure occlusion. AgenticTactileVLA is presented as an execution-time supervisor that shifts part of object-specific adaptation from prediction to physical interaction. A fixed VLA provides the approach and hand targets; the supervisor decides whether to remain transparent, refine finger flexion, retain or release the corrected configuration, return control to the VLA for retry, or select a compliant hand-control regime. It uses finger-position and motor-effort feedback as proprioceptive contact evidence and requires neither tactile sensors nor VLA retraining. On a Unitree G1 with a BrainCo Revo2 hand, a randomized matched-block evaluation on five objects held out from VLA training yields 61.3% completion for the base VLA, 72.0% for unconditional close-to-stall control, and 84.0% for the supervisor under a shared budget; the gain is positive on every object and persists under moderate pose perturbations. Ablations show the gain is not explained by extended closure alone, and that selective triggering reduces correction episodes by 65.7% with no detected change in completion. A retention audit shows acceptance predicts retention in 88.9% of held-out cases, while compliant objects expose conservative false rejection. A thin-walled-cup study demonstrates contextual routing to compliant control, matching an always-compliant reference. These results suggest that contact-guided execution-time adaptation can improve the object-level generalization of a fixed VLA to held-out objects by adapting physical realization without object-specific retraining. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A central challenge in robot learning is to generalize ma-nipulation policies to new objects while maintaining reliable physical execution. For vision-language-action (VLA) models, this is particularly important in contact-rich manipulation. Objects compatible with the same broad grasp strategy may differ in geometry and compliance, changing the finger contacts and interaction conditions required for successful execution. A VLA may therefore generate a suitable approach yet still leave fingers without sufficient support, produce an unsuitable terminal hand configuration, or over-compress a fragile object. Such failures are difficult to resolve visually during closure, when the hand increasingly occludes the contact region. Many objects can be handled using a limited repertoire of broad grasp strategies [1], [2], while their physical realization

This motivates reusing a fixed policy while adapting these physical details during execution rather than through object-specific retraining. We investigate a division of responsibility in which the VLA provides the transferable manipulation strategy, while an execution-time supervisor adapts its physical realization to the encountered object (Fig. 1). The supervisor can modify finger flexion and gate progression of the VLA-generated body reference, but does not replace the VLA approach policy or learn a residual action model. Prior work has explored tactile-conditioned VLA adaptation [3], [4] and execution monitoring and recovery [5], [6]. Our work lies at their intersection and asks: Can execution-time physical adaptation improve the realization of a fixed VLA policy on objects outside its training set without object-specific retraining? 

AgenticTactileVLA augments a fixed VLA with a stateful execution supervisor. At the terminal phase of nominal closure, it evaluates proprioceptive support evidence. If support is sufficient, execution remains transparent to the policy. Otherwise, body-reference progression is paused while whole-body stabilization remains active, and unsupported fingers are corrected independently. An accepted correction is retained; a rejected correction releases the hand override and returns control to the VLA for subsequent policy-generated retry. A key principle is selective intervention : the supervisor acts only when the policy-proposed closure is not sufficiently arXiv:2610.04391v1 [cs.RO] 3 Oct 2026 realized physically. To separate this effect from simply closing farther or longer, we compare against unconditional close-to-stall control under the same execution budget and use component ablations to examine selectivity, effort-assisted stopping, and verdict-dependent release. Geometric correction alone is insufficient when the inter-action regime must also change. Fragile objects may deform before proprioceptive resistance is large enough for reactive correction. AgenticTactileVLA therefore also selects between nominal position control and a predefined compliant mode before closure. Explicit handling cues can request compliant interaction, while a vision-language model (VLM) can select between the two modes from image and task context. The VLM selects only the interaction mode and does not generate controller parameters or material estimates. We use the term agentic to denote this bounded, stateful supervision: deciding whether to remain transparent or intervene, adapting individual finger references, accepting or releasing the corrected grasp, returning control to the VLA for retry, and selecting the interaction regime. This distinguishes the layer from low-level reflexes by enabling high-level decision-making based on multimodal context. The framework uses finger position and motor-effort signals as device-specific proprioceptive evidence and requires neither additional tactile sensors nor object-specific VLA retraining. The main contributions of this work are: 1) A contact-guided execution-time supervisor for a fixed VLA, combining selective intervention, bounded per-finger correction, grasp acceptance or release, and policy-mediated retry. 2) A proprioceptive execution mechanism using finger position and motor-effort feedback without additional tactile hardware, together with language- or vision-conditioned selection between nominal and compliant interaction modes. 3) Real-hardware evaluation on a Unitree G1 with a BrainCo Revo2 hand under matched execution budgets, including unconditional close-to-stall control, compo-nent ablations, pose perturbations, retention diagnostics, and fragile-object interaction tests. The intended scope is adaptation of feasible policy-generated manipulation strategies, not recovery from arbitrary incompatible approach errors. Within this scope, we view execution-time physical adaptation as a step toward more scalable object-level generalization of fixed VLA policies, re-ducing the need for object-specific retraining while preserving the policy’s reusable manipulation strategy. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Contact Sensing from Motor Feedback 

Motor feedback can reveal physical robot–object interaction without dedicated tactile sensors. Current as Touch [7], Transferring Contact, Not Just Motion [8], and Pseudo-Tactile [9] use actuator or gripper signals for contact-aware manipulation. AgenticTactileVLA uses finger positions and motor-effort signals as device-specific evidence of interaction resistance rather than calibrated contact force. This evidence determines whether the nominal closure provides sufficient support and, when necessary, guides local finger correction and grasp acceptance or release. 

B. Tactile Feedback for VLA Adaptation 

Tactile feedback complements vision when physical inter-action is difficult to infer from images alone. VLA-Touch [3] combines a frozen VLA with semantic tactile feedback and learned action refinement. TLA [10] and VTLA [11] incorporate tactile observations into action generation, while UniTacVLA [4] predicts contact changes to refine action chunks. RoboTacDex [12] provides multimodal data for visual–tactile dexterous humanoid manipulation. In contrast, AgenticTactileVLA does not train a tactile-conditioned VLA or a learned residual policy. Adaptation is placed in an external, nonlearned execution layer while the VLA remains fixed. The supervisor stays transparent when the policy-generated closure already provides sufficient support and intervenes only when physical correction is required. 

C. Execution Supervision and Interaction Control 

Execution supervision connects predicted actions with observed consequences. PLanAR [5] verifies expected effects and replans after deviations, VoLo [6] combines monitoring with recovery, and VLAs Fail Differently [13] analyzes failure structure in motor-command sequences. This work brings supervision to the closure-to-motion transition in dexterous manipulation. The supervisor deter-mines whether the physical realization of the VLA action is sufficient, whether individual fingers should be corrected, whether the resulting grasp should be retained or released, and whether control should return to the VLA for another approach. A separate interaction-mode layer selects between nominal position control and current-limited compliant control before closure. AgenticTactileVLA thus combines selective intervention, contact-guided finger adaptation, grasp acceptance or release, policy-mediated retry, and contextual interaction-mode selec-tion within a single execution-time layer over a fixed VLA policy. Object-Adaptive Finger Correction and Acceptance 

Once intervention transfers execution to the supervisor, each unsupported finger advances independently toward its permitted travel limit. When support is detected, the reference is fixed at the measured position plus a normalized preload of 0.02 , clipped to the travel limit; further advancement of that finger stops. Correction starts from measured positions and advances in increments of 0.004 every 20 ms. Independent stopping transforms the VLA-proposed termi-nal hand configuration into object-dependent finger references: different fingers may stop at different depths and times, while the policy-selected thumb-opposition target preserves the overall grasp arrangement. Reaching the travel limit without detector activation does not establish contact, because insufficient remaining motion may prevent the history and progress requirements of (6) from being satisfied. Adjustment lasts at least 0.6 s and at most 4.0 s. E. Language- and Vision-Conditioned Mode Selection 

Interaction-mode selection chooses between nominal po-sition control and compliant control. Explicit handling cues such as “gently”, “carefully”, or “fragile” activate compliant mode. When no explicit cue is present, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [16] selects between the two predefined modes from image and task context. The VLM runs asynchronously while the hand is open, with one decision cached per grasp episode. Language cues take priority; missing, late, or malformed VLM responses default to nominal control. The VLM selects only the interaction mode and does not estimate material properties or generate controller parameters. Selection is performed before closure because a fragile object may deform before sufficient proprioceptive resistance can be detected. To isolate mode selection in Section IV-F, the VLA receives the same task instruction in every condition. Handling cues are provided only to the interaction parser, while the VLM receives a fixed image-and-task prompt without those cues.

E XPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

We evaluate AgenticTactileVLA through a sequence of controlled experiments. The evaluation first establishes the task-level benefit of execution-time supervision, then separates this effect from unconditional finger closure. We further examine sensitivity to initial-pose perturbations, the physical meaning and failure modes of proprioceptive verification, and contextual selection of the interaction regime for fragile objects. 

A. Experimental Protocol 

All experiments use the same fixed VLA checkpoint. The five held-out test objects were excluded from VLA training, detector calibration, and controller development. A trial starts from an initialized scene and may contain multiple closures. A retry requires policy-commanded reopen-ing followed by a new closure. Task completion is recorded independently of the supervisor. The primary comparison uses randomized, interleaved matched blocks under a shared execution budget. Within each block, all conditions start from a common initial configuration. A trial terminates at successful placement, 60 s, or three arm approaches, whichever occurs first. No started trial is excluded. Paired task-level comparisons use two-sided exact McNe-mar tests with Holm correction over all pairwise contrasts reported within each experiment.

The primary experiment compares the base VLA, uncon-ditional close-to-stall control, and the full position-branch supervisor over 75 matched blocks (15 per held-out object). At each terminal grasp, this baseline pauses body-reference progression and advances finger flexion until the position-stall condition in (6), the travel limit, or the shared 4.0 s timeout. C. Component Ablation 

To determine which components of the execution supervisor drive the observed improvement, we conduct a component ablation over 100 matched blocks. The analysis examines whether the gain arises simply from allowing the fingers to close farther, whether selective intervention reduces unnec-essary corrections, and whether effort evidence and verdict-dependent release provide additional benefit. We evaluate five variants. Here, U denotes unconditional intervention at every closure, while S denotes selective intervention only when the supervisor detects insufficient support. U-stall simply extends every closure until position-based stall and serves as the additional-closure baseline. U-full applies the complete contact detector and grasp-verdict logic at every closure, testing whether structured supervision provides benefit even without selectivity. Pose-Perturbation Evaluation 

The previous experiments use matched initial configu-rations. We therefore test whether the supervisor’s benefit disappears when the initial object pose is perturbed. The same five objects are evaluated under nominal poses, horizontal translations within 20 mm, and yaw changes within 15 ◦, with 50 matched trials per regime and condition. Performance decreases under perturbation for all methods, but the supervisor remains the strongest condition in every regime. Its gain over the base VLA is 22, 28, and 28 percentage points for nominal, translated, and rotated configu-rations, respectively. E. Proprioceptive Verification and Failure Modes 

The previous results show that the supervisor improves task completion, but they do not establish whether its internal support decisions correspond to a meaningful physical state. We therefore examine the contact-support mechanism separately from task success. Across 70 diagnostic trials, the supervisor produces 85 correction events: 46 F ORMED verdicts, 26 C ROOKED ver-dicts, and 13 E MPTY verdicts. These internal decisions are not treated as contact-detection accuracy because independent per-finger contact labels are unavailable. Contact patterns vary by object (Fig. 3). In particular, highly compliant objects expose a characteristic limitation of proprioceptive contact inference.

There, retention given acceptance is 88.9%, while 25.6% of retainable configurations are falsely rejected. The support rule is therefore useful but conservative: acceptance provides strong evidence of a physically useful grasp, whereas rejection is less reliable, particularly for compliant or previously unseen objects. 

F. Contextual Interaction-Mode Selection 

The previous experiments adapt grasp realization during or after contact. Fragile objects pose a different challenge: damage may occur before sufficient resistance is observed. We therefore test whether the agent can select an alternative interaction regime before closure. A thin-walled cup is evaluated under position control, instruction-based routing, VLM-based routing, combined instruction–VLM routing, and always-compliant control (Ta-ble VI). Nominal position control damages all five cups, whereas instruction-based and combined routing preserve 5/5. VLM-only routing preserves 4/5; in the remaining trial, the correct compliant mode is selected after closure begins (Fig. 4(a)), showing that correct semantic selection must also arrive in time to affect the interaction. The experiments show that a fixed VLA benefits from execution-time adaptation. The supervisor improves comple-tion over both the fixed VLA and unconditional close-to-stall control, with gains on every held-out object. Additional closure explains only part of the improvement, while the ablation supports structured supervision over position-only stall control. Selective intervention reduces correction episodes by 65.7% relative to the always-on controller without detected com-pletion loss, and the benefit persists under moderate pose variation. The retention audit shows that accepted configurations retain the object in 70/76 cases (92.1% pooled; 88.9% on held-out objects), while 18/44 rejected configurations would still retain it. The support predicate is useful but conservative, especially for compliant objects. False rejections can still be recovered through policy-mediated retry. Interaction-mode selection extends adaptation before con-tact. Instruction-based and combined routing preserve 5/5 fragile cups, while VLM-only routing preserves 4/5 due to late mode selection. On rigid objects, the intended nominal mode is selected in 14/15 trials. Limitations remain. The ablation does not isolate the contri-butions of effort evidence and verdict-dependent release, and the lack of a detected difference between selective and always-on supervision does not imply equivalence. The supervisor relies on proprioceptive signals rather than calibrated force and cannot recover from arbitrary approach errors. The fragile-object study uses a training object, and compliant control is evaluated only for static grasping. Validation requires unseen objects and diverse embodiments. Overall, the results support execution-time adaptation as a complement to a reusable fixed VLA. VI. C ONCLUSION 

AgenticTactileVLA introduces contact-guided execution-time supervision for a fixed VLA. Rather than retraining for each object, it preserves the predicted strategy while adapting its physical realization through selective intervention, per-finger correction, grasp acceptance or policy-mediated retry, and interaction-mode selection. On five held-out objects, the supervisor achieves 84.0% task completion versus 61.3% for the base VLA and 72.0% for unconditional close-to-stall control. The benefit persists under pose perturbations, while selective supervision reduces interventions and accepted configurations provide support. Fragile-object experiments extend adaptation to pre-contact mode selection. These results support execution-time adaptation toward more scalable object-level generalization of fixed VLA policies without object-specific retraining or additional tactile hardware.
