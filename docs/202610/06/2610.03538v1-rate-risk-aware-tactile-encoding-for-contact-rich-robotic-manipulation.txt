# RATE: Risk-Aware Tactile Encoding for Contact-rich Robotic Manipulation 

Yuyao Jiang, Haichao Liu, Jiarui Zheng, Zihan Ding, Weihao Yuan, and Ziwei Wang 

Abstract — Tactile sensing is particularly valuable for contact-rich robotic manipulation. Recent work has made substantial progress in tactile representation learning for robotic manip-ulation. However, similar tactile observations can arise from interaction conditions with very different task-risk implica-tions, such as sensor noise, task-necessary variations, and emerging undesirable contact. Without context-grounded risk information, these cases can be ambiguous to downstream policies, leading to unnecessary corrections to benign vari-ations or delayed responses to genuinely risky contact. To address this limitation, we propose Risk-Aware Tactile Encoding (RATE), which learns tactile representations that encode task-conditioned interaction risk. Specifically, history-conditioned prediction captures interaction context, while alert supervision associates this context with task-conditioned risk. The learned risk-aware representation complements conventional tactile fea-tures through a lightweight residual adapter. Experiments in both simulation and the real world demonstrate substantial improvements in task success, with controlled ablations con-firming the complementary benefits of alert-guided learning and predictive temporal modeling. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humans naturally combine visual and tactile information during contact-rich manipulation. Vision provides object- and scene-level information, while touch provides local infor-mation about contact, force, friction, and slip [1]. Recent visuo-tactile research has demonstrated the value of tactile feedback for manipulation, particularly in tasks such as insertion and extraction where subtle interaction states are difficult to infer from vision alone [2]–[4]. At the same time, advances in tactile representation learning, temporal model-ing, and contact-aware manipulation have further expanded how tactile information can be learned and used in robotic manipulation [2], [4]–[13], [14]. However, despite these advances, existing tactile repre-sentations still provide limited context-grounded information about task risk. In contact-rich manipulation, sensor noise, task-necessary adjustments, and early signs of undesirable contact can produce similar tactile observations despite cor-responding to very different levels of task risk. If such risk information is not adequately captured in the repre-sentation, downstream policies may lack sufficient evidence to determine when corrective action is needed, leading to unnecessary corrections or responses that come too late.

To address this limitation, we propose Risk-Aware Tac-tile Encoding (RATE) , a tactile representation learning framework that augments conventional tactile representations with a complementary risk-aware representation. History-conditioned predictive pretraining provides contextual cues about interaction evolution by learning from tactile history and future prediction targets, while alert supervision asso-ciates these cues with task-conditioned risk. During policy adaptation, the learned risk-aware representation comple-ments conventional tactile features through a zero-initialized residual pathway, while alert-weighted action imitation em-phasizes task-critical steps. Only the residual pathway is updated, with the pretrained tactile encoders and policy backbone kept frozen, resulting in updates to approximately 

0.59% of the complete model parameters. Alert targets and future observations are used only during training and are not required during deployment. In this section, we review two lines of prior work. Sec-tion II-A focuses on visuo-tactile representation learning for contact-rich manipulation, while Section II-B discusses temporal tactile modeling and contact-aware manipulation. 

A. Visuo-Tactile Representation Learning for Contact-Rich Manipulation 

Recent robotic manipulation research has advanced both dexterous task planning and motion control [15] and general-purpose gripper-based manipulation [16]. Visuo-tactile rep-resentation learning has evolved from simple multimodal fusion toward more structured and transferable representa-tions. One line of work focuses on cross-modal learning, using contrastive objectives or shared latent spaces to capture complementary information between vision and touch [2], [17]. Another line introduces stronger structural priors, such as shared 3D representations and implicit contact geometry, to preserve spatial relationships among the robot, object, and contact regions [18], [19]. In parallel, large-scale simulation and tactile-rich demonstrations have improved the scalability of representation learning and its transfer to downstream manipulation tasks [4], [20]. Together, these approaches substantially improve the rep-resentation of contact appearance, geometry, and multimodal structure. However, their learning objectives are not explicitly designed to organize tactile information according to task-conditioned risk in the context of an evolving interaction. 

B. Temporal Tactile Modeling and Contact-Aware Manipu-lation 

Recent work has extended tactile modeling beyond in-stantaneous observations by incorporating temporal informa-tion. One class of methods uses recurrent or spatiotemporal models to capture contact evolution from tactile sequences and predict contact events [14]. Another class adopts pre-dictive representation learning, conditioning on current or recently observed tactile information to predict future tactile observations or tactile changes, thereby encouraging the learned representation to capture how interaction evolves over time [10], [13]. These studies demonstrate that tactile history and future-prediction objectives provide useful tem-poral information for modeling interaction dynamics. Tactile and force feedback has also been used for real-time adaptation in dexterous teleoperation [21]. Reactive approaches use high-frequency tactile feedback for online ac-tion correction [8], [11], while predictive approaches model future robot–contact trajectories or visuo-tactile dynamics to guide action generation and closed-loop refinement [9], [12]. These methods improve policy responsiveness to changing contact conditions, but temporal information is primarily used for dynamics prediction or action correction. A complementary line of work focuses on contact-aware manipulation, where tactile or contact feedback is used to assess or constrain undesirable interactions. Existing ap-proaches consider grasp-outcome prediction, collision detec-tion, contact-force regulation, and trajectory refinement [5]– [7]. These studies demonstrate the value of risk-related contact information for manipulation decisions, but such signals are typically used at the level of event prediction, control constraints, or policy optimization rather than to directly shape the tactile representation itself. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

This section presents the proposed Risk-Aware Tactile Encoding (RATE) framework. Section III-A formulates the problem and outlines the overall framework. Section III-B describes the learning of the risk-aware tactile representation, and Section III-C details its lightweight residual integration into a pretrained manipulation policy. 

A. Problem Formulation and Framework Overview 

Reliable contact-rich manipulation requires tactile repre-sentations that explicitly characterize task-conditioned risk, while existing tactile representations remain limited in this regard. We therefore formulate the problem as learning a complementary risk-aware tactile representation zrt from recent tactile interaction, using only observations available up to time t during deployment. Rather than replacing the existing tactile representation, zrt augments it with risk-related information, enabling lightweight adaptation of a pretrained manipulation policy. Figure 2 summarizes the overall RATE representation-learning and policy-adaptation pipeline. To learn this representation, RATE uses tactile history through short-horizon future prediction together with continuous task-conditioned alert supervision rt ∈ [0 , 1] . Alert targets are automatically derived from task-relevant interaction signals available in each domain and normalized to a common 

[0 , 1] scale. Higher alert values correspond to increasingly undesirable contact conditions. Through this joint supervi-sion, the learned representation is encouraged to distinguish sensor noise and task-necessary variations from emerging undesirable contact conditions. B. Learning Risk-Aware Tactile Representations 

In contact-rich manipulation, task risk depends on both the current contact state and how that state evolves over time. RATE therefore constructs interaction context from the bilateral contact configuration, local temporal changes, and their accumulation over a short tactile history, while continuous task-conditioned alert supervision simultaneously organizes these evolving cues according to their relevance to task risk. This allows interaction states with similar tactile appearance but different risk implications to become distinguishable in the learned representation. As shown in Stage II of Fig. 2, these complementary cues are jointly encoded into the risk-aware representation zrt .At each sampled time step k, the left and right tactile observations are processed by a shared tactile backbone to obtain f Lk = Er (τ Lk ) and f Rk = Er (τ Rk ), which are then fused as ek = ψ([ f Lk , f Rk ]) . Because task risk arises from the overall contact interaction rather than either tactile sensor in isolation, ek provides a joint description of the bilateral contact configuration. The contact configuration alone does not explicitly reveal how the interaction is evolving. We therefore compute the local feature transition ∆ek = ek − ek−1 and jointly encode the contact state and its temporal change as uk =

ϕ([ ek, ∆ek]) . Here, ek specifies the contact configuration in which a change occurs, while ∆ek captures the direction and magnitude of the local transition in feature space. Their combination is important because similar local changes can have different implications under different contact configura-tions, while similar contact states can evolve toward different outcomes depending on how they are changing. Risk-relevant interaction patterns may persist or accumu-late across multiple local transitions rather than appearing in a single adjacent-frame difference. We therefore aggregate the sequence {uk}Hk=1 over the recent interaction history, so that the resulting representation captures both the evolving contact state and the temporal progression of the interac-tion. C. Lightweight Residual Integration into the Manipulation Policy 

As illustrated in the policy-adaptation stage of Fig. 2, the learned risk-aware representation is integrated into the pretrained manipulation policy as a lightweight residual cor-rection to conventional tactile features. Rather than replacing the original tactile representation, this design preserves the contact information already captured by the pretrained tactile encoder and introduces the additional context-grounded risk information learned by RATE. For each tactile sensor h ∈ {L, R }, let zc,h t denote the feature produced by the pretrained conventional tactile encoder, and let sht = Pc(zc,h t ) be its projected tactile token. The risk-aware representation zrt , obtained from the bilateral tactile history in Sec. III-B, is mapped into the same feature space through a lightweight residual adapter: 

δt = tanh( A(Pr zrt )) , (4) where Pr is an input projection and A(·) is a two-layer MLP. The resulting residual is fused with the conventional tactile tokens as 

˜zht = sht + αtask δt, h ∈ { L, R }, (5) where αtask is a fixed task-level scaling coefficient. Because 

zrt is encoded jointly from the bilateral tactile history, the same risk-aware residual is applied to both tactile tokens. In this way, RATE does not form a separate policy input stream. Instead, it directly complements the conventional tactile representation with interaction-context information organized around task risk. To preserve the behavior of the pretrained policy at the beginning of adaptation, the final linear layer of A(·) is initialized with zero weights and bias. The residual therefore starts from δt = 0 , so the fused representation initially repro-duces the original tactile tokens exactly. During policy adap-tation, the conventional tactile encoder, the RATE encoder, the visual backbone, and the ACT backbone remain frozen, while only Pr and the residual adapter A(·) are optimized. This updates approximately 0.59% of the complete model parameters. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate RATE on eight simulated manipulation tasks and three real-world contact-rich tasks. Our experiments address three questions: (1) Can RATE outperform repre-sentative manipulation policies overall without relying on large-scale models? (2) How do alert-guided learning and interaction context contribute, individually and jointly, to RATE, and how effectively can the resulting risk-aware representation be integrated through lightweight residual adaptation? (3) How effectively does RATE achieve both task completion and contact-safe execution in real-world settings? 

A. Experimental Setup Simulation benchmark. We evaluate RATE on the eight simulated tasks of the UniVTAC benchmark [4]. Following the official taxonomy, Contact-Rich Interaction includes Pull-out Key, Insert Hole, Insert HDMI, and Insert Tube. Pose Reasoning includes Lift Bottle, Lift Can, and Put Bottle. Shape Perception includes Grasp Classify. All tasks use a Franka Panda robot equipped with a parallel gripper and two simulated GelSight Mini tactile sensors. The policy receives RGB images, bilateral marker-based tactile images, and an eight-dimensional proprioceptive state. In our internal experiments, we use 100 demonstrations per task, with head- and wrist-camera RGB inputs across all eight tasks. Visual and tactile observations are resized to 256 × 256 , and robot states and actions are normalized using statistics from the corresponding training set. We use ACT’s action chunking and temporal aggregation [22], with a 50-step action chunk in our configuration. We compare RATE with ACT (Vision Only) [22], ACT+UniVTAC [4], VITaL [2], π0.5 [23], StarVLA-α [24], and GigaWorld-Policy [25]. We report published success rates for these base-lines [4], [13]. For a fair comparison, we additionally retrain ACT+UniVTAC using the same demonstrations and camera configuration as RATE. RATE, ACT+UniVTAC (Retrained), and our internally trained variants are evaluated under the same protocol over 100 closed-loop rollouts per task. We report task-wise success rates, the eight-task macro average, and model scale. 

Ablation protocol. All ablation variants share the same task-specific static tactile base, trained separately for each task using the same task-specific demonstrations as RATE and the UniVTAC-style tactile representation learning pro-cedure. Starting from this common task-specific tactile base, we vary two factors: alert and context. The alert is evalu-ated using three formulations—Discrete Ordinal, Continuous Severity, and Transition-Aware Continuous—while context is introduced through history-conditioned predictive model-ing. The task-specific tactile base contains neither factor; the alert-only configurations enable one alert formulation without context; the context-only configuration introduces context without alert-related training; and Full RATE com-bines the selected continuous severity alert formulation with context. All other settings, including demonstrations, visual inputs, ACT backbone, action representation, data split, and evaluation protocol, are kept identical. 

Real-world setup. We further evaluate RATE on USB Insertion, Bottle Cap Screwing, and Plug Insertion. All three tasks use the same platform: an xArm 7 robot with an xArm parallel gripper, two Daimon tactile sensors, a RealSense D435 head camera, and a RealSense D405 wrist camera. We collect 50 task-specific demonstrations for each task. To retain a consistent image-based tactile interface, the native Daimon measurements are converted into GelSight-like RGB tactile images, on which the tactile encoder is retrained. Each method is evaluated over 20 trials per task. USB Insertion and Plug Insertion are considered successful when the USB connector and plug, respectively, are fully inserted. For Bottle Cap Screwing, a trial is considered successful if tightening does not wrinkle the tablecloth and the cap cannot be unscrewed with light manual force after execution. B. Simulation Results on the UniVTAC Benchmark 

As shown in Table I, RATE achieves the highest macro-average success rate of 71.8% across the eight tasks, ranking first or jointly first on six. It leads all four Contact-Rich Interaction tasks, achieves the highest category-average per-formance in Pose Reasoning, and ties for the best result in Shape Perception. Notably, RATE reaches an average success rate of 72.0% across the four Contact-Rich Interaction tasks, exceeding the strongest comparison method in this category, StarVLA-α, by 23.0 percentage points. This pattern is con-sistent with RATE’s design goal of representing task risk in the context of evolving tactile interaction. RATE also maintains a clear advantage under the con-trolled experimental setting. When ACT+UniVTAC is re-trained using the same demonstrations and camera config-uration as RATE, RATE achieves a macro-average success rate of 71.8%, compared with 47.4% for ACT+UniVTAC (Retrained). On the four Contact-Rich Interaction tasks, RATE improves success rates by 38, 48, 27, and 41 per-centage points on Pull-out Key, Insert Hole, Insert HDMI, and Insert Tube, respectively. For a fairer comparison with UniVTAC, we additionally retrain ACT+UniVTAC using the same demonstrations and camera configuration as RATE. This provides a same-configuration external comparison, while RATE’s controlled ablations are evaluated relative to the task-specific tactile base in Sec. IV-C. Beyond Contact-Rich Interaction, RATE also achieves leading performance in the other task categories. It attains the highest category-average success rate of 62.0% on Pose Rea-soning and ties for the best result in Shape Perception with 100% success on Grasp Classify. These results demonstrate strong performance across task categories, with the most pronounced gains on the contact-rich interactions targeted by RATE. With approximately 133.2M parameters, RATE achieves a higher reported macro-average success rate than all com-pared methods, including large-scale visuomotor policies with 3.3B–5B parameters. In particular, RATE exceeds the strongest large-scale baseline, StarVLA-α, by 15.6 percent-age points based on unrounded averages. Overall, RATE achieves the highest reported macro-average success rate across the benchmark and maintains leading performance across all three task categories, despite using a substantially smaller model than the large-scale visuomotor policies considered. These results demonstrate that strong overall manipulation performance can be achieved without relying on large-scale models. 

C. Ablation Studies 

Table II reports the controlled ablation results for alert-guided learning and context under the protocol defined in Sec. IV-A. Starting from the common task-specific tactile base, we compare three alert formulations, context alone, and their combination in Full RATE. The task-specific tactile base achieves a macro-average success rate of 44.0%. Adding the three alert formulations increases the macro average to 56.1%, 57.5%, and 56.0% for Discrete Ordinal, Continuous Severity, and Transition-Aware Continuous, respectively. Continuous Severity Risk achieves the best overall result and is therefore adopted in Full RATE. Independently introducing context yields a macro-average success rate of 56.0%, corresponding to a 12.0 percentage-point improvement over the task-specific tactile base. When continuous alert and context are jointly incorpo-rated in Full RATE, the macro-average success rate further increases to 71.8%, outperforming the task-specific tactile base, continuous alert configuration, and context-only con-figuration by 27.8, 14.3, and 15.8 percentage points, respec-tively. We further use the continuous alert configuration as the direct controlled baseline for residual adaptation to evaluate the additional benefit of integrating RATE’s learned risk-aware representation. On the Contact-Rich Interaction tasks, the average success rate increases from 49.3% to 72.0%, including improvements from 32% to 71% on Insert Hole and from 18% to 42% on Insert HDMI. This comparison keeps the task-specific static tactile representation and alert-guided training fixed while additionally introducing context and the learned residual representation, thereby isolating the benefit of RATE’s history-conditioned risk-aware adaptation. During this stage, the tactile encoders, visual backbone, and ACT backbone remain frozen, with only approximately 0.59% of the model parameters updated. Taken together, these results show that alert-guided learn-ing and context each provide independent gains over the common task-specific tactile base, while their combination in Full RATE yields a further improvement, supporting their complementary roles within the same risk-aware representa-tion.

Together, the results across the three real-world tasks answer the third research question by demonstrating higher task success on every task and a higher macro-average proportion of successful executions that remain within the prescribed tactile-deformation limit. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented RATE, a risk-aware tactile representation learning framework for contact-rich robotic manipulation. RATE grounds task-conditioned risk in evolving tactile in-teraction context through history-conditioned prediction and alert supervision, and complements conventional tactile fea-tures through a lightweight residual pathway for parameter-efficient risk-aware adaptation. Experiments across eight simulated tasks and three real-world tasks demonstrate that RATE consistently improves manipulation performance with-out relying on large-scale models, with particularly strong gains on contact-rich interactions. Ablation studies further confirm the complementary effects of alert-guided learning and context within RATE. Real-world evaluations addition-ally show improved task completion together with a higher proportion of contact-safe successful executions. Although RATE explicitly models local temporal changes through feature differences, subtle but task-relevant tactile variations may still become less distinguishable when contact mag-nitudes vary substantially across interaction stages.
