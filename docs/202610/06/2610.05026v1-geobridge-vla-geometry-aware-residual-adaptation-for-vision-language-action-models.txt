# GeoBridge-VLA: Geometry-Aware Residual Adaptation for Vision-Language-Action Models

Abstract — Vision-language-action (VLA) models encode se-mantic information from vision-language pretraining, but ma-nipulation also requires precise spatial reasoning. We present GeoBridge-VLA, a two-stage method for learning geometric features from a pretrained VLA’s frozen visual encoder and using them for action prediction. Stage I trains a feature bridge and geometry decoder with depth supervision. Stage II freezes these modules and trains a gated residual interface together with the action-side projections and action expert. The residual augments the existing visual tokens without adding a second image encoder or increasing the token count. Deployment requires RGB, robot state, and language, but no depth obser-vations. Under matched evaluation conditions, GeoBridge-VLA achieves 70.9% success on LIBERO, compared with 60.0% for SmolVLA. Disabling the residual in the same trained checkpoint reduces success from 70.90% to 69.85%, with mixed effects across suites. On a physical ROBOTIS OMY robot, GeoBridge-VLA succeeds in 148 of 200 trials (74.0%) across four tasks, compared with 108 of 200 (54.0%) for SmolVLA.

Robotic manipulation, however, also depends on geometric properties such as depth, relative position, object extent, and local spatial relationships. Representations opti-mized primarily for semantic alignment do not necessarily preserve this information with the precision required for physical interaction. Recent work has addressed this limitation through explicit depth or 3D observations, external spatial representations, calibrated camera geometry, and auxiliary geometric super-vision. We investigate whether the frozen visual hierarchy of a VLA can provide depth-supervised features for action prediction without a separate image encoder, addi-tional geometry tokens, or depth observations at deployment. > •

We propose a two-stage geometry acquisition and geometry-to-action transfer strategy in which depth su-pervision first adapts the feature bridge and geometry decoder, after which they are frozen and a gated residual interface transfers their representation into the native visual-token space for action learning.

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Monocular Depth Estimation 

Transformer-based monocular depth estimation has pro-gressed from DPT and ZoeDepth to large-scale foundation models such as Depth Anything V2 and Depth Anything 3 [10]–[13]. CLIP-based approaches further demonstrate that depth-related information can be recovered from frozen vision–language representations: DepthCLIP exploits patch– text correlations, while SPACE-CLIP directly decodes se-mantic and structural features from a frozen CLIP vision encoder [14], [15]. These works primarily target depth prediction; GeoBridge-VLA instead uses depth supervision to adapt geometric information for closed-loop action gen-eration. 

B. Vision-Language-Action Models 

VLA models extend pretrained vision–language repre-sentations to robotic control. RT-2 and OpenVLA demon-strate large-scale vision–language-to-action transfer [1], [2], while π0 uses flow matching for continuous action genera-tion [3]. Recent systems also emphasize efficient policy de-sign and deployment, including OpenVLA-OFT, TinyVLA, and SmolVLA [4], [16], [17]. We use SmolVLA as the policy backbone throughout our action-policy experiments. 

C. Geometry-Aware Vision-Language-Action Models 

Recent VLA systems incorporate geometric or spatial structure through several mechanisms. Explicit 3D ap-proaches use depth, point clouds, or dedicated spatial rep-resentations: 3D-VLA couples 3D perception and action generation [18], FP3 and PointVLA operate on point-cloud features [19], [20], and 3D-CAVLA combines depth perception with task-oriented spatial reasoning [21]. Other methods improve spatial reasoning without requiring metric depth at deployment. IVRA reuses affinity information from the native visual encoder at inference [22], while RetoVLA repurposes Register Tokens for spatial reasoning [23]. FAL-CON injects features from an embodied spatial model [7], and GIFT imposes geometry-aware structural supervision on intermediate policy representations [9]. Other approaches incorporate geometric structure into VLA policies through different mechanisms. SpatialVLA predicts depth and back-projects image features into egocen-tric 3D structure [5]. G 3VLA injects calibrated camera ge-ometry through geometric encodings and cross-view fusion [8], while GP3 and AugVLA-3D introduce dedicated spatial encoders or VGGT-derived 3D features before action pre-diction [24], [25]. QDepth-VLA and DepthVLA incorporate dedicated depth experts or pretrained depth transformers [6], [26], whereas Evo-Depth extracts implicit depth features from RGB and progressively aligns depth-aware modulation with action learning [27]. Concurrent GaussVLA lifts seman-tic and depth features into compact 3D Gaussian tokens for geometric reasoning [28]. GeoBridge-VLA instead learns a depth-supervised geo-metric readout directly from intermediate features of the frozen native VLA visual encoder and transfers the re-sulting representation through a gated residual interface. This separates geometry acquisition from geometry-to-action transfer while requiring neither a second image encoder nor additional visual tokens at deployment. III. M ETHOD

A. Stage I: Depth Model Adaptation 

Feature bridge. We adopt a geometry decoder adapted from SPACE-CLIP [15] with fixed local token aggregation, termed K4. At each aggregation stage, the decoder combines features sampled at four fixed neighboring positions using content-dependent weights, preserving local spatial structure. This design improves standalone depth prediction over the original decoder under matched backbone and data settings. The formulation and standalone comparison are provided in the appendix. We connect this decoder to the native VLA features through a learnable bridge. Both LIBERO and OMY follow this Stage-II optimization scheme; they differ in the source of Stage-I depth targets and the action demonstrations used for Stage II. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate manipulation success on LIBERO and aphysical ROBOTIS OMY robot, depth prediction from the shared encoder, and the effect of disabling the residual at inference. All policy experiments use SmolVLA; transfer to other VLA backbones remains untested. The method preserves the native visual-token count but adds a geometry decoder and residual interface. 

A. LIBERO Data and Training 

Geometry supervision. For Stage-I depth adaptation, we use a LIBERO-derived RGB-D collection with metric-depth supervision. We train Stage I for 20,000 optimization steps with a batch size of 4. Consistent with Sec. III, the native visual encoder remains frozen during Stage I, while the feature bridge and geometry decoder are optimized using Eq. 2. 

Action learning. Policy training uses the Hugging-FaceVLA LIBERO collection containing 1,693 demonstra-tions and 273,465 action frames. SmolVLA and GeoBridge-VLA use the same action data and policy-training conditions. Both policies are trained for 50,000 optimization steps with a batch size of 32. Evaluation protocols are described in Sec. IV-B. Before Stage-II action learning, the feature bridge and ge-ometry decoder are initialized from Stage I and subsequently frozen. During Stage II, only the residual interface, action-side projections, and native action expert are optimized using Eq. 4. No depth supervision is used during this stage. At inference time, both methods receive RGB observa-tions, robot state, and language instructions. GeoBridge-VLA derives geometric features from the same frozen visual hierarchy used by the policy and injects them through the learned residual interface. Neither method receives depth as a rollout observation.

C. Real-World ROBOTIS OMY Setup 

Table I lists the four OMY tasks and the language instruc-tion used for each task. Figure 4 shows the wrist and external camera views for the four physical task settings. 

Data collection and training. We collect 50 demonstra-tions per task using a ROBOTIS OMY robot, recording front- and wrist-view RGB observations, robot states, and actions. The task-specific SmolVLA baselines use the same collected action demonstrations. OMY training follows the two-stage procedure in Sec. III. Stage I is trained for 20,000 optimization steps with a batch size of 4, while Stage II is trained for 50,000 optimization steps with a batch size of 32. The task-specific SmolVLA baselines use the same batch size and number of optimization steps as Stage II for policy training. In Stage I, we use Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [13] as a frozen teacher to generate pseudo-depth targets for the collected RGB observations. These targets supervise the feature bridge and K4 geometry decoder using Eq. 2, while the native SmolVLA visual encoder remains frozen. In Stage II, we initialize the bridge and geometry decoder from Stage I and freeze them. We then train the residual interface, including the front- and wrist-view routing/readout components, together with the action-side projections and native action expert on our directly collected robot demon-strations. Optimization uses flow-matching loss and residual regularization in Eq. 4. The visual encoder, visual projector, and VLM remain frozen. Stage II uses no depth targets. At deployment, the DA3 [13] teacher is removed, and both methods receive only RGB, robot state, and language. 

Evaluation. We evaluate each method over 50 matched autonomous trials per task. Each initial configuration is shared between SmolVLA and GeoBridge-VLA, with the robot start state, object placement, target placement, and task-specific initial state reset before each rollout. Both methods use the same instruction, camera setup, execution limits, and task-success criterion. A trial succeeds only when the complete task is achieved without human intervention; an incomplete task, timeout, or intervention is counted as a failure. V. R ESULTS

C. Residual Conditioning Analysis 

Table IV compares GeoBridge-VLA with and without the geometric residual at inference using the same trained checkpoint and the protocol in Sec. IV-B. This analysis examines the role of the geometry-to-action interface beyond depth prediction. Enabling the residual yields 70.90% overall success, com-pared with 69.85% when it is disabled, a difference of 1.05 percentage points. The residual improves Spatial and Long by 3.20 and 2.80 points, respectively, but reduces Object and Goal by 1.20 and 0.60 points. The effect therefore varies across suites. Because both conditions retain the weights learned during Stage II, this experiment measures the effect of enabling the residual at inference. It does not isolate the contribution of geometry to policy learning.

VI. D ISCUSSION 

Our results support three observations about the proposed geometry retrofit. First, the shared-encoder depth results show that depth-related geometric information can be de-coded from intermediate representations already produced by the VLA visual encoder, without introducing a second image encoder. Together with the manipulation results, this supports the feasibility of using the native visual hierarchy as a substrate for geometry adaptation and subsequent action conditioning. Second, the improvement of the complete GeoBridge-VLA pipeline should be distinguished from the effect of the residual interface at inference. GeoBridge-VLA improves overall LIBERO success from 60.0% to 70.9%, whereas disabling the residual in the same trained checkpoint reduces overall success from 70.90% to 69.85%, with mixed effects across suites. Because the zero-residual condition retains the weights learned with the geometry pathway, this intervention measures the trained policy’s inference-time sensitivity to the geometry-to-action interface rather than isolating the contribution of geometry during policy learning. The results characterize GeoBridge-VLA under these settings but do not establish that the same gains will transfer to other VLA architectures or robot embodiments. For the physical-robot setting, Stage-I adaptation relies on teacher-generated pseudo-depth, and sensitivity to the choice and quality of this supervision remains untested. Evaluating the retrofit strategy across additional backbones, robot morpholo-gies, depth supervision sources, and manipulation domains is an important direction for future work. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented GeoBridge-VLA, a two-stage method that adapts frozen VLA visual features with depth supervision and transfers the resulting geometric representation to action prediction through a gated residual interface. The deployed policy reuses its native visual encoder and requires no depth observations. GeoBridge-VLA improves overall LIBERO success from 60.0% to 70.9% and OMY success from 54.0% to 74.0% relative to SmolVLA. These results demonstrate the feasi-bility of retrofitting depth-supervised geometric information into an existing VLA visual pathway without introducing a second image encoder at deployment.
