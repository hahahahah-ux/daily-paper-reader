## Abstract 

World models learn to predict how their environment will evolve, making them an important foundation for general-purpose robotic control. Yet World Action Models (WAMs) rely on camera inputs, and manipulating those inputs can corrupt the visual representations that underpin their tasks and action policies. Existing attacks on these models optimize against actions or predicted futures and require access to victim outputs or generation modules. We propose TAPDreamer , a transferable fixed local patch whose pixel optimization uses only a public visual encoder and requires no target-policy queries. Its design addresses two requirements: a local modification should affect representations beyond its footprint, and the resulting disturbance should persist across observations. We optimize a fixed local patch on six frames from one source task by maximizing the mean L1 distance between clean and patched encoder outputs over all latent positions and channels. Mechanism analysis reveals that interactions between patch-induced changes in attention weights and value vectors broadcast a nearly identical representation shift beyond the patch footprint, while a shared perturbation response persists across task observations despite differences in clean latents. In closed-loop evaluation, one frozen patch per benchmark, covering about 6.5% of the input, reduces FastWAM’s success rate from 97.65% to 0.00% across 40 LIBERO tasks and from 90.86% to 0.00% across 50 RoboTwin tasks; matched random patches retain 81.45% and 79.20% success. The same patches reduce success to 1.45% and 1.00% on two DreamWAM configurations and to 10.60% on Motus. Behavioral analysis connects these representation shifts to grasping failures and separates rapid command recovery after patch removal from persistent physical consequences. These results show that protecting downstream action generation alone is insufficient: WAM defenses must also secure shared visual encoders against persistent local perturbations. 

## 1. Introduction 

World Action Models (WAMs) are emerging as a powerful foundation for general-purpose robotic control. Rather than predicting actions from the current observation alone, a WAM learns how the visual world evolves together with the robot’s actions. This world-modeling objective captures objects, spatial relationships, and motion, and can support action generation either through explicit future imagination (Du et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024) or through representations learned during video prediction (Yuan et al., 2026b). By unifying visual prediction and control, WAMs promise policies that can reason about the consequences of their actions and replan as the environment changes (Zhu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026). This coupling, however, also creates a security risk. Current WAM pipelines implicitly trust the visual representation on which world prediction and action generation are built. A perturbation that corrupts this shared interface can therefore bypass model-specific action heads. If the channel remains exposed, a perturbation can be injected consistently. In this paper, we investigate whether an attacker can exploit this vulnerability and launch attacks against WAMs that transfer to unseen tasks and models. We propose TAPDreamer (illustrated in Figure 1), a transferable attack that applies a patch on visual inputs to WAMs. TAPDreamer maximizes the displacement of the final latent produced by visual encoder. The search uses a few demonstration frames from a single source task. Once the patch is found, the patch pixels, area, and position are frozen and the same artifact is deployed unchanged throughout each episode and across target tasks and models. Throughout the attack, TAPDreamer does not require action labels, task rewards, simulator, victim-model weights, or policy outputs, making TAPDreamer simple and inexpensive to initiate. 81 .45% and 79 .20% success, respectively. The same frozen patches also transfer across WAM architectures without retraining or target-model queries: success falls from 84 .55% and 86 .10% with matched random patches to 1.45% and 1.00% on DreamWAM-uncond and DreamWAM-joint, and from 82 .40% to 10 .60% on Motus. We further trace how a fixed patch causes failure during closed-loop control. Our results show that changing a small set of pixels distorts visual features across the scene, including those used to select actions, and thus leads to reduced task success rate.

## 2.2. Threat Model 

Attacker’s objective. 

We consider an attacker who aims to reduce the task success rate of a WAM-controlled robot by modifying a small region of its visual input. This is an untargeted, test-time attack. 

Attacker’s knowledge and access. 

The attacker has white-box access to a publicly released visual encoder, including its weights and gradients, and a small set of public demonstration images from one source task. We assume that the attacker knows the relevant visual preprocessing, including image normalization and camera-view composition. Patch optimization requires only the WAM’s encoder and a small set of demonstration frames from a single source task. The attacker does not require access to the downstream world model or action decoder or their gradients, action labels, or task rewards for pixel optimization. 

Attacker’s capabilities. 

The attacker can replace pixels within a fixed local region of the visual input before encoding. Let 𝑀 be a fixed binary mask and 𝑝 the patch. The attacked observation is 

𝑇 𝑝 (𝑥 𝑡 ) = (1 − 𝑀 ) ⊙ 𝑥 𝑡 + 𝑀 ⊙ 𝑝, (3) where ⊙ represents elementwise multiplication. The attacker chooses the patch content. At deployment, the patch content, position, and area remain unchanged. The same patch is reused across target tasks and models within each benchmark. The attack does not manipulate pixels outside the mask, language instructions, proprioceptive inputs, or policy parameters. The attacker cannot directly set the robot’s actions or the environment state.

## 2.3. Attack Design and Implementation 

Design rationale. 

The attack exploits the reuse of public visual encoders across WAMs with different downstream control architec-tures. The encoder output therefore provides an attack objective independent of a particular action interface. For local pixel changes to affect broader scene representations, they must exploit propagation pathways within the encoder. We maximize the difference between the encoder’s clean and patched representations over the entire latent output, including all spatial positions and channels. The same patch is optimized over multiple source observations.

## 3. Experiments 

## 3.1. Experimental Setup 

Datasets and models. 

We conduct closed-loop evaluation on LIBERO and RoboTwin (Liu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025). LIBERO contains four task categories (Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long-horizon) with 10 tasks each. RoboTwin contains 50 tasks. FastWAM is the default evaluation model. DreamWAM-uncond (DW-U), DreamWAM-joint (DW-J) (Yuan et al., 2026a), and Motus (Bi et al., 2026) are used for transfer evaluation within the same benchmark. Each model configuration retains its own action generation process and preprocessing. 

Attack setup. 

We run TAPDreamer using patches optimized on source tasks; cross-model transfer occurs within each bench-mark. Patches are injected before encoding. On LIBERO, the external and wrist views form a 224 × 448 canvas; the main patch occupies an 81 × 81 region in the upper-left corner of the external view, covering 6.54% of the input. The corresponding coverage on RoboTwin is 6.59%. Patches remain visible throughout each trial in standard attack evaluations. Images used for optimization come from public LeRobot demonstrations. We filter episodes by source-task identifier, then select one frame from each of six episodes at temporal indices 5, 20, 40, 60, 15, and 35, respectively. When generating the patch, TAPDreamer sets 𝐽 = 6 with 500 Adam gradient-ascent updates on patch pixels, using a learning rate of 0.02. Patch pixels are initialized uniformly within [−1, 1] . After each update, they are projected back to this range, with support enforced by the fixed mask. 

Baselines. 

We compare TAPDreamer with baselines to show its effectiveness. We evaluate the policy on unmodified observations to establish the clean baseline. For random patch , we replace the same image region with a random patch matched to the adversarial patch in position and area, to measure the effects of ordinary local occlusion and pixel replacement. Random patches are generated following a uniform distribution within admissible range of pixel values. Extra ablation of transferred VLA attack is deferred to Appendix A.2. 

Metrics.

TAPDreamer reduces the success rate to zero on both benchmarks. 

We evaluate FastWAM on all four LIBERO suites with 10 tasks each ( 4 × 10 tasks), using 50 trials per task, and on all 50 RoboTwin tasks, using 100 trials per task. ## 5. Related Work 

Transferable and localized adversarial attacks. 

Adversarial perturbations can alter neural-network predictions (Goodfellow et al., 2015). Cross-model transfer has been studied using ensembles (Liu et al., 2017), momentum (Dong et al., 2018), and input diversity (Xie et al., 2019). Universal perturbations reuse a single perturbation across images (Moosavi-Dezfooli et al., 2017), with data-independent constructions such as Fast Feature Fool (Mopuri et al., 2017). Feature-level objectives have been explored through feature disruption (Ganeshan et al., 2019), feature importance (Wang et al., 2021), and neuron attribution (Zhang et al., 2022). Localized attacks include LaVAN (Karmon et al., 2018) and attention-aware Patch-Fool (Fu et al., 2022); physical attacks include robust object perturbations (Eykholt et al., 2018) and camera stickers (Li et al., 2019). PhotoGuard also studies encoder-level attacks on diffusion-based image editing (Salman et al., 2023). In sequential decision-making, adversarial policies can disrupt reinforcement-learning agents through an opponent’s behavior (Gleave et al., 2020). 

Attack on vision-language-action policies. 

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies such as OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2025) and 𝜋 0 (Black et al., 2025) map visual and language inputs to robot actions. Action-based attacks on VLA policies optimize action differences, motion directions, target actions, or action freezing (Wang et al., 2025a,b; Yan et al., 2025). Their gradient-based objectives require access through the corresponding action generation interfaces. Representation-based methods attack image–text alignment, feature displacement, projection features, attention relationships, or visual proprioception (Xu et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). Clean–perturbed representation distances already appear in prior attacks; here, we study their effectiveness for fixed local attacks on a public generative visual encoder and the mechanism of transfer. In the WAM pathway studied here, image–text interaction occurs downstream of the public VAE, so the corresponding alignment 

> 10 TAPDreamer: Transferable Adversarial Patches for World Action Models

and attention objectives fall outside the default encoder-only access. We therefore define the objective directly on the encoder output and evaluate its effects on control through closed-loop experiments and mechanism interventions. 

Adversarial threats to world models and world action models. 

BadWorld (Shen et al., 2026) evaluates attacks on generated videos, without testing closed-loop robotic tasks. BadWAM (Li et al., 2026) perturbs the full visual input through online target-model queries at each replanning step. Attacking the Trusted Imagination (Chen et al., 2026) optimizes observation perturbations using end-to-end gradients through the encoder and world model, corrupting future predictions used by downstream planning or safety checks. JailWAM (Liu et al., 2026) induces unsafe actions and requires attacker control over language instructions. Unlike these WAM threats, TAPDreamer achieves closed-loop attacks across tasks and WAM architectures with a fixed local patch optimized solely through a public visual encoder, without using target-policy action outputs or future predictions. 

## 6. Conclusion 

TAPDreamer optimizes a fixed local patch using only a public visual encoder and six observations from one task, yet produces strong closed-loop interference across two complete benchmarks and different downstream action architectures. Mechanism analysis reveals that interactions between attention-weight and value changes broadcast local interference to distant representations, while optimized perturbation responses remain highly consistent across task observations. Behavioral analysis connects this representation vulnerability to grasping and pose interference, and separates the immediate effects of sustained visual input from state consequences accumulated through execution. These results show that WAM robustness must cover the full path from local visual input through representation, generation, and control. 

## Ethics statement 

This study aims to identify vulnerabilities of World Action Models to local visual perturbations to inform robot safety evaluation and defense research. All experiments were conducted in the LIBERO and RoboTwin simulation environments, with attacks implemented by modifying model input images. We recognize that such methods could be misused to disrupt normal robot operation and emphasize that their use should be restricted to authorized security testing and research. Our mechanism analysis suggests potential defenses that attenuate keys and values from suspicious image regions or train the encoder to preserve representations outside locally perturbed regions. These interventions target the attention-mediated spread of the patch-induced shift; their effectiveness against adaptive attacks and their impact on clean-task performance remain to be evaluated.

## B. How the Latent Shift Changes Action 

## B.1. Attention-restoration interventions 

We want to understand which patch-induced changes within attention contribute to perturbation propagation, particularly the roles of attention weights 𝐴 and value vectors 𝑉 in downstream representation and action changes. To investigate this, we conduct three restoration interventions: restoring the entire attention branch, restoring only the attention weights 𝐴 , and restoring only the value vectors 𝑉 . Each intervention retains the patched residual branch and replaces only the specified component. We measure the remaining change relative to the clean output, normalized by the change without restoration. For latent retention, both changes are measured using the L2 norm over the entire latent, including the patch footprint; action retention uses the corresponding L2 ratio for action outputs. The random patch is evaluated with the same latent-retention metric. Because attention reconstruction uses a different numerical precision from model inference, the patched reference without restoration is computed through the same reconstruction pathway. These ratios measure retained changes, not task success rates. Table 7 shows that all three interventions leave approximately 33% of the latent change and 32% of the action change induced by TAPDreamer . In comparison, random-patch latent retention remains approximately 1.000. Thus, restoring the attention branch, its weights, or its values removes most of the TAPDreamer -induced latent and action changes, while having little effect on the random patch. ## B.3. Behavioral effects 

The preceding analyses show how patch-induced perturbations propagate through attention and how optimiza-tion establishes a shared direction before amplifying its magnitude. We now ask how these representation changes affect action commands and, in turn, lead to task failure. 

Visible execution failures. 

The same representation shift can affect different actions as a task unfolds. In Figure 8, the patch drives the end effector below its clean trajectory and prevents task completion. We use same-state action comparisons, internal restoration, and patch removal to connect the shift to commands and physical consequences. 

Immediate action changes. 

Holding the physical state fixed, we compare actions from clean and patched observations before their tra-jectories diverge. In FastWAM, the patch suppresses lifting, biases pose commands downward, and shifts gripper commands toward opening. A fit in which each patched six-dimensional pose command mixes its clean counterpart with a shared target explains 78.2% of the variation across 1,500 paired commands. These are state-level measurements, not independent episodes. The same bias can prevent a lift, lose an object after grasping, or move the end effector away from its intended position, depending on the task stage. These action patterns emerge from the encoder-level objective, which specifies no target action.
