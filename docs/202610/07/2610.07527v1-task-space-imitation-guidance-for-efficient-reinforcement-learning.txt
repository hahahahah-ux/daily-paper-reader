# Task-Space Imitation Guidance for Efficient Reinforcement Learning 

Salar Asayesh, Hossein Darani, Todd Cao, Evgeny Andriash, Mani Ranjbar 

Sanctuary AI, Canada 

{salar.asayesh,hossein.darani,todd.cao, evgeny.andriash,mani.ranjbar}@sanctuary.ai 

Abstract: We introduce Task-Space Imitation Guidance for Efficient Reinforce-ment Learning (TIGER), a reward-construction and pretraining framework for sparse-reward tabletop robotic manipulation. TIGER treats an action-chunked imitation policy not as an executable controller or action prior, but as a local task-space progress estimator: predicted action chunks are converted, using controller-aware action-to-motion mapping, into short-horizon end-effector references, and the RL agent receives dense progress rewards toward these references while the sparse environment reward remains the dominant objective. During pretrain-ing, TIGER uses imitation-guided look-ahead signals to relax conservative value penalties for actions predicted to make task-space progress, reducing off-manifold exploration during early online RL. Across simulation and real-robot experiments, TIGER improves early sample efficiency and reduces measured safety violations while matching or improving final success rates relative to prior RL and IL–RL baselines on the evaluated tasks. Videos and an overview of this work are available at https://sanctuary.ai/news/tiger/ .

Keywords: Reinforcement learning, Imitation guidance, Sample efficiency 

## 1 Introduction 

Robotic imitation learning (IL) has become a central paradigm for learning manipulation skills from demonstrations, with action chunking, diffusion policies, and generalist robot policies show-ing strong results on diverse tasks [1, 2, 3]. By learning directly from demonstrations, these methods avoid manually engineered dense rewards and accurate task dynamics models. However, deployment-oriented manipulation exposes limitations that are less visible in benchmark settings: policies must maintain high success under variation while also satisfying reliability and cycle-time constraints. Achieving this with pure IL typically requires broad, high-quality demonstration cover-age, making physical data collection costly through teleoperation or careful curation. More funda-mentally, imitation objectives optimize agreement with demonstrated actions rather than task suc-cess, robustness, or execution time. Thus, IL provides a strong behavioral prior, but not a mechanism for autonomous task optimization or systematic improvement beyond demonstrations [4, 5, 6]. Reinforcement Learning (RL) can improve beyond imitation by directly optimizing task success through interaction. Unlike IL, RL can collect new experience and discover behaviors that are more reliable or efficient than the demonstrations. Discounted objectives also favor receiving task rewards earlier, making RL appealing when cycle time matters. However, direct robot RL remains difficult: sparse or binary rewards provide little feedback during early exploration, while unstructured ex-ploration can cause unsafe motions, workspace violations, object drops, or frequent manual resets. Thus, RL provides a principled mechanism for task optimization, but requires structured guidance to become sample-efficient and practical for real-world manipulation. Prior IL–RL methods improve sample efficiency by using demonstrations for initialization, replay-buffer augmentation, auxiliary losses, or action proposals [7, 8, 9]. However, in sparse-reward set-arXiv:2610.07527v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 tings these approaches can still rely on unreliable early critics to balance imitation and exploration. Dense trajectory-alignment rewards offer another source of feedback [10, 11], but typically require ground-truth state trajectories, image-level alignment, or task-specific supervision. This motivates extracting dense guidance from imitation itself: demonstrations naturally specify task-relevant mo-tion, while hand-engineered rewards require detailed state, models, or task-specific design. Rather than using imitation only as an action prior, such guidance can provide task-aligned feedback before sparse rewards or accurate value estimates are available. We propose Task-Space Imitation Guidance for Efficient Reinforcement Learning (TIGER) ,which turns imitation policies into dense task-space rewards and imitation-guided critic pretrain-ing for sparse-reward robotic manipulation. TIGER treats an imitation policy not as an executable controller or action prior, but as a local task-space progress estimator: given the current observa-tion, the policy predicts an action chunk that is mapped to a short-horizon end-effector reference trajectory. The RL agent receives dense reward for making progress toward this reference, while the sparse environment reward remains the dominant task objective. TIGER also uses imitation-guided look-ahead signals to modulate conservative critic pretraining, reducing early off-manifold exploration. Because guidance enters through rewards and pretraining, TIGER can combine with different off-policy RL algorithms. TIGER targets tasks where short-horizon end-effector motion is informative for progress, assuming reliable end-effector pose estimates and an approximate action-to-end-effector mapping. We evaluate TIGER in simulation and on real robots across sparse-reward manipulation tasks with randomized initial conditions, including grasping, opening, and insertion. Across settings, TIGER improves early sample efficiency, reduces measured safety violations, and achieves competitive or higher final success rates than prior RL and IL–RL baselines. Our contributions are: (i) an imitation-guided reward construction method that maps predicted action chunks into short-horizon task-space reference motion and dense progress rewards for sparse-reward manipulation; (ii) an offline pretraining objective that uses imitation-guided look-ahead signals to stabilize value learning before online interaction; and (iii) simulated and real-robot evidence that TIGER improves early sample efficiency and reduces measured safety violations while maintaining competitive final success rates. 

## 2 Related Work 

A related line of research studies learning from fixed datasets—offline reinforcement learning—as formalized by Levine et al. [12], where off-policy methods must mitigate value overestimation from out-of-distribution (OOD) actions. Kumar et al. [13] introduce pessimistic regularization that lowers the value of unsupported actions, Kostrikov et al. [14] restrict policy learning to the dataset support, and Nakamoto et al. [15] add a calibration mechanism that stabilizes online fine-tuning. Building on these advances, several approaches combine offline pre-training with online fine-tuning (Nair et al. [16], Nakamoto et al. [15], Haldar et al. [17]), achieving substantial gains in sample efficiency, though most rely on dense reward signals. Kostrikov et al. [14] constrain policy learning to the dataset support, and Nakamoto et al. [15] cali-brate value estimates to stabilize online fine-tuning. Building on these advances, several approaches combine offline pretraining with online fine-tuning [15, 16, 17], achieving substantial gains in sam-ple efficiency, though most assume access to dense rewards. A complementary line of work uses demonstrations to address sparse or binary rewards in contact-rich manipulation. Replay mixing with demonstrations [8, 18] can accelerate learning, but becomes less effective as task horizons grow. Other methods regularize the policy toward the demonstrated action distribution [19, 20]. GAIL [21] and AIRL [22] instead learn imitation objectives through adversarial discriminator or reward optimization. Li et al. [23] learns a state-only goal-proximity reward from constrained demonstrations and extends it to online states through confidence-guided 2interpolation. TIGER uses a fixed imitation policy to provide task-space guidance without online discriminator or reward learning. Recent advances show that end-to-end reinforcement learning on physical robots is feasible within practical time budgets. For instance, Luo et al. [7], building on Ball et al. [8], introduce a unified framework where careful system design enables scalable real-world training from scratch, though many approaches restrict operational space and focus on short-horizon tasks. In contrast, Hu et al. [9] train an imitation policy from few demonstrations to guide exploration and bootstrap value es-timation, achieving state-of-the-art efficiency but lacking a principled early-stage balance between imitation and exploration, which can slow or destabilize convergence. More recently, dense surrogate rewards from learned perceptual or task-space representations have guided exploration in sparse-reward settings. For example, Zhao et al. [11] employ a pretrained DINOv2 encoder to construct an embedding space for forward prediction, encouraging exploration near demonstration trajectories and enabling efficient real-robot learning, albeit under constrained randomization and increased computational cost. ReWiND [24], Robometer [25], and RoboRe-ward [26] instead estimate task progress in learned visual or vision-language spaces. TIGER con-verts imitation-predicted action chunks into short-horizon task-space progress signals.

## 4 Method 

The method consists of three components: controller-aware trajectory mapping, imitation-guided reward and observation design, and offline pretraining with a task-aware conservative regularizer. 

4.1 Controller-Aware Trajectory Mapping 

We convert each imitation-predicted action chunk into an execution-aligned EEF trajectory using a controller-aware mapping. This avoids directly rolling out actions in their policy parameterization, which can disagree with realized robot motion due to controller scaling, frame conventions, ori-entation parameterization, latency, tracking bandwidth, and load-dependent execution error. Such mismatch can degrade both the target pose xgt and the alignment reward in Eq. (3). We therefore map each imitation-predicted action chunk through three stages:

The second stage, fϕ, is a lightweight learned residual model fitted from demonstrations to predict the executed counterpart ˆu of each con-troller command. This residual captures only first-order, time-invariant tracking effects, such as latency, finite bandwidth, and command attenuation; it is not intended to model contact dynam-ics, force interactions, or task-specific object motion. The final stage, Πeef , converts the predicted executed commands into an EEF reference trajectory. In our experiments, where commands cor-respond to relative EEF motions, Πeef accumulates the predicted position deltas and composes the 3orientation deltas from the current measured EEF pose. More generally, Πeef can be replaced by the identity map for absolute EEF targets, or by forward kinematics for joint-space commands. We denote the complete mapping as the composition Fmap = Π eef ◦ fϕ ◦ gctrl .At every chunk refresh, the trajectory is anchored to the current measured robot state, so prediction errors remain local to the short guidance horizon rather than accumulating over the full episode. This mapping should therefore be viewed as a local geometric guide rather than a task dynamics model: it keeps early exploration near imitation-guided behaviors, while online RL still optimizes the true sparse task objective and can correct residual mapping errors through interaction. We ablate the learned residual component by comparing TIGER with and without fϕ, isolating the effect of correcting systematic controller mismatch beyond the analytic transformations in gctrl . The mapping is trained once per robot/controller setup and reused across tasks; details, prediction-error analysis, and this ablation are provided in Appendix B.

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate TIGER on simulated and real-robot sparse-reward manipulation tasks. Our experiments ask whether TIGER (i) accelerates sparse-reward learning and improves beyond its BC prior, (ii) outperforms learned reward alternatives under a matched RL backend, (iii) retains its11 benefit in a multi-task benchmark, and (iv) improves real-robot learning efficiency and measured safety. We instantiate TIGER with two off-policy RL backends, RLPD [8] and IBRL [9]. We compare against representative sparse-reward robot learning methods that use demonstrations, pretrained im-itation policies, or dense surrogate rewards: RLPD, which mixes prior demonstrations with online replay; IBRL, which uses imitation for action proposal and value bootstrapping; LaNE [11], which constructs dense rewards by aligning observations with expert trajectories in visual representation space; DSRL [29], which improves a frozen flow-matching policy through latent-space RL; and ResFiT [30], which fine-tunes behavior cloning through residual action-space corrections. We use flow-matching [31] behavior cloning as BC policy. For the targeted learned-reward comparison, we additionally evaluate ReWiND [24],which learns language-conditioned progress rewards from demonstrations, and Robometer [25], a general-purpose video-language reward model trained with frame-level progress and trajectory-comparison supervision. We analyze the learned mapping residual, offline pretraining, and measured safety violations to assess their contributions to TIGER’s performance. Additional ablations on demonstration quality, guidance design, and mapping transfer are in Appendix A.3. 

Effect of pretraining. To isolate pretraining, we repeat Block Picking and Drawer Opening with TIGER from scratch (Fig. 2). TIGER without pretraining still outperforms IBRL, showing that the imitation-guided reward is beneficial on its own. However, pretraining improves early exploration and reduces boundary violations. We also pretrain IBRL with CalQL and observe no meaningful gain in performance or boundary violations, suggesting that generic offline RL pretraining is less aligned with the task objective. 

Safety analysis. We log unsafe events during real-robot training on Block Picking and Drawer Opening (Fig. 3). A violation is leaving the task-specific safety box or triggering a controller fail-ure; Appendix A.2 gives the full bounds and reset conditions. TIGER pretraining substantially re-duces early resets by keeping behavior closer to the demonstration manifold. Removing pretraining increases exploration and resets, whereas sparse-reward IBRL incurs fewer early violations partly because it engages the task more slowly. DSRL likewise incurs few violations under identical safety boxes and reset rules, consistent with conservative behavior inherited from its frozen BC prior; that conservatism can limit improvement beyond the prior. CalQL-pretrained IBRL does not yield simi-lar safety gains, indicating that TIGER’s effect is not due to value initialization alone. 

Effect of the learned controller residual. We ablate only the learned residual in the controller-aware trajectory mapping on Square, leaving the known controller transformations gctrl and EEF trajectory accumulation Πeef active. The residual captures systematic execution mismatch, including latency, action smoothing, and finite tracking bandwidth, rather than contact dynamics. As shown in Fig. 4, it improves training speed, but TIGER without the residual still learns faster than IBRL. In this Square ablation, short-horizon task-space guidance provides the main gain, while the learned residual improves execution alignment and training 

Choice of pretraining objective. We compare three offline pretraining variants on the Square task: CalQL with sparse rewards, CalQL with the TIGER dense reward, and full TIGER pretraining. All variants use the same dataset from Sec. 5; policies are evaluated over 100 rollouts per epoch, and Fig. 5 reports a moving average with window 4. TIGER pretraining yields higher offline success and more stable learning. Standard CalQL often degrades during training, consistent with prior observations in offline robotic RL [36]; adding the dense reward partially mitigates this degradation, while the full TIGER objective gives the most robust gains. 

## 6 Limitations 

TIGER assumes that short-horizon end-effector motion is an informative local proxy for task progress, which is appropriate for the reaching, grasping, insertion, and articulated-object manipu-lation tasks studied here. This assumption may be weaker when success depends primarily on force regulation, tactile feedback, deformable-object dynamics, dexterous in-hand manipulation, hidden object state, or contact-mode reasoning rather than end-effector pose. TIGER also relies on locally meaningful imitation guidance: for multimodal demonstrations, the imitation policy selects one short-horizon reference at each replanning step, providing locally consistent guidance rather than enforcing global trajectory matching; however, systematically misleading IL predictions can still bias the shaped reward and target-conditioned observation. Finally, TIGER inherits the standard offline-to-online challenge that a pretrained policy may transiently degrade when exposed to online exploration; improving this handoff is an important direction for future work. 9Acknowledgments

A.1 Baseline Details 

We choose baselines that are most relevant to TIGER’s setting: sparse-reward robot learning with access to a small number of expert demonstrations or a pretrained imitation policy. Specifically, we compare against methods that incorporate demonstrations into RL, use demonstrations to construct auxiliary guidance signals, or improve a frozen behavior-cloning policy through online RL. In both simulation and real-robot experiments, we use a flow-matching BC policy as the imitation backbone and report its standalone performance as an imitation-only baseline. This comparison set allows us to isolate whether TIGER’s gains come from controller-aware task-space guidance, rather than simply from access to demonstrations, imitation-policy initialization, or residual policy improvement. 

IBRL: Imitation Bootstrapped Reinforcement Learning (IBRL) [9] is our closest baseline. IBRL first trains an imitation policy from demonstrations and then uses it during online RL to propose candidate actions for exploration and to bootstrap target values in critic updates. We include IBRL because it tests the standard use of imitation as an action prior. In contrast, TIGER does not treat the imitation policy as an action proposal mechanism; it converts imitation-predicted action chunks into controller-aware task-space references and rewards RL for progress toward those references. 

RLPD: Reinforcement Learning with Prior Data (RLPD) [8] is a strong sample-efficient off-policy RL baseline for online learning with offline data. RLPD incorporates demonstrations through symmetric replay sampling, drawing training batches from both the online replay buffer and the offline dataset, and uses critic stabilization such as Layer Normalization to mitigate value over-extrapolation. It therefore tests whether retaining demonstrations as replay data is sufficient for sparse-reward online RL, without explicit imitation objectives, auxiliary rewards, or task-space ref-erence construction. Whenever possible, RLPD uses the same observation space, action space, and off-policy learner as TIGER, so performance differences primarily reflect the effect of TIGER’s imitation-guided task-space reward. 

LaNE: Latent Nearest-demonstration-guided Exploration (LaNE) [11] constructs dense rewards by aligning observations with expert demonstrations in a pretrained visual representation space. We include LaNE because it is a recent sparse-reward visual RL method that also uses demonstrations to provide dense guidance. The comparison is important because LaNE represents the alternative design choice of reward shaping in image/latent observation space, whereas TIGER shapes reward in end-effector task space. This directly tests whether task-space imitation guidance is more suitable for contact-rich manipulation than visual trajectory alignment. 

DSRL: Diffusion Steering via Reinforcement Learning (DSRL) [29] is a recent method for improv-ing pretrained diffusion behavior cloning policies through online RL. Rather than fine-tuning the diffusion policy weights directly, DSRL treats the diffusion policy as a fixed black-box prior and learns to steer its latent noise space toward higher-reward actions. We include DSRL because it is closely aligned with our setting: it improves a frozen imitation policy using RL while preserving the pretrained BC prior. The comparison highlights a key difference between the two approaches. DSRL uses RL to steer the action distribution of a frozen diffusion policy, whereas TIGER uses the imi-tation policy as a task-space guidance mechanism for a separate RL agent through controller-aware reference trajectories, dense progress rewards, and offline pretraining. 

ResFiT: ResFiT [30] is a residual off-policy RL fine-tuning method for behavior-cloning policies. It keeps a pretrained BC policy as the base controller and trains an RL residual to correct its actions. We include ResFiT because it is a strong and conceptually relevant baseline for improving imitation policies with sparse rewards. Unlike TIGER, ResFiT uses imitation as the executable controller and learns residual corrections in action space; TIGER uses imitation as a short-horizon task-space guide while allowing the RL policy to learn its own behavior. 13 Flow Matching: We use a flow-matching imitation policy [31, 3] as the behavior-cloning backbone. The policy learns a generative action-chunk distribution by transporting noise to expert action se-quences, providing an expressive imitation prior for multimodal demonstrations. TIGER uses this policy to generate action chunks for task-space guidance, and we also evaluate the same policy di-rectly as an imitation-only baseline. This isolates the benefit of online RL with imitation-guided task-space rewards from the performance of the underlying generative BC model. 

ReWiND. ReWiND [24] learns a language-conditioned visual reward function ( Rψ (o1, z )) that maps a prefix of image observations ( o1) and a task instruction ( z) to a dense, ( [0 , 1] )-normalized estimate of task progress. In the original training recipe, the reward model is trained on successful language-labeled demonstrations together with a curated subset of Open-X. Matched sequence– instruction pairs receive increasing progress targets, mismatched pairs receive zero-progress targets, and video-rewind augmentation appends reversed frames to a forward trajectory prefix with de-creasing progress targets along the reversed portion. Target-environment instructions are addition-ally augmented with LLM-generated alternatives. The original method uses this progress estimate, together with a success bonus, for offline policy pretraining followed by online RL fine-tuning. 

RoboMeter. RoboMeter [25] is a general-purpose video–language reward model that conditions on a task instruction and a trajectory video to predict dense, per-frame task progress and task-success probabilities. It is trained with per-frame progress and success losses together with a pairwise trajectory-preference loss. Preference pairs include same-task comparisons between executions of different quality, different-task instruction negatives, and synthetic failures produced by video rewinding, allowing the model to use expert demonstrations as well as real and augmented subop-timal trajectories. We include RoboMßeter as a complementary learned reward baseline because its explicit pairwise preference objective differs from ReWiND’s direct progress-prediction objective and TIGER’s task-space progress construction. 

A.1.1 Setup and Description 

We evaluate our approach on simulation benchmarks spanning four MetaWorld tasks [32] (Assem-bly, Box Close, Coffee Push, and Stick Pull), three tasks from the Robomimic benchmark [33] (Can, Square, and ToolHang), and a humanoid shape-sorting insertion task. Coffee Push is included as an additional MetaWorld task in the appendix beyond the three MetaWorld tasks summarized in the main paper. In the humanoid setting (Fig. 8), the robot must insert a cylinder into a shape sorter under tight tolerances and substantial initial-state randomization ( 35 cm × 35 cm for the cylinder and 

4cm × 4cm for the sorter), making standalone imitation brittle. All tasks use a sparse binary success reward except for the humanoid and ToolHang environments, where we define intermediate sparse signals to support curriculum learning. In the humanoid task, the environment provides two sparse signals: lifting the cylinder to a target height and successfully inserting it into the sorter. The final insertion reward remains difficult to discover from random exploration, making the task challenging for standard sparse-reward RL. In Robomimic ToolHang, the task has two stages: assembling the frame by inserting the hook/frame into the stand, and then hanging the tool on the assembled frame. We evaluate two sparse-reward settings. The first provides a reward after the frame-insertion stage is completed, while the second provides a reward only after full task completion, when the tool is successfully hanging on the frame. The second setting follows the original Robomimic success condition, which requires both frame assembly and tool hanging. For ToolHang and humanoid shape sorting, we use curriculum learning shared across all baselines. The curriculum is included to make the long-horizon sparse tasks trainable within the fixed inter-action budget, not as a TIGER-specific advantage. For humanoid insertion, we use a five-stage curriculum that progressively increases the randomization of the insertion setup. For ToolHang, we use a two-level curriculum. In the first level, the behavior-cloning policy executes the first 150 steps, bringing the hook near the stand but not performing the insertion. Control is then transferred to the 14 RL policy. Once a method achieves 50% success on the full task, including hook insertion and tool hanging, we switch to the second level, where the RL policy controls the full episode. For the Robomimic tasks, we use two camera views per task. For MetaWorld and humanoid shape sorting, we use a single camera view. In the humanoid task, we use a one-dimensional action for hand posture. Across all tasks, all methods use relative end-effector control. We additionally conduct a targeted learned-reward comparison on MetaWorld Assembly, Box Close, and Stick Pull and Robomimic Square. ReWiND, Robometer, and TIGER use the same task-specific demonstrations, RLPD backend, interaction budget, and five-seed protocol; no additional simulation environments are introduced for this comparison. We also evaluate on all ten LIBERO-Spatial tasks [34]. These simulated tabletop tasks share the objective of placing a black bowl on a plate but vary the bowl’s initial location or spatial relationship to the surrounding objects. We consider settings with 10 and 50 demonstrations per task and train one shared multi-task BC policy and one shared multi-task RL policy per method across all ten tasks, using a one-hot task identifier. Training and evaluation use the same task set, so this experiment measures multi-task learning rather than unseen-task generalization.

This section details the real-robot task setups used in our experiments. A key objective of the study is to evaluate learning across comparatively large workspaces, where early-stage exploration can occa-sionally drive the robot into unsafe or uninformative regions. Workspace limits are task-dependent and are therefore enforced at the environment level to provide consistent training signals and to explicitly measure safety violations, consistent with common practice in real-world RL implemen-tations. If the end-effector breaches these limits for several consecutive steps, the episode is termi-nated and the environment is reset, without applying any additional penalty reward. Commanded actions are also down-scaled by the high-level controller before being translated into low-level con-trol signals. 

A.2.1 Block Picking Description. The objective is to grasp a solid red block. At the start of each episode, the block pose is uniformly sampled inside a 25 cm ×30 cm workspace that is fully visible to the wrist camera when the robot is at its nominal home configuration. We collected ten human teleoperation demonstra-tions, and each episode is capped at 100 environment steps. Controller action scales are (0 .05 , 0.20) 

for Cartesian position and orientation commands, respectively. 

Operational workspace. Cartesian positions are specified relative to the initial end-effector pose, whereas Cartesian orientations (roll–pitch–yaw) are absolute in the world frame: 

plow = [ −0.19 , −0.35 , −0.90] m,

phigh = [ 0 .16 , 0.35 , 0.05 ] m,

θlow = [ 0 .75 π, 0.0, 0.0 ] rad ,

θhigh = [ π, 0.25 π, π ] rad .

These bounds define the task-specific safety region used for training, evaluation, and violation count-ing. 

Reset. After an episode terminates, the robot opens the gripper, releasing the object if it was grasped, and returns to its home pose. The block is manually repositioned within the defined workspace. Dur-ing training, following [9], the block is initially placed near the center of the region; after the robot achieves several successful grasps, the initial block pose is gradually moved toward the workspace boundaries. 

Reward. The task uses an automated binary reward. Success is signaled only when all of the following conditions are met: (i) the gripper is closed beyond a tightening threshold, (ii) the object is held stably without slipping for at least three consecutive time steps, and (iii) the object is lifted at least 2 cm above its initial height. 

A.2.2 Drawer Opening Description. In the Drawer Opening task, the policy must pull open the second drawer in a stack of plastic drawers. The drawers have small handles that are difficult for the gripper to grasp; other drawers may be opened but yield no reward. The task requires fine, smooth contact control and tests whether sparse-reward RL can use demonstration-derived guidance to reliably engage the correct handle. We collected ten teleoperation demonstrations. Controller action scales are (0 .05 , 0.20) for Cartesian position and orientation commands, respectively, and each episode is capped at 100 steps. 17 Operational workspace. Reward. A binary reward is issued once the second drawer, marked with red tape, is detected as fully open by an auxiliary reward camera. Partial openings or openings of other drawers do not yield reward. 

A.2.3 Rotary Insertion Description. The Rotary Insertion task requires the robot to pick up a blue object and insert it into a designated hole marked in white. The insertion point can rotate around the vertical axis, and the object’s initial position is randomized within a 15 cm × 15 cm region. The task is challenging because it combines long-horizon grasping, spatial search, target tracking, and precise contact-rich insertion. We collected 50 teleoperation demonstrations. Controller action scales are (0 .05 , 1.5) for Cartesian position and orientation commands, respectively, and each episode is capped at 150 steps. To improve learning stability, we use curriculum learning during training. In each curriculum stage, the disk orientation is advanced by 45 ◦, and the policy must successfully complete the task five times before progressing. Previous stages are sampled non-uniformly to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. After completing the full rotation cycle, we randomize the insertion location; in the final stage, the policy must insert the object while the disk is actively rotating.

plow = [ −0.25 , −0.35 , −0.10] m,

These bounds define the task-specific safety region used for training, evaluation, and violation count-ing. 

Reset. At episode termination, the robot opens the gripper, releasing the object if it was grasped, and returns to the initial end-effector pose. The object is then placed at a random position following the same center-out curriculum used in Block Picking. 

Reward. A binary reward is given once an infrared sensor on the rotating device detects that the object is fully seated in the insertion hole; the episode then terminates immediately.

The mapping models only low-order, time-invariant action-to-motion effects that arise from con-troller preprocessing and finite execution bandwidth. It is not intended to predict contact transitions, force interactions, object motion, or task-specific physics. We use a linear model intentionally be-cause (i) it admits a closed-form fit that is robust at the demonstration sample sizes typical for sparse-reward manipulation (5–50 demos); (ii) the mapping is used only to construct local guidance, while online RL optimizes the true environment reward; and (iii) the dense reward in Eq. In contact-rich phases, the model should therefore be interpreted as correcting systematic controller-induced attenuation or delay in the commanded end-effector motion, not as predicting the outcome of the contact interaction itself.

## E Network Architectures and Baseline Implementation Details 

This appendix describes the network architectures and baseline implementation choices used in our experiments. TIGER, IBRL, and RLPD use the same IBRL-style actor–critic architecture [9], consisting of a lightweight ViT image encoder followed by actor and critic heads. TIGER differs from these baselines only through the augmented target-pose observation, the imitation-guided re-ward, and the offline value-pretraining objective. For external baselines, we follow the original architectures and hyperparameters whenever possible, and modify only the task-interface parame-ters required to match our observation space, action space, controller frequency, and demonstration format. 

E.1 IBRL-Style Networks (used by TIGER / IBRL / RLPD) 

TIGER, IBRL, and RLPD use the same IBRL-style actor–critic architecture. The network consists of a lightweight ViT encoder for 96 ×96 RGB observations, followed by separate actor and critic heads. The critic uses the token-wise fusion architecture from IBRL: each visual channel is combined with proprioception and action before aggregation, allowing the Q-function to condition visual features on the candidate action. The actor instead flattens the visual tokens, compresses them to a compact visual feature, concatenates proprioception, and outputs a bounded 7-D action. E.2 External Baselines 

For LaNE, ResFiT, and DSRL, we use the original implementations and default hyperparameters un-less otherwise stated. When a baseline admits multiple reasonable settings for our action-chunking setup, we evaluate the alternatives and report the stronger-performing configuration. This gives each baseline the benefit of a task-compatible implementation while preserving the original method design. 

LaNE. For LaNE [11], we use the default architecture, optimization settings, and hyperparameters from the original implementation. We only adapt the environment interface to match our observa-tion and action spaces. In particular, LaNE is trained with the same action parameterization and environment-step budget. The LaNE latent dynamics model uses latent dimension 16 and action dimension 7. The DINOv2-based encoder takes two 3×112 ×112 random crops as input and uses the dinov2_vits14_reg 

backbone. The two DINOv2 embeddings are concatenated and passed through three 512 -D ReLU MLP layers, followed by a linear layer producing 32 outputs, corresponding to a 16 -D mean and 

16 -D log standard deviation. The decoder mirrors this MLP and predicts two 768 -D DINOv2 em-beddings. For the non-DINOv2 image encoder, LaNE uses stacked convolutional layers followed by two 128 -D ReLU MLP layers and a 32 -D output. The locally linear dynamics model predicts 

u ∈ R16 , v ∈ R16 , B ∈ R16 ×7, and offset c ∈ R16 . The RL encoder maps 6 × 112 × 112 image inputs to a 32 -D feature, followed by actor and critic heads with two 1024 -D hidden layers. 

ResFiT. For ResFiT [30], we use the default architecture and training hyperparameters from the original implementation, adapting only the task interface to our observation and action spaces. In simulation, we use the standard ResFiT warmup protocol, including 10 k environment warmup steps and 10 k critic warmup updates before online actor–critic training. In simulation, we evaluate ResFiT with the default actor learning rate 10 −6 and with a larger actor learning rate 10 −5, and report the better-performing configuration for each task. We include this sweep because the default actor update was overly conservative in several of our settings. We also observed that ResFiT is sensitive to random seed in several sparse-reward tasks. This effect was most pronounced in the humanoid shape-sorting task, where some seeds failed to discover successful be-havior while others reached high final success. We therefore report ResFiT using the stronger of the two actor learning rates evaluated above, while retaining the same number of seeds and evaluation protocol used for the other methods. For real-robot experiments, we do not use environment warmup steps, so that all methods are com-pared under the same online interaction budget. We use 1k critic warmup updates before actor–critic training and set the actor learning rate to 10 −5, which was the stronger and more stable configuration in preliminary runs. 

DSRL. For DSRL [29], we use DSRL-SAC as the primary implementation. DSRL fine-tunes a frozen behavior-cloning policy by learning an RL policy in the latent noise space of the BC model, rather than directly learning residuals in the executed action space. For chunked policies, the full latent-noise action has dimension C × d, where C is the action chunk size and d is the per-step noise dimension. Training an actor and critic over this full high-dimensional noise vector can be difficult. Following the DSRL paper, we first evaluated the recommended single-step noise parameterization, where SAC learns a policy over wsingle ∈ Rd and repeats this same noise vector across the chunk dimension before querying the frozen BC policy. 30 In our setting, this repeated-noise parameterization performed poorly with the long-horizon BC pol-icy used by TIGER, which predicts chunks with horizon H = 20 and action step As = 10 . We therefore trained a shorter-horizon BC policy on the same data with H = 4 and As = 4 and re-ran DSRL under this setting. The shorter chunk reduces the dimensionality of the latent-noise control problem and allows DSRL to predict distinct per-step noise variables across the four-step chunk, rather than repeating a single noise vector over a long horizon. In simulation, we evaluate both DSRL-SAC and DSRL-NA with this H = 4 , As = 4 BC policy and report the better-performing variant. For real-robot experiments, we also use the H = 4 , As = 4 BC policy, since this con-figuration performed better than the longer-horizon H = 20 , As = 10 policy for DSRL in our setup. 

Flow-matching imitation policy. Our imitation policy uses an ACT-style [1] visual encoder fol-lowed by a conditional 1D UNet flow-matching action head. Each camera image is processed in-dependently by an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbone with frozen batch normalization. The resulting 512 -channel feature map is projected to 256 channels with a 1×1 convolution and spatially average-pooled to a 256 -D vector. Robot proprioception is projected to the same dimension with a linear layer. The camera features and proprioceptive feature are concatenated and passed through a small MLP with LayerNorm, ReLU, and dropout to produce a 128 -D conditioning vector. The action head predicts action chunks of length H. In our experiments, H = 20 for all tasks, and we execute the first 10 actions from each chunk before replanning. The head is a conditional 1D UNet operating along the chunk horizon. It uses base width 128 , three resolution levels with channel multipliers (1 , 2, 2) , and FiLM conditioning from the concatenated visual conditioning vector and diffusion-time embedding. Each residual block contains GroupNorm, SiLU activations, Conv1d layers, dropout, and a residual skip connection. The final layer projects the UNet output back to the action dimension. We train the policy with conditional flow matching. Given an expert action chunk xstart and Gaus-sian noise ϵ, we sample t ∼ U (0 , 1) and form 

xt = tx start + (1 − t)ϵ. 

The network predicts the target velocity 

v⋆ = xstart − ϵ

using an MSE loss. At inference time, we initialize the chunk from Gaussian noise and integrate the learned flow using 10 Euler steps on a linear t ∈ [0 , 1] schedule. 31
