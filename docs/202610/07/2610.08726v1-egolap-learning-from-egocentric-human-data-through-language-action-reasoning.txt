EgoLAP: Learning from Egocentric Human
Data through Language-Action Reasoning
Lihan Zha1∗, Shresth Grover1∗, Tenny Yin1, Samuel M. Bateman1, Hengkai Pan1, Mengchao
Zhang2, Aykut Onol2, Allen Z. Ren3, Dhruv Shah1,†, Anirudha Majumdar1,†
1Princeton University, 2Toyota Research Institute, 3Physical Intelligence
∗Equal contribution., †Equal advising.
Egocentric human data offer a path to scaling robot learning beyond costly robot demonstrations, yet
the embodiment gap makes raw human trajectories a poor supervisory target for control. Our key
insight is that, although low-level actions are embodiment-specific, their underlying motion intent can
capture task-relevant structure that transfers across humans and robots. We introduce EgoLAP, a
VLA pre-training framework that jointly learns from human and robot trajectories through a shared
language-based action chain-of-thought. EgoLAP expresses motion intent as structured, temporally
abstracted language actions and pairs them with motion-level reasoning grounded in scene geometry,
physics, and object affordances. Across extensive real-world and simulated experiments, EgoLAP
transfers human experience to robot control more effectively than alternative action representations
and reaches 80.1% mean real-world task progress, a 2.3× performance gain over alternative action
representations. Motion-level reasoning also outperforms a composite reasoning format that combines
subtask, object-box, and visual-trace reasoning.

INTRODUCTION
Figure 1 EgoLAP turns egocentric human experience into transferable robot control. Human hands and robots
differ in embodiment and low-level execution, yet share the same underlying motion intent when performing a task.
EgoLAP captures this intent through motion-level reasoning and language actions, providing a shared supervision
target across both data sources and leading to a 2.3× performance gain over alternative action representations.
Robot foundation models have advanced rapidly by scaling robot demonstrations across tasks, environments,
and embodiments [1–6], yet such demonstrations remain costly to collect and narrow relative to the breadth
of human activity. Egocentric human video offers the scale and behavioral diversity that robot data lacks,
capturing long-horizon manipulation in everyday environments and growing independently of any robot
platform.

To leverage human data for robot learning, prior work extracts intermediate representations through hand-
engineered pipelines, such as interaction hotspots and affordances [7, 8], hand poses or image-space tracks [9–12],
latent plans [13–15], or visually edited embodiments [16–18]. These representations capture useful facets of
human behavior, but inherit the inductive biases and failure modes of their extraction pipelines that restricts
scalability. Moreover, differences in morphology, kinematics, and control create an embodiment gap that
limits the direct transfer of action-level knowledge from human data to robot policies [19].
Our key insight is that humans and robots need not share low-level actions; instead, they need to share
motion intent: what motion needs to be produced, and why it’s appropriate given the current physical state.
We represent the what using language actions: structured, temporally abstract descriptions of changes in
pose and gripper state, such as “move forward 3 cm, rotate clockwise 14 degrees, and close gripper.” Unlike
embodiment-specific trajectories or non-linguistic action tokens [20, 21], language actions reuse the vocabulary
and compositional structure of a vision–language model (VLM), thereby providing control-relevant supervision
within its pretrained semantic space [22–24]. However, the same task may require different motions across
embodiments, so specifying what motion to execute is insufficient. We therefore introduce motion-level
reasoning to represent the why: local, physically grounded rationales that relate contact, geometry, and
object affordances to the intended effect of the next motion. Unlike subtask plans or visual traces, which
primarily specify what action to take or where to move [25–27], motion-level reasoning captures the physical
relationship between the current state and the motion required to achieve the intended effect, providing a
form of supervision that can connect directly to low-level actions, yet transfer across embodiments. Together,
motion-level reasoning and language actions define a physically grounded “action chain-of-thought”.
Statement of contributions. We introduce EgoLAP, a VLA pre-training framework that co-trains on
egocentric human and robot trajectories through motion-level reasoning and language actions, achieving 44.9
points of real-world performance more than the strongest action representation. Unlike previous embodied
reasoning methods [25, 26], EgoLAP only runs a lightweight action expert during inference time, where the
textual targets provide auxiliary supervision and require no autoregressive generation. Across extensive
zero-shot real-world and simulation experiments on a bimanual robot, language actions transfer human
experience better than non-linguistic action representations [20, 21], and motion-level reasoning contributes
44.7 points of real-world task progress more to EgoLAP than the baselines. Motion-level reasoning also
strongly outperforms a strong composite of subtask, object-box, and visual-trace reasoning supervision. Finally,
EgoLAP adapts effectively through reinforcement learning over language actions and motion-level reasoning.

RELATED WORK
Learning Robot Policies from Egocentric Human Data.
Human data provides scalable supervision for robot
learning, but leveraging them requires bridging the embodiment gap. Prior methods rely on method-specific
representations: interaction hotspots and affordances [7, 8], hand poses or image-space tracks [9–12], latent
plans [13–15], or visually edited embodiments [16–18]. Although effective, these hand-designed interfaces
require modular processing (e.g., hand-pose estimation, point tracking, or embodiment editing) and inherit its
inductive biases and errors, limiting their scalability. The gap is evident in direct policy learning: despite the
3,000 hours of egocentric video available in Ego4D [28], MimicPlay and Motion Tracks train on only about 10
minutes of task-specific human video, ATM evaluates transfer from 100 human videos, and Phantom trains
on 250–350 demonstrations per task [10, 12, 13, 17].
Another line directly trains policies on large-scale egocentric human datasets [29, 30] along with robot
data [31–38]. These systems typically retain embodiment-specific pose targets or encode trajectories with
non-linguistic action tokens as training targets [20, 39, 40], which impede cross-embodiment transfer [22].
Language-Action Pre-training (LAP) [22] instead expresses motion as structured natural language, showing
improved zero-shot transfer among robots. EgoLAP extends this representation to human–robot co-training
and pairs it with motion-level reasoning, which provides both embodiments with shared language-based
targets and improves human-to-robot transfer.
Action Representations for VLAs.
VLAs commonly supervise a VLM with discretized action tokens [4, 5],
sometimes alongside a separate continuous action expert [3]. The discrete target is especially consequential
when gradients from the action expert do not reach the VLM backbone [41]. Learned tokenizers such as

FAST, OAT, ActionCodec, and X-Tokenizer optimize action compression or token structure [20, 21, 42, 43];
LAP instead describes motion in natural language to remain compatible with the VLM’s pretrained semantic

EgoLAP applies this representation to bimanual human–robot co-training and shows that
motion-level reasoning yields larger gains with language actions than with non-linguistic action tokens.
Reasoning for Visuomotor Control.
Prior work augments visuomotor policies with textual reasoning
over goals, subtask plans, and spatial cues, or with visual intermediates such as affordances and 2D point
traces [25–27, 44–47]. With human video, plans, reasoning labels, and dense annotations improve policy
performance under distribution shift [48–51]. These results establish the value of reasoning supervision, but
leave unresolved which reasoning content best supports cross-embodiment motion transfer. For example,
DeMiAn finds that no single annotation type performs best across tasks [50], while a large-scale study finds
no benefit from conditioning actions on chain-of-thought traces [52].
This gap motivates motion-level reasoning: supervision that captures the embodiment-invariant physical
rationale underlying human and robot motions, thereby bridging the two data sources. Unlike prior ap-
proaches [25, 47], EgoLAP requires neither multi-stage distillation nor text generation at inference, simplifying
training and ensuring fast inference.
3
METHOD
EgoLAP is a VLA pre-training framework for transferring motion knowledge from large-scale egocentric human
experience to robot manipulation. Its central design choice is to map human-hand and robot-end-effector
trajectories to the same language-based interface. We first formalize this interface (Sec. 3.1) and describe
the model architecture and training objective (Sec. 3.2). We then detail the construction of language actions
(Sec. 3.3) and motion-level reasoning targets (Sec. 3.4).
3.1
Problem Formulation
Let DR contain robot samples (ot, ℓ, st, at:t+K), where ot contains one or more RGB observations, ℓis the
task instruction, st is the proprioceptive state, and at:t+K is an action chunk of horizon K. Let DH contain
egocentric human samples (ot, ℓ, qt:t+K), where q denotes one or two tracked hand poses in the head-camera
frame. For human samples, the current pose qt supplies the state input st, and the relative motion between
successive poses defines the continuous action chunk at:t+K.
Each sample provides up to three training signals. First, a deterministic canonicalizer Ge, specialized only by
embodiment metadata e — single arm robot, bimanual robot, or human data, produces a shared discrete

action-token target mt:
mt = Ge(qt:t+K)
or
mt = Ge(at:t+K, st) .
(1)
In EgoLAP, mt is a language action summarizing one second of hand or end-effector motion in an egocentric
frame (Fig. 2; Sec. 3.3). Controlled baselines replace these discrete action tokens with FAST or OAT tokens
while preserving the remaining training recipe. Second, a subset of samples includes a motion rationale rt
that grounds mt in visible spatial relations, contact evidence, object affordances, and the intended physical
effect of the motion (Fig. 2; Sec. 3.4). Third, the continuous action chunk at:t+K directly supervises real-time
control.
The model consists of a vision–language backbone fθ and a continuous action expert vϕ. The backbone
predicts mt and, when available, rt on both human and robot data, thereby aligning their motion semantics.
The action expert learns at:t+K through flow matching. We stop action-loss gradients at the backbone
boundary [41], insulating the language-pretrained representation from the randomly initialized expert. At
inference, only the action expert is rolled out to generate control commands; neither a language action nor a
rationale is decoded.

Training objective. Let D denote the action dimension. The action expert, parameterized by flow matching [3],
predicts at:t+K ∈RK×D in delta end-effector pose space, and is optimized by a flow matching loss LFM. Let
Ltext denote the expected masked token-level objective for language action and motion-level reasoning, and
let λtext weight the two objectives. The complete training loss is
L = E(xt,at:t+K,mt,rt)∼pmix [λtextLtext + LFM]
(2)
where pmix is the fixed human–robot sampling distribution. In our model, K=30 is the action horizon. We
set λtext=0.8, slightly downweighting the autoregressive VLM objective relative to continuous flow matching
to align their convergence speed.

At inference, the action expert starts from Gaussian noise at τ=1 and integrates its learned velocity field to
τ=0, conditioned only on the current observation, instruction, and state. Because the textual targets are
absent from the expert’s attention context, EgoLAP retains real-time continuous control without test-time
language generation. Appendix A.3 gives the complete flow-matching and inference specification, while
Appendix A.4 defines the cross-entropy loss Ltext.
3.3
Language Actions
Following LAP [22], we supervise the VLM backbone with language actions: deterministic natural-language
descriptions of hand or end-effector motion over a fixed horizon. Any trajectory with known Cartesian poses
can be converted automatically; this conversion requires no manual labeling, VLM labeling, or learned action
tokenizer.

Reinforcement learning on language actions. Because Ge is deterministic and the grammar is machine-
parseable, a language action is a verifiable target: any sampled completion can be parsed and scored against
the demonstrated motion with no learned reward model. We use this to post-train EgoLAP with GRPO [56],
updating only the language backbone while the vision encoder and action expert stay frozen. Appendix A.9
gives the reward and full protocol, and results are presented in Sec.4.5.
3.4
Motion-Level Reasoning
A language action specifies what motion occurs, but not why that motion is appropriate in the current physical
state. We therefore annotate subsets of human and robot trajectories with a motion rationale rt. Given the
task instruction, ordered trajectory frames, recent action history, and the ground-truth language action at the
current frame, we produce a rationale that identifies a concrete end-effector–object relation, relevant contact
or affordance evidence, and the immediate physical effect of the labeled motion. For example, a rationale for
kneading dough might read:
5


The dough mass rests at the base of the metal bowl, with both palms directly contacting the top and sides
of the dough. Kneading requires deforming the dough against the bowl surface. Both hands translate
dominantly backward while tilting wrists up and opening fingers, pulling across the compliant dough toward
the body before the next pressing stroke.
Motion-level reasoning is deliberately local and physics-focused. It differs from the high-level task or subtask
reasoning studied in prior work [25–27, 47]: it lives on a lower hierarchy and directly connects to low-level
actions, describing the underlying motion intent shared between embodiments. Appendix A.5 describes the
annotation procedure, and Appendix D provides generated robot and human examples together with the
labeling prompt. We apply reasoning dropout and reasoning loss dropout during training, which is discussed
in Appendix A.4.
4
EXPERIMENTS
We organize the evaluation around four questions: 1. Which action representation is most effective when
co-training with human data? 2. Does motion-level reasoning improve transfer from human data to robots,
and are motion-level reasoning and language actions mutually reinforcing? 3. How does motion-level reasoning
compare to established reasoning formats? 4. Can EgoLAP be effectively adapted to downstream tasks using
reinforcement learning?
4.1
Experimental Design
Zero-shot evaluation. In the real world, we evaluate our models and baselines on a customized bimanual
YAM robot (Fig. 4 (b)) whose arm placement (e.g., distance between the two arms) and camera configuration
differ from every training embodiment. We test five behaviors spanning rigid, articulated, and deformable
manipulation: Pick Place (Simple), Fold Pants, Handover & Place, Wipe Object, and Pick Place (Complex).
We run 10 trials per task, except Pick Place (Complex), for which we run 12 trials, and report mean task
progress. Appendix B describes each task and gives the complete scoring rubric. Error bar is reported as ±1
SEM.
We complement the real-world experiments with a bimanual simulation benchmark built upon ABC [57],
including 14 tasks that span picking, placing, open-ended language following, and cloth folding. Representative
tasks are visualized in Fig. 4 (a). Notably, none of our models is trained on simulation data, so this is a
zero-shot simulation evaluation. We evaluate each model for 100 episodes per task, 1,400 episodes in total,
and report the mean success rate for each task group. Appendix B reports the suite composition and full
evaluation protocol.
Baselines. We instantiate three policy families differing only in action representation. EgoLAP uses language
actions and motion-level reasoning; EgoFAST replaces language actions with FAST tokens [20] and uses the
same motion-level reasoning; and EgoOAT instead uses OAT tokens [21] plus motion-level reasoning. We
re-train the OAT tokenizer on our data mixture. In addition, we compare to LAP [22], which is EgoLAP
trained without reasoning and without human data, as shown in Fig. 5. In Sec.4.4, we compare EgoLAP with
our reimplemented ECoT baselines, which replaces motion-level reasoning with subtask plan, bounding box
and visual trace reasoning. Appendix A.8 discusses the implementation details for baselines.
Unless stated otherwise, all variants share everything except the action representation — same backbone,
same architecture, same data mixture, same data pre-processing (e.g., egocentric canonicalization in Sec. 3.3),
same optimization budget, and same evaluation protocol. All action representations correspond to the same
one-second action chunk. We train every variant for 40k steps and select its checkpoint with the same validation
procedure before the final evaluation; the only exception is EgoLAP, which converges faster and we train it up
to 30k steps (Appendix C). Appendix A reports the shared training recipe and data mixture. Notably, within
our matched implementation, EgoFAST reproduces the discrete action-token interface of π0.5 [3], differing
primarily in its training mixture. We also compare with the released checkpoint for ABC-VLA [57], which is
trained directly on the ABC YAM platform.
No publicly released multi-task world-action model supports the bimanual YAM platform used in our evaluation,
so we do not include WAMs in the zero-shot comparison.

We first show that EgoLAP benefits from human data. Holding the

We next compare action representations. Changing only the action rep-
resentation and using no reasoning, EgoLAP reaches 16.4% success in
simulation and 44.5% progress in the real world, against 1.6% and 35.2%
for EgoFAST and 2.7% and 18.0% for EgoOAT. The margin is largest
exactly where the domain gap is biggest: on the unseen simulator, lan-

strongly suggests that language actions transfer human experience to robot
tasks better than non-linguistic action tokenizers do, even without any
reasoning supervision. We believe this is because language actions align
with the VLM’s pre-training distribution, so human and robot motions
that share an intent are described by the same words and land in the same
region of a representation space the backbone already understands. The
full EgoLAP policy also beats ABC-VLA, which is trained directly on the
data from the same simulation environment, by 16.9 points in simulation
and 54.5 points in the real world. This strongly suggests EgoLAP overcomes an in-domain data advantage
and generalizes better by transferring human data knowledge.

4.3
Motion-Level Reasoning and Language Actions Are Mutually Reinforcing
We further compare each EgoX (no reasoning) policy with its reasoning-supervised counterpart. Motion-level
reasoning raises EgoLAP from 44.5% to 80.1% mean progress in the real world, a 35.6-point gain that holds
on every one of the five tasks (Fig. 4 (b)), and from 16.4% to 38.3% in simulation, a 21.9-point gain that
holds on every task family (Fig. 4 (a)). This first suggests that motion-level reasoning is very helpful on its
own. The same supervision, however, adds only 7.1 points to EgoFAST in simulation and in fact hurts it
in the real world, dropping it from 35.2% to 26.1%. EgoOAT benefits more modestly, gaining 0.7 points in
simulation and 7.6 points in the real world.
This gap strongly suggests a synergy between language actions and motion-level reasoning. As shown in
Fig. 2, the two form a single textual “action chain-of-thought”, moving from reasoning about why a motion is
appropriate, grounded in visuals and physics, to the actual motion to execute. Non-linguistic action tokens
like FAST and OAT lack this semantic correspondence, leaving the backbone to infer the correlation from
co-occurrence alone, without transferring the knowledge already in the VLM backbone.

We next ask whether the benefit comes from motion-level
reasoning specifically or from reasoning tokens in general. We
compare against a strong composite target in the style of
ECoT [25], which predicts the current subtask, visible-object

Motion-level reasoning helps every target we tried (Fig. 6): it
adds 7.1 points to FAST, 9.1 points to the ECoT composite,
and 21.9 points to plain language actions. The composite itself,
however, does not help. On its own it reaches 5.3%, well below

Because all action-representation variants are trained on the
same trajectories with identical action-conditioned rationales,
and the ECoT-style control retains the same language-action
target while adding subtask, box, and future-trace supervision,
EgoLAP’s substantially larger gain cannot be attributed to
privileged action information or merely to additional auxiliary
supervision; it specifically demonstrates the effectiveness of pairing motion-level rationales with a language-
based action interface.

We construct the language action reward by summing the
negative squared translation, geodesic rotation, and a binary gripper
error of the parsed language action. This can be seen as an indirect
behavior cloning alignment reward of the language actions. We test
three variations: reasoning pre-training without reasoning generation
(i.e., only generates language actions during sampling), reasoning pre-

reasoning). Appendix A.9 gives the reward, objective, and full protocol.
Even with a frozen expert, RL improves simulation success in all three
arms (Fig. 7). Comparing the orange bar and the blue bar suggests
reasoning pre-training supplies a stronger initialization for a stronger
post-RL policy. Secondly, generating reasoning during RL sampling
hurts performance relative to only sampling language actions (+12.7%
vs. +19.4%): ABC rationales were never seen in training, so the sampled
rationales are out of distribution, which makes the subsequent language
actions generation also worse. We leave investigating how to better
leverage motion-level reasoning for RL in future works.

CONCLUSION
We introduce EgoLAP, a VLA pre-training framework that transfers egocentric human experience to robots
through a shared language of motion. By pairing language actions that describe what motion to produce with
physically grounded reasoning that explains why, EgoLAP reaches 80.1% real-world task progress and 38.3%
simulation success on bimanual manipulation, substantially outperforming alternative action representations
and reasoning formats. Beyond policy performance, motion-level reasoning improves cross-embodiment skill
retrieval by up to 25 points, suggesting that it organizes human and robot experience by shared skill structure
without erasing embodiment identity. EgoLAP also adapts effectively through reinforcement learning, while
its textual targets remain training-only and add no generation cost during control.
Limitations and future work. Language actions currently summarize fixed one-second windows with quantized
9


translation and rotation, which may miss the dynamics or precision required for fast, contact-rich manipulation.
Their linguistic form, however, can naturally support adaptive precision: future representations could combine
coarse, long-horizon descriptions with fine, high-frequency corrections, while tolerating approximate labels
from pose tracking, UMI, or internet video. Our evaluation remains focused on bimanual tabletop tasks.
Finally, our reinforcement-learning study is confined to simulation and optimizes a motion-matching proxy
rather than task reward; future work should jointly post-train motion-level reasoning and the action expert
using task-level feedback.

AI Use Statement
We used large language models to edit and polish the manuscript. We also used Google Gemini to generate
offline motion-level reasoning labels: gemini-3.5-flash for DROID and gemini-3.7-flash for MECKA.
These labels serve as training targets for the reasoning-enabled policies; Appendix A.5 documents the inputs
and annotation procedure, and Appendix D provides the prompts and representative outputs. The authors
designed and checked the labeling pipeline, reviewed the AI-assisted manuscript edits, and take responsibility
for all content, claims, results, and AI-assisted artifacts in this work.
Reproducibility Statement
We provide the methodological and experimental details needed to reproduce our results. Section 3 specifies
the model, objectives, shared motion representation, and motion-level reasoning formulation, while Section 4.1
describes the matched comparison protocol. Appendix A documents the architecture, prompt and action
serialization, flow-matching objective, reasoning-annotation pipeline, training hyperparameters, data mixture,
and baseline implementations. Appendix B specifies the evaluation tasks and number of episodes, Appendix A.9
gives the reinforcement-learning setup, and Appendix C reports additional results and checkpoint-selection
details. The complete Gemini labeling prompts and representative annotations are included in Appendix D.

Figure 8 Detailed EgoLAP architecture. A shared SigLIP encoder maps each camera view to visual tokens. The 2B

Let B be the batch size and V the number of enabled camera slots. Before tokenization, a sample contains

Each 224 × 224 image produces 256 SigLIP patch tokens. The normalized state is discretized into 256 uniform
bins and serialized with the instruction, so it is part of the text prefix rather than a separate action-expert
token. For EgoLAP, the padded text tensor has shape B × 320. Thus, with three views, the maximum VLM
prefix has shape B × (3 · 256 + 320) × 2048. Padding and absent-image tokens are removed by the attention
mask. The VLM has 18 transformer blocks, width 2048, an FFN width of 16,384, eight query heads, and one
key–value head.

A.5
Motion-Level Reasoning Annotation
Coverage. Motion-level reasoning labels exist for only two of the datasets in our mixture: the MECKA subset
of EgoVerse [29], which supplies all of our egocentric human reasoning, and DROID [2], which supplies all of
our robot reasoning. Together they account for 32% of each training batch (Fig. 9). Every other dataset —
ABC YAM, the remaining bimanual robot datasets, the Scale and Aria subsets of EgoVerse, and the remaining
OXE datasets — contributes language actions and continuous actions but no rationale. Those samples take
B=C=0 in Eq. 8 and are therefore trained exactly as in the no-reasoning variants. The same two annotated
sources are used for every reasoning policy we report, including EgoFAST and EgoOAT, so reasoning coverage
is never a confound between action representations.
Annotation procedure.
We generate the annotations offline with gemini-3.5-flash for DROID and
gemini-3.7-flash for MECKA. Both runs use temperature 0. Gemini is given the task instruction and a
temporally ordered set of action anchors. The target language action is an input to the annotation procedure
to provide Gemini with the action context, which we empirically find to lead to more grounded reasoning labels
compared to only providing images. Appendix D shows representative DROID and MECKA annotations
together with the labeling prompt.
18


For DROID, anchors are formed from adaptive action segments with a nominal stride of 15 filtered steps
(approximately one second). A segment can end early at a persistent gripper-state change or a large change
in translational direction. At each anchor, Gemini receives the current primary and wrist-camera images,
the current gripper and transition metadata, the language action computed from the numeric actions over
that segment, and a compact text-only summary of up to five seconds of preceding actions. The prompt
distinguishes the state visible at the anchor from the command to be executed over the following segment,
which prevents a future grasp, release, or contact event from being described as already complete.
For MECKA, subsequences belonging to the same high-level recording are first concatenated in their recorded
order. Then, adaptive anchors are selected with a separation of 15–30 frames at 30 Hz. Each anchor contains
the head-mounted egocentric image, the high-level instruction and stitched subtask context, the segment-level
bimanual language action, and the same compact action history. Since MECKA records human manipulation,
the prompt maps the shared action vocabulary to left and right hands, fingers, contact, support, and release
rather than robot grippers.
Gemini outputs a trajectory summary, a subtask plan, and, for every requested anchor, the completed,
current, and next subtasks; observation–action grounding; the main rationale; a visual-relation sentence; a
motion-effect sentence; and an alternative-motion diagnostic. However, during training, we only use the main
motion-level reasoning rationale.

A.8
Baseline Implementation Details
All matched policies use the architecture in Fig. 8, the continuous 30 × 14 flow-matching target, the data
mixture in Fig. 9, and the optimization settings in Table 4. FAST and OAT change only the autoregressive
action target. Their action tokens supervise the VLM branch; they do not replace the continuous action
expert. The expert therefore receives the same observations and continuous targets for every representation.
FAST. We use the released FAST tokenizer [20] to encode each normalized 30 × 14 action chunk. In the
no-reasoning policy, the FAST tokens directly follow the observation–instruction–state prefix. In the reasoning
policy, the motion rationale is placed before the codes. The tokenizer is fixed while the policy is trained.
OAT. We separately train the OAT codec [21] on normalized 30 × 14 action chunks sampled from the same
mixture used for policy pretraining, and freeze it during policy training. In our configuration, the codec
represents each action chunk with a maximum-length sequence of 16 ordered discrete tokens. Since OAT is
trained with power-of-two prefix dropout, any prefix of 1, 2, 4, 8, or 16 tokens can reconstruct the complete
action chunk, with later tokens progressively refining the reconstruction. The encoder and decoder have
hidden width 256, eight attention heads, and six Transformer blocks each, with dropout 0.1. Each latent is
quantized using finite scalar quantization with levels (8, 8, 6, 5), yielding 8 × 8 × 6 × 5 = 1,920 possible codes
per token. We train the codec for 100,000 updates and use the same frozen checkpoint for both OAT policy
20


variants. In the reasoning variant, reasoning tokens precede the OAT action tokens; the no-reasoning variant
predicts the OAT action tokens directly.
ECoT-style reasoning [25]. We implement the composite baseline in Sec. 4.4 as an ordered prefix containing
subtask context, visible-object boxes, and a visual trace, followed by the same language action used by
EgoLAP. The subtask context is reused from the offline Gemini annotations of Appendix A.5: for DROID, we
extract the standardized full-plan and current-subtask sentences from each annotated segment; for MECKA,
we read the corresponding structured fields after stitching subsequences from the same recording. We obtain
object boxes in a separate offline grounding pass: given an anchor image, the task instruction, and the current

The visual traces are computed from trajectories and camera parameters directly, rather than generated by

C.1
Should the Action Expert Learn from Human Actions?
EgoLAP applies the flow-matching loss to human and robot action chunks alike, so the continuous action
expert is trained on human hand motion as well as on robot end-effector motion. This is not an obvious
choice: human motion is faster, noisier, and kinematically unlike anything the robot can execute, and one
might expect it to corrupt an expert whose only job is real-time control. We therefore train a matched variant
that masks the action-expert loss on every human sample while keeping the human language actions and
motion-level reasoning that supervise the backbone. The variant isolates a single question: is human data
useful only as language supervision for the VLM, or also as motion supervision for the expert?
23


Masking the loss is the worse choice overall (Fig. 10). The masked variant loses 4.4 points of overall success,
and the loss is concentrated in the language-conditioned tasks, where it falls from 26.0% to 10.0%; it is roughly
on par on picking and placing and slightly better on cloth folding, the one task family whose dynamics are
least like anything in the human corpus. We read this as evidence that the two roles of human data are
not separable. Once the backbone is supervised with an action representation that already transfers across
embodiments, the features the expert consumes are embodiment-agnostic enough that additional, noisier
