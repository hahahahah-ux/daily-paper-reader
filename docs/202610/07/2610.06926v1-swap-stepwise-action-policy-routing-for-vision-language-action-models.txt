# SWAP: Stepwise Action Policy Routing for Vision-Language-Action Models 

Mousumi Das ∗, Aditeya Prajapati ∗, Abrar Anwar †, Jesse Thomason †

University of Southern California 

Abstract — Robot manipulation systems using Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model backbones typically use just one VLA for task execution. However, individual VLAs do not perform well across different task states and environments. We introduce a framework for dynamically composing multiple VLA policies during execution: StepWise Action Policy Routing (SWAP). SWAP formulates policy routing as an offline reinforcement learning problem, learning a routing critic that selects the most appropriate policy at each decision step given the current observation. SWAP enables robots to select new policies to execute online rather than committing to a single policy for the duration of an episode. We evaluate SWAP on both real-world DROID manipulation tasks and LIBERO simulation experiments. SWAP improves over fixed-policy execution and routing baselines, giving absolute improvements in real-world task success up to 33% while reducing successful trajectory robot action step length by 28.3%. 

Index Terms — Policy Routing, VLAs, Inference 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Robot manipulation systems are increasingly powered by large foundation models such as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that directly map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. These systems have demonstrated impressive generalization across manipulation tasks, but they are typically deployed under a restrictive assumption: that a single policy should control the robot for the duration of execution. In practice, however, no individual policy performs consistently well across every episode. A policy that performs well under one scene configuration may fail under another, while a stronger policy overall may still make poor decisions in specific states. For robots operating in dynamic environments, effective execution may therefore require not a single policy, but a collection of complementary policies that can be selectively composed during execution. Existing approaches largely ignore this setting by training a single large generalist VLA. Recent progress in scaling VLA models has focused on improving the capability of individual policies through larger datasets [1], [2], better architectures [3], [4], broader pretraining [5], [6], and bet-ter post-training [7], and has shown increasingly capable general-purpose manipulation systems. However, these ap-proaches implicitly assume that a single policy can robustly handle the full distribution of task states encountered during deployment. This assumption is particularly limiting in real-world manipulation, where distribution shift, compounding

Rather than selecting a single policy for an entire episode, we consider the setting where a robot has access to multiple candidate policies and must dynamically determine which policy should act at each decision step. This introduces a new challenge: routing decisions must account for long-horizon downstream results, since a chosen locally-reasonable policy may ultimately lead to failure, while an alternative choice may enable recovery or successful completion. To address this, we introduce Step-

Wise Action Policy Routing (SWAP), a framework that formulates policy routing as an offline reinforcement learning problem over a discrete action space of policy identities. SWAP learns a routing critic from offline robot interac-tion data containing successful and failed executions from multiple candidate policies. At deployment, the routing critic evaluates the current observation and dynamically selects the policy predicted to maximize task success. This enables the robot to compose complementary behaviors across policies within a single trajectory. We evaluate SWAP in both real-world and simulated manipulation settings. On a real-world DROID [1] setup, SWAP improves task success from 63.3% for the best stan-dalone policy to 96.7%, while reducing successful trajectory length by 28.3%. Across large-scale LIBERO simulation experiments, we further show that learned policy routing is effective. Together, these results suggest that robust robot execution may emerge not solely from building stronger individual policies, but from learning how to effectively route between them. II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Recent progress in robot foundation models has produced a growing collection of policies with diverse capabilities and failure modes. Our work draws connections between VLA models, routing methods in language models, and value-function-based decision making in robotics. 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models have demon-strated strong generalization across manipulation tasks by di-rectly mapping visual observations and language instructions to robot actions [3]–[5], [7], [8]. Work on evaluating robot policies has found that VLAs often remain brittle on certain arXiv:2610.06926v1 [cs.RO] 3 Oct 2026 Fig. 1: Policy routing for robot manipulation. We introduce SWAP, an offline reinforcement learning framework for online policy routing. Given candidate robot policies, SWAP dynamically selects the best policy online for an instruction and current observation during task execution. types of distribution shifts such as language rephrasing, lighting changes, or increased clutter [9]–[18]. Finetuning VLAs on a new target task to mitigate these shifts can reduce robustness on the original training distribution [17]. Our key insight relies on leveraging the diverse behavior of existing VLAs during a single task rollout. 

Routing in LLMs Recent work in large language models has explored routing mechanisms that dynamically select among multiple models, experts, or inference strategies at test time. Rather than relying on a single model for all inputs, these approaches allocate queries to specialized experts based on the current context [19]–[21], estimated difficulty [22], [23], or predicted downstream performance [19], [24]–[27]. Routing has been studied with model cascades [28]–[30] and multi-model systems [19], [24], [31], [32] that balance cost factors [32], [33] such as latency and capability by selecting the most appropriate model for a given request. A common theme across this literature is that different models exhibit complementary strengths, and that adaptive routing can outperform any individual model alone. In this work, we propose an adjacent problem of policy routing for robotics, where the routing decision must be made online throughout robot motion, as the robot’s actions directly influence future observations and task outcomes. Policy routing is also related to mixture-of-experts architectures, which learn a gating network jointly with the experts; in contrast, SWAP treats the candidate policies as fixed black boxes and learns only the router, so new policies can be added without retraining any expert. 

Value Functions for Robotics Value functions play a central role in robot learning by estimating the long-horizon consequences of actions and policy decisions. Model-free offline RL algorithms [34]–[40] typically require value func-tions to train a robot policy. These value functions enable these policies to predict actions that maximize long-term rewards. Recent work builds more general value [7], [41] and reward [42]–[44] functions. Beyond policy optimization, value functions have been used to guide high-level decision making and planning. SayCan combined language model outputs with learned value functions to select skills that are both relevant and executable [45]. Value functions have been used for failure detection [46], and more recently have been used to steer policies of larger VLAs at deployment time [47]. We take inspiration from these works leveraging value functions at test-time, and focus on learning a routing critic that enables selection among multiple candidate robot policies for policy routing. III. R OBOT POLICY ROUTING 

While modern VLA policies can exhibit strong gener-alization, no single policy is uniformly optimal across all task states, environments, or execution regimes. A policy that performs well during coarse free-space motion may fail during contact-rich manipulation, while another may excel at precise object interaction yet behave conservatively during exploration or recovery. More broadly, policies trained with different data distributions, architectures, or objectives may exhibit complementary strengths and failure modes, motivating policy routing . Rather than committing to a single policy for an entire episode, we argue an agent should dynamically select which policy to execute next based on the current observation context, enabling multiple policies during a single rollout. We formalize action taking in this setting as a Markov decision process (MDP), M = ( S , A , P, r, γ), where at timestep t, the robot observes state st ∈ S and executes action at ∈ A . Rather than learning a single policy π(at | st ),we assume access to a finite library of candidate robot policies, P = {π1, π2, . . . , πK }, where each πk(at | st ) rep-resents a pretrained robot controller. In our setting, these policies may correspond to distinct VLA models trained with different architectures, datasets, or algorithms. Our key insight is to leverage the assumptions that each policy induces different behavior under identical observations and that no single πk dominates across all reachable states. We aim to learn a routing policy , φ , that selects which robot policy to execute at each timestep. The routing policy is defined as φ (zt | st ), where zt ∈ { 1, . . . , K} denotes the se-lected policy index at time t. The robot action to take is then sampled from the chosen robot policy: at ∼ πzt (· | st ). We pose an objective to maximize long-horizon task performance for the routing policy: argmax φ Eφ ,P

Action Policy Routing (SWAP), an offline reinforcement Fig. 2: Overview of SWAP. SWAP learns a routing critic that estimates which policy is most likely to lead to successful task completion given the robot’s current execution state. Training is performed entirely offline from logged robot interaction data from pretrained VLAs, after which the learned critic is used at deployment to dynamically swap between VLA policies during execution. The central component of SWAP is a routing critic that estimates the long-horizon utility of selecting a candidate robot policy given the current observation. Given an observation ot , the critic predicts the expected future returns of executing robot policies πi ∈ P from that state onward. 

Offline Dataset Construction. To train the routing critic, we construct an offline dataset from robot interaction trajec-tories collected using each candidate policy across the target manipulation tasks that includes both successful and failed executions across all policies. Robot policies often predict and execute c chunked low-level control actions. Rather than performing routing at every low-level control step, SWAP routes after each chunk as produced by the underlying VLAs. Routing at the chunk level aligns routing decisions with the VLAs’ own inference cadence, so no additional policy queries are introduced, and it avoids interrupting a policy mid-chunk, where intermediate actions are not individually meaningful. Each routing transi-tion therefore corresponds to executing a selected policy for a fixed chunk of robot actions before re-evaluating which policy should act next. The resulting offline routing dataset consists of transitions (ot , zt , rt , ot+c, dt ), where ot is the robot observation at the beginning of the chunk, zt denotes the selected policy, rt is the resulting sparse reward, ot+c is the observation after chunk execution, and dt indicates whether the episode terminated. The reward signal is sparse and binary, reflecting whether successful task completion occurs following selection of a policy and the execution of its policy chunk. This signal is particularly important in our routing setting, where the quality of a policy choice may only become apparent several timesteps later. 

Offline Critic Learning. Any offline reinforcement learn-ing algorithm could be used to fit the critic given this offline routing dataset. In our setting, the critic must accurately rank competing policy choices under mixed-quality offline data collected from multiple robot policies. We therefore instantiate SWAP using Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) [36], which is well-suited for stable offline value learning. Given routing transitions (ot , zt , rt , ot+c, dt ), IQL learns two routing critics, Q1 and Q2, along with a state-value function, V , by minimizing a temporal-difference objective over the two critics:

At inference time, the learned routing policy φ is induced directly from the critic by selecting the highest-valued candidate policy under the current observation. 

Routing Critic Architecture. We instantiate the routing critic using frozen visual-language embeddings from the Qwen2.5-VL-3B [49] VLM. Using a frozen vision-language encoder allows SWAP to leverage strong pretrained visual and language representations while learning routing behavior from relatively small offline robot datasets. The VLM en-codes the current visual observation and language instruction into a 2048-dimensional embedding. This embedding is concatenated with a learnable embedding corresponding to the candidate policy identity and passed into a lightweight double-Q critic architecture consisting of MLPs that predict scalar action-values over the candidate policy set, along with a separate value network used during IQL training. 

Test-Time Policy Routing. Once the routing critic has been trained, SWAP uses it at deployment to dynamically select among candidate robot policies throughout task execu-tion. Given the current robot observation ot , SWAP evaluates the routing critic over the candidate policy set, and selects the policy with the highest predicted long-horizon utility 

zt = arg max z min (Q1(ot , z), Q2(ot , z)) . The selected policy is queried to produce a short-horizon chunk of robot actions to execute. This routing procedure repeats after each low-level chunk of robot actions is executed, allowing SWAP to dynamically swap between policies as execution unfolds. V. E XPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

We evaluate SWAP in both real-world and simulated ma-nipulation settings to assess whether learned stepwise policy routing improves robot manipulation performance over fixed policy execution. In each setting, SWAP is trained using offline interaction data collected from the candidate policies. We compare SWAP against two classes of baselines. First, we evaluate each candidate policy independently, cor-responding to the standard setting where a single policy is executed for the full episode without routing. Second, we compare against random routing, where candidate policies are selected uniformly at each control step without a learned critic, to isolate the contribution of learned routing from the benefits of policy switching alone. 

A. Experiment Setup 

Real-World DROID. We evaluate SWAP on real-world tabletop manipulation using the DROID Franka setup. The platform consists of a 7-DoF Franka Panda arm with RGB observations from multiple external and wrist-mounted cam-eras. For these experiments, SWAP aggregates Q-values across multiple camera views during inference to improve robustness to viewpoint ambiguity and occlusion. We evalu-ate three manipulation tasks: placing a marker into a bowl, placing a cube into a cup, and transferring a cube from a cloth into a bowl. Our candidate policy set consists of three policies from Physical Intelligence: π0 [5], π0.5 [7], and π0-FAST [50]. We use the publicly released DROID checkpoints of π0.5 and π0-FAST zero-shot; we fine-tune π0 on a small amount of data since it did not present zero-shot capabilities on our tasks. 

LIBERO Simulation. We additionally evaluate SWAP in simulation on the LIBERO [48] and LIBERO-Plus [18] benchmarks, focusing specifically on the LIBERO-Spatial subset. To further test the generalization of SWAP, we evaluate on the spatial subset of LIBERO-Plus. This setting

Training Procedure. To train SWAP, we collect offline interaction trajectories from each candidate policy across the evaluated tasks. For DROID, we collect 10 successful and 10 failed rollouts per policy per task, for a total of 180 trajectories across three tasks and three policies; each method is then evaluated on 10 held-out trials per task. For LIBERO, we collect 50 rollouts per policy per task on 8 of the 10 LIBERO-Spatial tasks, holding out tasks 5 and 9 for evaluation. We also evaluate the more diverse LIBERO-Plus Spatial subset. We use sparse rewards in the range where successful task completion sets a final transition reward of 10, failure of 0, and in both cases all intermediate transitions receive 0 reward. We deliberately use only sparse task-completion reward signals and do not explore intermediate reward shaping, as that would require denser rewards that may be difficult to acquire. We train separate routing critics for the DROID and LIBERO environments. For real-world experiments, we train for 4k–8k epochs; for simulation we train for 200 epochs.

Qualitative analysis. To qualitatively reinforce these quantitative notions of how routing behavior changes over time, we also look into how SWAP learns to specialize on when to call different policies in Figure 4. Early in the rollout, SWAP selects π0-FAST while the robot traverses free space toward the target object. As the robot approaches the marker and the task requires more precise manipulation, SWAP switches to π0.5, which consistently receives higher Q-values during the grasping phase. After the marker is accidentally dropped, the predicted Q-values for all policies decrease as expected. SWAP then re-selects π0.5 to recover and successfully reattempt the grasp. Once the marker has been secured, the Q-values again favor π0-FAST , and SWAP switches back to it for faster movement to the bowl. These behaviors suggest that the learned routing critic captures complementary policy strengths, preferring π0-FAST for free-space motion and π0.5 for fine-grained object manipulation and recovery. We found that for the individual policies, after a failure, there would be no attempts at recovery beyond simple hovering until a timeout was reached. We did not observe visible discontinuities in robot motion when SWAP switches policies; since all candidate policies consume the same observation and output actions in the same DROID action space, switching amounts to querying a different model for the next chunk from the current state. 

Routing transfers under distribution shift. We eval-uate SWAP under OOD conditions in both LIBERO and DROID settings. For these OOD experiments, we re-train all 3 base policies on the in-distribution tasks, and add unseen distractor objects to two DROID tasks and evaluate SWAP over 10 trials each without retraining the critic (Table IV), achieving 90% and 80% success, compared to 40% and 30% for the best individual policies. Similar to our qualitative analysis section, we found strong correlation of policy swapping during key stages of execution like grasping, moving, and dropoff. In simulation, Table III (a) presents a setting with five heterogeneous policies whose ranking inverts under shift: π0 is the strongest in-distribution (81%) but only fourth on LIBERO-Plus (54.2%). SWAP achieves 62.65%, far above random routing (2.7%), which collapses here because switching between behaviorally incompatible policies is destructive; it also correctly ignores OpenVLA-Spatial (0%), whose removal changes performance by only 0.35 points. The remaining gap to the best fixed policy stems from π0: removing it raises SWAP to 70.65%. Since π0 is the best policy on the critic’s training distribution, this preference is correct for the data SWAP saw; the failure is that in-distribution quality does not predict OOD quality. The router itself thus generalizes to novel scenes, with the limiting factor being whether candidate strengths are stable across the shift.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduce SWAP, a framework for learned step-wise policy routing across Vision-Language-Action policies. Rather than committing to a single policy for the duration of an episode, SWAP learns a routing critic that dynamically selects among candidate policies based on the robot’s current observation. Across both real-world DROID evaluations and large-scale LIBERO simulation experiments, we show that learned policy routing improves over fixed-policy execution and non-adaptive routing baselines. These results suggest that individual robot policies often exhibit complementary strengths, and that explicitly routing between them can result in more reliable manipulation. More broadly, SWAP highlights policy routing as a simple but effective mechanism for composing heterogeneous robot policies. 

Limitations. Our experiments focus on routing across a small set of candidate policies, and it remains an open question how learned policy routing scales to dozens or hun-dreds of specialized models, especially under increasingly OOD scenarios. Our evaluation is also restricted to short-horizon tabletop manipulation; on longer-horizon tasks the sparse terminal reward becomes a weaker training signal for the routing critic, and intermediate subtask completion signals would likely be needed. As the candidate set grows, querying multiple heterogeneous policies also introduces systems challenges that may make on-device deployment intractable. Finally, our formulation optimizes only for task return and does not account for costs such as inference latency or monetary cost. Incorporating cost-aware objectives while scaling routing to more heterogeneous policies is an exciting direction for future work.
