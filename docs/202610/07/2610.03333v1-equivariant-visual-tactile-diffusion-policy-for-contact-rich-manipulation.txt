Abstract: Imitation learning for contact-rich manipulation requires high-quality expert data that is expensive to obtain. This makes learning a sample-efficient pol-icy a key issue. To address this, we propose VISTA, a workspace-level equivari-ant visuotactile diffusion policy for data-efficient contact-rich imitation learning. VISTA projects visual and tactile observations into spherical tokens, injects tac-tile contact cues into visual spherical directions through permutation-equivariant spherical fusion, and rotates the fused harmonic representation using the end-effector orientation. The resulting representation conditions an equivariant dif-fusion policy to predict spatially consistent actions. Extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world robotic settings show that VISTA substantially im-proves data efficiency over strong visuotactile imitation learning baselines. ## 1 Introduction 

Many daily contact-rich tasks, such as inserting a USB drive or wiping a table, require tactile sensing in addition to vision. For robots, however, using both modalities effectively remains challenging. Recent robotics research has increasingly adopted imitation learning with visual and tactile feedback for contact-rich manipulation [1, 2, 3], but expert demonstrations are expensive to collect and often cover only a limited range of object poses, gripper poses, and contact configurations [4]. Moreover, for tasks such as insertion, success depends heavily on the policy’s robustness to small deviations in observed object and robot poses [5]. Therefore, how can we learn robust visuotactile policies with fewer demonstrations? Equivariance provides a principled way to reduce the spatial coverage required from data for a policy to achieve spatial robustness [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. In an equivariant policy, if the observation is transformed by a spatial transformation from a symmetry group, the predicted action should trans-form in the corresponding way. By building this symmetry into the architecture, the model can im-prove spatial generalizability across object poses, gripper poses, and scene configurations with fewer demonstrations than non-equivariant policies [11, 12]. This is especially appealing for contact-rich manipulation, where demonstrations are costly, and task success is sensitive to small spatial devia-tions. However, existing equivariant manipulation policies are still largely vision-centric. They are typically designed around RGB images, point clouds, or other global geometric observations [13], limiting their ability to handle contact-rich settings where local tactile feedback is critical. In this paper, we propose VISTA, a workspace-level equivariant visuotactile diffusion policy. Our method is inspired by the observation that both egocentric camera and tactile sensors are often mounted on the end effector of a robot for manipulation tasks; therefore, global workspace rotations change the sensor-to-world transform while preserving the local contact measurement. Concretely, our method first maps egocentric vision and tactile observations into local spherical representations.

The fused spherical representation is then transformed by the end-effector pose rotation and processed with an equiv-ariant diffusion policy to achieve workspace-level equivariance. As a result, VISTA improves data efficiency and generalization under limited demonstrations. We evaluate VISTA on both contact-rich simulation tasks and real-robot experiments and show that the proposed framework enables more data-efficient contact-rich manipulation learning. Our main contributions are: • We introduce VISTA, extending the workspace-level equivariant backbone from Image-to-Sphere Policy (ISP) [14] to the visuotactile setting for contact-rich tasks, enabling more data-efficient learning by incorporating local tactile feedback into an equivariant policy. • We design a spherical cross-modal fusion module that integrates tactile cues into the structured visual representation, allowing the downstream equivariant diffusion policy to leverage the fused representation effectively. Across eight simulated tasks, this design improves mean success by 6.8 percentage points over direct visual-tactile feature concatenation on the sphere. • We demonstrate data-efficient contact-rich manipulation across eight simulated tasks and three real-robot tasks: VISTA achieves 80.9% mean success, outperforming EquiDiff+Tac by 15.9 percentage points, and 73.3% average success on real robots. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Equivariance in robot manipulation 

Equivariance embeds workspace symmetry into the policy class by requiring that transformed scenes, objects, and robot states yield actions transformed in the same physical manner [15, 16, 17, 18, 7]. Existing equivariant manipulation methods primarily enforce this constraint in two observa-tion domains. The first operates on 3D geometry such as point clouds [19, 20, 8, 9, 13, 21, 22, 23], where scalar, vector, or higher-order features are attached to 3D points and propagated according to their transformation rules [21, 22, 23]; translation is typically handled by centering, relative coordi-nates, centroid recovery, or canonicalization, while rotation can be modeled with vector neurons [9]. Although this provides a natural interface for workspace-level equivariance, it often relies on reliable depth sensing, calibration, point cloud extraction, and alignment. The second route starts from RGB observations, motivated by large-scale robot datasets and practical systems based on monocular or wrist-mounted cameras; recent work has explored planar transport, bi-equivariant pick-and-place, equivariant diffusion for 6-DoF actions, and image-to-sphere projection for egocentric RGB in-puts [24, 25, 26]. However, both routes remain largely vision-centric: their symmetries are defined over visual observations, proprioception, and actions. 

2.2 Contact-rich visuotactile manipulation 

Tactile sensing provides contact-local cues—geometry, deformation, shear, slip, and force—that vi-sion often cannot resolve, making it crucial for contact-rich manipulation. Existing work studies visuotactile representation learning through contrastive or simulation-based pretraining, while also integrating touch into policies for precise or reactive control [2, 27, 28, 29, 30, 3, 31, 32, 33, 5]. Although these methods show the value of tactile feedback when vision is insufficient, they focus mainly on representation, benchmark design, or reactive feedback rather than explicitly modeling workspace-level symmetry. Thus, tactile observations are usually encoded by CNNs, pretrained encoders, deformation features, or temporal modules and then fused with visual and proprioceptive inputs, allowing policies to use touch effectively but without explicitly enforcing consistent transfor-mations among vision, touch, robot state, and 6-DoF actions under changes in object pose, gripper pose, or task frame. 2.3 Tactile equivariance and geometric contact policies 

The most closely related works also study symmetry in tactile or contact-driven manipulation, but they realize equivariance in different settings and through different mechanisms. EquiTac defines tactile equivariance for in-hand object rotation by reconstructing tactile normal maps whose in-plane components transform as SO (2) vectors while out-of-plane components remain invariant, and then uses an equivariant network to predict an angular residual for correcting a base policy [34]; how-ever, this symmetry is local to the fingertip/object-in-hand frame and serves as a residual orientation correction, rather than a global workspace-level policy that jointly transforms RGB observations, tactile feedback, proprioception, and 6-DoF action chunks. In contrast, geometric contact poli-cies achieve workspace-level symmetry from a control-theoretic perspective: contact-rich SE (3) -equivariant manipulation relies on body-frame error and wrench representations with impedance control [35], while hierarchical variants further combine global reference-frame estimation with lo-calized compliant policies [36]. Thus, although these methods provide important forms of tactile or contact equivariance, VISTA complements them by building workspace-level equivariance directly into an end-to-end diffusion policy over visuotactile and proprioceptive observations. 

## 3 Method 

VISTA is a workspace-level equivariant visuotactile diffusion policy for contact-rich manipulation. Our method is compatible with a now-common robotics setting where both visual and tactile cam-eras are rigidly attached to the end effector. In this setting, VISTA first projects visual and tactile observations into spheres and fuses them, then rotates the fused representation into the workspace frame using the end-effector orientation. The representation conditions an equivariant diffusion pol-icy that predicts 6-DoF action chunks. The architecture of VISTA is illustrated in Figure 1.

3.2 Visuotactile Spherical Representation 

Under a global rotation, the relative geometry observed in both the egocentric camera and the tactile sensor is unchanged. Under this joint workspace rotation of the robot-object-sensor system, the ideal sensor-local measurements are invariant: the relative camera-object geometry and local tactile deformation remain unchanged. This property suggests a simple route to workspace equivariance, as proposed in Image-to-Sphere Policy (ISP) [14]: first encode egocentric vision into a steerable spherical representation, then use the end-effector orientation to rotate the resulting representation into the workspace frame. VISTA additionally lifts tactile observations into spherical tokens and fuses them with the visual field before the same correction. 

Spherical projection. Concretely, for each observation step, VISTA first extracts 2D feature maps from the visual and tactile images using an equivariant ResNet [38, 39]. Both the visual and tactile feature maps are then projected to a set of tokens on a discretized sphere [40, 26] (details in Sec. A.2). Concretely, let V = {vi}Pi=1 denote the visual spherical tokens and T = {τj }Pj=1 denote the tactile spherical tokens; P is the number of sampled sphere points for both vision and tactile.

Harmonic projection and equivariance cor-rection. Following ISP [14], VISTA projects the tactile-conditioned spherical tokens to spherical harmonic coefficients. Let si ∈

We then align the local sensor representation to the workspace frame using the end-effector orientation Rt. For each harmonic degree ℓ, the correction is bcℓ,t = Dℓ(Rt)cℓ,t , where Dℓ is the Wigner-D representation of SO(3) . The corrected coefficients are processed by spherical convo-lution layers to produce an SO(3) -structured observation feature. Proprioception is encoded using scalar features for gripper state and vector features for end-effector position and orientation axes. The fused visuotactile and proprioceptive features are concatenated to form the final observation representation used by the downstream diffusion policy.

To make the denoising process equivariant, VISTA represents both the noisy action chunk and observation condition as steerable features. ## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate VISTA on simulated and real contact-rich manipulation tasks. The experiments are organized to answer the following questions: (1) Does VISTA improve contact-rich imitation learn-ing under a limited demonstration budget? (2) Does tactile spherical representation provide benefits beyond an image-only equivariant policy? (3) Does tactile-conditioned spherical fusion improve over simpler ways of injecting tactile? (4) Does VISTA improve data efficiency as the number of demonstrations changes? (5) Does the same design transfer to real-robot contact-rich manipulation? 

4.1 Simulation Experiments Experimental setup. We compare with ManiFeel [29], a non-equivariant visuotactile diffusion policy. VITAL [2] and UniVTAC [28] are Action-Chunking Transformer (ACT) [44]-based policies that fuse pretrained visual and tactile encoders without an explicit workspace-equivariant represen-tation. EquiDiff + Tac [25], adopted from [29], provides the closest equivariant comparison: it uses a C8-equivariant visual ResNet, treats tactile features as trivial C8 representations, and conditions a 

C8-equivariant diffusion U-Net. It therefore has equivariant decoding and tactile input, but no tactile spherical lifting or visual–tactile fusion before harmonic projection. For a fair comparison, all base-lines and ablations use the same end-effector-based 10-D absolute action interface as VISTA. All methods share the demonstration source and evaluation protocol. ## 5 Limitations 

First, the equivariance guarantee is workspace-level and exact only for the selected finite group; it does not provide intrinsic tactile equivariance to arbitrary in-hand object rotations. Second, the equivariance analysis assumes rigidly mounted sensors and joint robot–object–sensor rotations that leave the local visual and tactile observations unchanged before workspace correction. Background or illumination changes, gravity-dependent contact, in-gripper slip, calibration errors, external cam-eras, and changing extrinsics can violate this assumption. Explicitly modeling local tactile symme-tries and calibration uncertainty remains future work. Finally, despite sub-100 ms model-side inference, the deployed policy executes six insertion actions or eight erasing actions at 10 Hz before replanning. New tactile observations thus affect decisions only every 0.6–0.8 s; the system is not a high-rate tactile closed loop and may miss transient slip or contact changes. We have not studied the trade-off of shorter chunks. Adaptive or event-triggered replanning and fast tactile residual control are promising extensions. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented VISTA, a workspace-level equivariant visuotactile diffusion policy for data-efficient contact-rich manipulation. VISTA projects wrist RGB and tactile observations into spherical tokens, lets each visual direction query tactile features before harmonic projection, and aligns the fused representation with the end-effector orientation before equivariant diffusion decoding. Across eight simulation tasks, VISTA reaches an 80.9% mean success rate with only 50 demonstrations per task, improving over the image-only spherical policy by 12.8 points and over EquiDiff + Tac by 15.9 points. With 100 demonstrations, it reaches 84.7%, and on three real-robot tasks it achieves 7.33/10 successful trials compared with 1.33/10 for the strongest equivariant baseline. These results support the value of combining tactile spherical lifting and direction-dependent fusion with workspace-level equivariance. Future work could study broader object distributions, faster tactile feedback, multiple tactile streams, and automatic selection of task symmetries. 9Acknowledgments

A.4 Equivariance Correction Details 

The fused spherical coefficients are initially expressed in a local sensor-rig frame. Let Rt ∈ SO(3) 

be the end-effector orientation in the workspace frame. For each harmonic degree ℓ, VISTA applies 

bcℓ,t = Dℓ(Rt)cℓ,t .

Under a global workspace rotation g, the local visual and tactile measurements are unchanged, while the end-effector orientation becomes gR t. Therefore, 

bcℓ,t (g · ot) = Dℓ(gR t)cℓ,t (ot) (14) 

= Dℓ(g)Dℓ(Rt)cℓ,t (ot) (15) 

= Dℓ(g)bcℓ,t (ot). (16) This is the coefficient-level equivariance used by the observation encoder. The tactile sensor may have a fixed extrinsic transform relative to the end effector. This does not change the argument: a fixed extrinsic only changes the local sensor-rig coordinate convention and can be absorbed into the learned tactile lifting and fusion modules. The global transformation still acts by left multiplication on the end-effector orientation. 

A.5 Equivariant Diffusion Details Action representation. Each action contains translation, two rotation axes, and a scalar grip-per command. Translation and rotation axes are represented as vector features, while the gripper command is represented as a scalar. This gives a natural equivariant feature structure: the vector components transform under rotations, and the gripper component remains invariant. 

Finite-group sampling. The observation encoder outputs coefficients of shape 

[B, H o, 128 , 455] ,

and the action encoder outputs coefficients of shape 

[B, H p, 64 , 455] .

These coefficients are evaluated on a finite rotation group. The SO(3) variants use an icosahedral group I60 with NG = 60 samples, while the SO(2) variants use a cyclic group C8 with NG = 8 

samples. After sampling, the tensors have shapes 

[B, H o, 128 , N G], [B, H p, 64 , N G].

The group axis is then flattened into the batch axis: 

[B, H p, 64 , N G] → [BN G, H p, 64] ,

and the same temporal 1D U-Net is applied to every group element. The group axis is treated as additional samples only after the steerable features have been evalu-ated on the selected finite group. The temporal U-Net uses shared weights and group-independent normalization layers such as GroupNorm or LayerNorm in the diffusion path; we avoid BatchNorm over the flattened BN G axis because batch statistics that mix group samples can introduce group-dependent numerical effects and weaken equivariance. 16 Why weight sharing gives equivariance. For an element g in the selected finite group, acting on the input permutes the finite-group samples. Since the U-Net is shared across all group elements, applying the U-Net before or after this permutation gives the same result up to the same permutation. The final equivariant decoder maps the permuted group signal back to transformed action vectors and an invariant gripper scalar.

Early Fusion. Visual and tactile 2D feature maps are concatenated in the image plane and fused by a 1 × 1 convolution, group normalization, and ReLU. ISP+Concat-Tac. Visual and tactile maps are lifted separately: visual tokens use the Gaussian viewing-direction projection, and tactile tokens use the learned tactile projection in Appendix A.2. A.8 Real-Robot Protocol Details 

The real-robot experiments use a Franka arm with an end-effector-mounted Orbbec wrist RGB cam-era and a left GelSight Mini tactile sensor. Each task uses 100 real demonstrations for training; all demonstrations are real-only and no simulation checkpoint is transferred. We train with a sin-gle seed, split the demonstrations 8:2 into training and validation sets, save checkpoints every 30 epochs, and select the snapshot with the lowest validation diffusion loss. The 10 reported trials per task are fully held-out and are not used for checkpoint selection. During rollout, the policy observes two history frames, predicts action chunks, and executes actions at 10 Hz; the Franka-side controller runs at 300 Hz. The wrist RGB stream runs at 30 Hz and the GelSight Mini stream runs at 10 Hz. For insert USB and insert pencil, the evaluation initial end-effector pose is uniformly perturbed by 

±1 cm along each Cartesian axis, while the socket/sharpener is placed randomly within the visible workspace and the in-hand object is given a rotational offset. In erase, the whiteboard is placed on the table and the marker stroke is drawn randomly within the wrist-camera-visible area. A trial is counted successful if the USB is inserted into the socket, the pencil is inserted and activates the sharpener, or the target marker stroke is removed by wiping.
