ABSTRACT
Human demonstrations offer rich examples of precise dexterous manipulation and
a promising source of robot training data. However, high-fidelity reproduction
of demonstrated motions and hand–object interactions across robot embodiments
remains challenging under physical constraints. We present DexForge, a differen-
tiable physics-grounded framework for converting human video demonstrations
into high-fidelity robot trajectories.
We reconstruct spherical-Gaussian object
models and hand–object motion from visual observations, then build a differ-
entiable simulator combining efficient Gaussian collision detection with existing
differentiable dynamics. Based on this simulator, DexForge combines contact-
aware kinematic retargeting with force-aware dynamics retargeting: robot-adapted
stable contacts guide kinematic reference construction and subsequent gradient-
based control refinement for precise physical motion reproduction. Experiments
on 130 DexYCB and HOT3D demonstrations across seven dexterous hands show
success-rate gains of approximately 35–53 percentage points over the baseline,
with object position and orientation tracking errors on successful trajectories re-
duced by approximately 34–67% and 71–78%, respectively. Further experiments
demonstrate open-loop transfer to MuJoCo and real-robot execution. Our project
page is available at https://wmz1226.github.io/DexForge/.

INTRODUCTION
Enabling robots with different embodiments to reproduce precise human manipulation is an impor-
tant step toward transferring human expertise to robots (Xu et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026). High-
fidelity reproduction requires more than task completion; the demonstrated motions and hand–object
interactions must also be preserved. For example, establishing a stable grasp is only the starting
point; precisely controlling the object’s subsequent movement and orientation captures finer aspects
of manipulation. Human manipulation videos contain rich examples of these behaviors, yet translat-
ing them into high-fidelity trajectories executable by different dexterous hands remains challenging.
Recent advances in learning from human demonstrations and retargeting are narrowing this
gap.
Hand–object reconstruction recovers explicit 3D geometry and motion references from
videos (Wang et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026), while kinematic retargeting adapts these references
to robots, increasingly incorporating contact preservation (Lakshmipathy et al., 2025) and colli-
sion constraints (Tian et al., 2026) to improve geometric consistency. Building on these references,
physics-based retargeting uses policy learning (Li et al., 2025) or trajectory optimization (Pan et al.,
2026) to find controls that drive object motion in simulation, with contact guidance helping preserve
demonstrated interactions (Mandi et al., 2025). Meanwhile, advances in differentiable collision
detection (Beker et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026) and dynamics modeling (Paulus et al., 2026) en-
able control optimization through simulation gradients, providing another route to precise trajectory
refinement (Liu et al., 2024). Together, these developments extend retargeting beyond geometric
hand-motion matching toward physically grounded manipulation reproduction.
Despite these advances, high-fidelity reproduction across different robot hands remains difficult.
Several problems need to be solved in sequence. First, the reconstructed object geometry must be ac-
curate enough for collision and contact reasoning. Second, contacts from the human demonstration
may not be reachable or stable for a robot with a different hand shape. Third, even a penetration-free

robot trajectory with geometrically appropriate contacts may not be executable under contact dynam-
ics and actuation constraints. Physics-based optimization also involves a trade-off. Sampling-based
methods can handle complex contact changes, but obtaining very accurate trajectories may require
many samples. Gradient-based optimization can refine trajectories more directly, but it is sensitive
to initialization and local minima. This motivates us to use robot-adapted contacts to provide a good
kinematic reference and continued guidance during gradient-based dynamics optimization.
We introduce DexForge, a differentiable physics-grounded framework for high-fidelity retargeting
from human video demonstrations. Our key insight is that human contacts should not be copied
directly to a robot with a different hand. Instead, we first adapt these contacts to the target robot, and
then use the adapted contacts to guide both kinematic retargeting and dynamic control optimization.
To support this process, we reconstruct spherical Gaussian object models that provide both visual
appearance for pose estimation and geometry for collision checking. We then extract stable contacts
from the human demonstration and refine them together with robot configurations so that they are
reachable, collision-free, and stable for the target hand. These contacts are used to build a robot
kinematic reference. Finally, we combine differentiable Gaussian collision with ComFree dynamics
and optimize robot controls through simulation gradients to reproduce the demonstrated motion
more accurately. We further use system identification to reduce the mismatch between simulation
and the target system. Our contributions are:
• We propose DexForge, a complete framework that combines visual reconstruction, contact-aware
kinematic retargeting, and force-aware dynamics optimization to transform human video demon-
strations into high-fidelity, executable demonstrations across robot embodiments.
• We introduce robot-adapted stable-contact guidance through joint anchor and robot-configuration
optimization, supporting both kinematic reference construction and dynamic refinement.
• We jointly train spherical Gaussians for appearance and geometry and develop efficient differen-
tiable collision detection for them, supporting integration with existing simulators.
• We evaluate DexForge on 130 DexYCB and HOT3D demonstrations across seven hands, outper-
forming sampling-based baselines in retargeting success and object-tracking accuracy, and demon-
strate open-loop MuJoCo transfer and real-robot execution.

RELATED WORK
Dexterous Manipulation from Human Demonstrations. Human demonstrations can be collected
through portable motion-capture systems (Wang et al., 2024) and wearable interfaces (Xu et al.,
2025a; Tao et al., 2025). Egocentric human videos also provide scalable supervision for dexterous
policy learning (Zhang et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). Explicit scene and motion references recov-
ered from videos enable robot trajectory generation (Mu et al., 2026; Paliwal et al., 2026) or guide
policy learning in simulation (Lum et al., 2025). DexForge reconstructs object models from separate
capture sequences and recovers hand–object motion from demonstration videos, then converts these
references into high-fidelity robot trajectories through contact adaptation and physical optimization.
Dexterous Motion Retargeting. Kinematic retargeting maps human motion to robot configurations
through geometric correspondence objectives (Yin et al., 2025; Xin et al., 2026). Interaction-aware
methods further preserve contact regions or local hand–object geometry (Lakshmipathy et al., 2025;
Wu et al., 2026), while constrained formulations incorporate collision avoidance (Tian et al., 2026).
Such interaction structure can also be encoded through interaction meshes for whole-body retar-
geting (Yang et al., 2026) and dexterous manipulation (Feng et al., 2026). The kinematic stage of
C2Dex (Ren et al., 2026) uses stable object-side contacts to guide reference construction. For grasp
configurations, GenHand (Qi et al., 2026) refines human-derived contact anchors through force-
closure optimization, while GraspQP (Zurbr¨ugg et al., 2025) develops a differentiable force-closure
energy for grasp synthesis. DexForge jointly optimizes robot keyframe configurations and anchors
shared within stable-contact intervals, considering reachability and force closure.
Physics-based retargeting accounts for contact dynamics, with representative approaches using
reference-guided reinforcement learning (Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Mandi et al., 2025; Xu
et al., 2025b; Ren et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026) or sampling-based trajectory optimization (Yang
et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2026; Paliwal et al., 2026). SPIDER (Pan et al., 2026) progressively re-
laxes virtual contact guidance during optimization, while Do as I Do (Paliwal et al., 2026) adapts
sampling-based retargeting to noisy reconstructed references. Gradient-based transfer is also ex-
plored by QuasiSim (Liu et al., 2024), which progressively tightens physical constraints through a
curriculum of parameterized quasi-physical simulators. DexForge uses robot-adapted stable con-
2


tacts to construct collision-constrained kinematic references for initialization and retains the same
refined anchors as interaction targets during gradient-based dynamics optimization.
Differentiable Simulation and Physical Scene Modeling. Differentiable collision detection pro-
vides geometric derivatives for optimization (Tracy et al., 2023; Montaut et al., 2023; Beker et al.,
2026), while contact models and simulators advance differentiable dynamics (Jin, 2024; Ye et al.,
2026; Paulus et al., 2026). Gaussian representations (Kerbl et al., 2023) also support physical mod-
eling: ContactGaussian-WM (Wang et al., 2026) unifies appearance and collision geometry with
spherical Gaussians but optimizes geometry and appearance separately, whereas D-REX (Lou et al.,
2026) combines Gaussian rendering and mesh-based collisions with differentiable mass identifica-
tion and grasping policy learning. DexForge jointly optimizes spherical Gaussians to better capture
appearance and collision geometry, providing an efficient differentiable collision-query interface di-
rectly on these primitives. Integrating it with ComFree dynamics (Borse, 2026) yields ComFree-GS,
supporting contact-aware kinematic retargeting and gradient-based physical retargeting.

METHOD
Our goal is to transform human video demonstrations into high-fidelity, physically executable robot
rollouts. We formulate the problem in Section 3.1 and address it by progressively establishing
the physical structure required for robot execution, as illustrated in Figure 1. We first reconstruct
the geometry of the hand and object and recover their pose trajectories from visual observations
(Section 3.2). We then extract stable human contacts, adapt them to the target robot, and generate
a robot-adapted kinematic reference that satisfies collision constraints (Section 3.3). Finally, we
realize this reference by optimizing controls through a differentiable simulator and calibrating the
dynamics model against observations from the target system (Section 3.4).

3.2
HAND AND OBJECT RECONSTRUCTION
In this section, we reconstruct high-fidelity object assets from multi-view RGB-D videos and recover
object pose trajectories and MANO hand motion from hand–object interaction videos. The recon-
structed assets must combine accurate geometry for physical simulation in subsequent retargeting
with faithful appearance for pose estimation. ContactGaussian-WM (Wang et al., 2026) demon-
strates how spherical Gaussians can unify visual appearance and collision geometry, avoiding the
need to maintain and align separate models. However, its image-based initialization does not di-
rectly constrain interior distances, potentially yielding unreliable penetration-depth estimates. Its
subsequent color-fitting stage holds this potentially biased geometry fixed, which can also limit
appearance reconstruction accuracy. We therefore introduce depth-derived distance supervision to
constrain geometry while allowing geometry and color to be optimized jointly. Specifically, we
define the Gaussian collision distance field at a query point pi ∈R3 as:

3.3
CONTACT-AWARE KINEMATIC RETARGETING
The hand–object trajectories recovered in Section 3.2 describe the demonstrated interaction but do
not directly specify how a robot with a different morphology should reproduce it. In particular,
morphological differences can make human contacts unreachable or unsuitable, while MANO re-
construction errors, such as substantial hand–object penetration, can make extracted anchors unreli-
able. We therefore extract stable contacts and adapt them to the target embodiment while preserving
the demonstrated interaction semantics. These adapted contacts then guide full-trajectory optimiza-
tion under configuration and collision constraints, yielding a robot-compatible kinematic reference
without yet assuming that the motion is dynamically executable.

3.4
FORCE-AWARE DYNAMICS RETARGETING
Kinematic retargeting provides a reference motion but does not determine the controls and con-
tact forces required for execution. Force-aware dynamics retargeting therefore optimizes controls
through a differentiable simulator, accounting for hand–object contact forces to generate trajecto-
ries under the modeled actuation and contact physics. We further calibrate the simulator’s physical
parameters to reduce model mismatch and support transfer to the target system.

into ComFree-Sim (Borse, 2026) to obtain our differentiable simulator, ComFree-GS. We use multi-
start gradient-based MPC, with gradients backpropagated through ComFree-GS. At each replanning
step t, we optimize position-control knots U over an L-step rollout horizon:

Transferability. Can the generated trajectories transfer to other simulators and real robots?

4.2.2
ABLATION STUDY
To assess individual component contributions, we compare our full method with three cumulative ab-
lation variants: (1) replacing reconstructed hand–object trajectories with dataset-provided ones; (2)
further disabling contact refinement; and (3) finally removing raw contact guidance. We evaluate all
variants on five DexYCB and five HOT3D sequences covering diverse objects and contact patterns,
using seven robotic hands in ComFree-GS. Stochastic optimization is repeated with five random
seeds, and Table 1 reports mean success rates. Detailed settings are provided in Appendix G.
Ablation Analysis. All variants retain dynamics optimization, as kinematic trajectories alone cannot
ensure physical executability. With dataset ground-truth (GT) hand–object trajectories, raw contact
guidance improves retargeting success, and contact refinement yields further gains. These improve-
ments are consistent with the components’ roles: guidance provides explicit targets for establishing
and maintaining contacts, while refinement adapts these targets to the robot’s morphology through
joint anchor and configuration optimization, accounting for reachability and encouraging force clo-
sure. Replacing GT trajectories with our reconstructions largely preserves success rates, with small
decreases for some hands, supporting the framework’s effectiveness with reconstructed inputs.

GAUSSIAN TRAINING DETAILS
We jointly optimize Gaussian geometry and appearance using RGB reconstruction and signed-
distance supervision from multi-view observations. This section describes reference SDF construc-
tion, supervision sampling, and the joint objective combining geometric and photometric losses.

(TSDF) and convert it into a reference Euclidean signed distance field ϕref following (Oleynikova
et al., 2017). The reference field measures the shortest Euclidean distance to the reconstructed
surface, with negative values inside the object and positive values outside, and provides geometric
supervision for the Gaussian collision representation during training.

distances di = ϕref(pi). We store these pairs as DSDF = {(pi, di)}106

B
HAND AND OBJECT MOTION RECONSTRUCTION
We reconstruct MANO hand motion and object poses from synchronized, calibrated multi-view
RGB videos using the pretrained Gaussian object model O. Hand–object foreground masks are
obtained with SAM 2 (Ravi et al., 2024) video segmentation, prompted once in the first frame of
each view. This stage uses only RGB observations; depth supervision is used separately for object-
model training.
Multi-View MANO Fitting. We detect right hands using the WiLoR (Potamias et al., 2025) hand
detector and obtain per-view MANO estimates with HaMeR (Pavlakos et al., 2023). Confidence-
weighted rotation averaging and keypoint triangulation initialize the hand motion in world coordi-
nates. We jointly optimize per-frame poses and translations with a shared hand shape, minimizing
robust multi-view reprojection error, temporal acceleration, and deviations from the initial estimates.
A strong pose prior limits articulation changes caused by noisy keypoint predictions. Optimization
uses Adam for 800 iterations.
Object Pose Tracking. We extend the render-and-compare refinement of GS-Pose (Cai et al., 2024)
to multiple views while keeping Gaussian geometry and appearance fixed. The fitted MANO mesh
provides hand masks that exclude occluded pixels. Object poses are optimized through differen-
tiable rendering using silhouette overlap and photometric losses over the remaining pixels. The first
frame uses multiple rotation hypotheses, while subsequent frames are initialized from the previ-
ous pose and a constant-velocity prediction. Coarse-to-fine refinement, rotation regularization, and
loss-triggered re-initialization improve tracking robustness.
Hand–Object Refinement. With object poses fixed, we further refine the hand using the Gaussian
collision distances in Eq. 2. Vertex–frame pairs within 5 mm of the object, including penetrating
vertices, form a fixed candidate contact set. We augment the hand-fitting objective with squared
penetration and contact-distance penalties, weighted by 10 and 1, respectively, with distances mea-
sured in millimeters, and optimize for another 400 iterations. The resulting MANO motion and
object poses provide the reference trajectories for retargeting.
Reconstruction Evaluation. We evaluate all 130 selected sequences using object translation and
rotation errors, world-frame and wrist-aligned MPJPE, and hand–object penetration depth and
penetrating-frame percentage. Table 5 reports means and standard deviations across sequences,
while Fig. 7 shows a qualitative DexYCB example. Our method achieves accurate reconstruction
with eight views on DexYCB and one RGB plus two grayscale SLAM views on HOT3D, with
substantially less penetration than dataset annotations on both datasets.

GAUSSIAN COLLISION DETECTION
We describe the spatial acceleration, contact geometry, differentiation, and simulator integration
of Gaussian-based collision detection. Each object is represented by spherical Gaussian primitives
rigidly attached to its body, with collision radii ri = 3σi. Robot surface query points are represented
as zero-radius primitives and handled by the same collision formulation. All centers used in the
following distance and contact formulas are expressed in a common world coordinate frame. We
first describe the default hard contact mode, which is used for all results in this paper, and then an
optional soft mode.
BVH Construction. We construct binary bounding volume hierarchies (BVHs) (Bergen, 1997)
over each object’s primitives in its body-local coordinate frame using a binned surface-area heuris-
tic. Each node stores an axis-aligned bounding box of the primitive centers and their maximum
collision radius. The distance between two center bounding boxes, minus their maximum radii, pro-
vides a conservative lower bound on the corresponding sphere-pair distances. The hierarchies are
constructed during model loading and reused under rigid-body motion. During traversal, relative
body transforms are applied to express the bounding boxes in a common coordinate frame.

KINEMATIC TRAJECTORY OPTIMIZATION
Contact Targets. For each finger and frame, we select the temporally nearest stable-contact interval.
The distance is zero inside an interval and otherwise equals the distance to its nearest endpoint; ties
are resolved in favor of the earlier interval. Both the anchor and robot contact query point are taken
from the selected interval. The blending weight αt,i is one within that interval and zero elsewhere.
The contact-position fitting weight is 100(1 + 99αt,i). Fingers without stable-contact intervals
instead track the MANO fingertips with weight 1 in the position-fitting term.
Full-Trajectory Regularization. In Eq. 4, Lreg applies the palm, hand-shape, and contact-normal
penalties at every frame, together with the temporal operator over the full trajectory. The palm target
is the MANO palm pose composed with spline-interpolated keyframe palm offsets. The keyframe
proximity prior is not used in this stage.
Initialization and Solver. The trajectory is initialized by interpolating the keyframes; frames with-
out demonstrated contact, such as approach and release, instead follow the MANO motion while
matching every keyframe exactly. Keyframes that violate the collision constraints are released,
and the remaining keyframes, indexed by Kf, are fixed. After repairing the initial trajectory to be
collision-free at every frame, we run up to 40 Gauss–Newton SQP iterations with a trust region
and an Armijo line search. Collision checks are performed at every frame and, where applicable,
at 16 substeps between consecutive frames. Substep checks are skipped for intervals with residual
between-frame collisions after repair, which are reported, and for intervals between adjacent fixed
keyframes. Trial updates are accepted only if they pass all frame-wise checks and all applicable
substep checks, maintaining frame-wise collision feasibility. For DexYCB, the hand and object are
shifted vertically together so that the object rests on the table, and the object is then settled once in
simulation. The rigid transformation from the pre-settling to the settled object pose is then applied
uniformly to the entire hand–object trajectory.

DYNAMICS RETARGETING DETAILS AND ADDITIONAL RESULTS
Both methods use warm-started MPC and interpolate control nodes to produce commands at 100 Hz.
The horizon H is measured in control intervals, so the prediction-window duration equals H times
the control-node spacing. SPIDER retains its default configuration: a control-node spacing of 0.4 s,
H = 4, a parallel batch size of 1024, and at most 16 optimization iterations. Our method uses a
control-node spacing of 0.06 s, H = 5, a parallel batch size of 64, and at most 40 iterations. The
resulting prediction windows are 1.6 s and 0.3 s, respectively. We retain method-specific temporal
configurations because imposing the same prediction window can disadvantage either optimizer.
To examine the performance–runtime trade-off, we compare DexForge and Contact-aware+SPIDER
on a fixed subset of ablation sequences successfully retargeted by both methods across all tested
budgets. After object drops, smaller tracking errors do not necessarily indicate better retargeting;
failures are instead reflected in the success rates reported above. For each method, we vary only the
optimization iteration limit and measure total optimization time, disabling SPIDER’s early stopping
to control the budget through the iteration limit. All other MPC settings remain at their respective
defaults. Both methods use identical kinematic references, stable-contact guidance, and simulation

REAL-WORLD OPEN-LOOP REPLAY DETAILS AND RESULTS
We replay optimized wrist and finger references on a Unitree G1 equipped with a LEAP Hand v1.
The hand is mounted on the right wrist using a custom adapter, whose wrist-to-hand transformation
is incorporated into the forward-kinematic model. Before replay, we place the object at the origin of
a table-mounted motion-capture rigid-body frame with a predefined orientation to establish its initial
pose. We then transform the precomputed wrist trajectory accordingly, select a reachable starting
point, and move the robot to the corresponding configuration. A 100 Hz process uses Unitree’s arm
IK solver to generate arm joint-position targets and sends them together with the precomputed finger-
position targets. Motion-capture torso poses compensate for body motion to preserve the intended
world-frame wrist trajectory. A separate 50 Hz process runs NVIDIA’s Decoupled WBC to main-

Results.
We conduct 10 trials for each of the five objects, resetting the object to its predefined
initial pose before each trial. Since object poses are not tracked during execution, a trial is considered
successful if the object is stably grasped and held for 5 s without dropping. This criterion measures
executability rather than tracking accuracy. The success counts are 10/10 for the gelatin box, 8/10
for the pudding box, 6/10 for the wood block, 10/10 for the power drill, and 6/10 for the tomato soup
can, yielding an overall success rate of 80% (40/50). Videos are available on the project page.
