ESP: Energy-Score Policy for One-Step Multimodal Action Generation
Lilika Makabe1,2, Heecheol Kim1, and Yasuyuki Matsushita1
1Microsoft Research Asia - Tokyo
2Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, the University of Osaka
lilika.makabe@gmail.com, {heecheolkim, yamatsushita}@microsoft.com
Abstract— Generative action models based on diffusion and
flow matching have been increasingly adopted in vision-
language-action (VLA) policies for their ability to capture di-
verse behaviors, including multiple valid action sequences under
the same observation and instruction. Their iterative sampling
procedures, however, require repeated network evaluations to
generate each action chunk, increasing inference latency in
closed-loop control. We propose ESP (Energy-Score Policy),
a teacher-free approach that maps policy context and noise
directly to an action chunk in a single network evaluation.
ESP trains the action head with the energy score rather than
mean squared error. Whereas squared-error regression targets
the conditional mean, the energy score is strictly proper: its
expected value is uniquely minimized by the target distribution.
This provides a principled objective for learning multimodal
action distributions without iterative sampling, with exact
recovery at the population optimum when the model can rep-
resent the target distribution. Experiments on both simulation
and real-world manipulation tasks demonstrate competitive
task success with substantially lower action-generation latency
than the flow matching baseline. These results support direct
distributional learning as an efficient alternative to iterative
generative robot policies. Project page
I. INTRODUCTION
Diffusion and flow matching models are increasingly used
in robot policies, including vision-language-action (VLA)
models, to represent diverse behaviors conditioned on ob-
servations and instructions [1–3]. These models can capture
multiple valid action sequences for the same context rather
than restrict the policy to a single deterministic prediction.
Their standard sampling procedures, however, require re-
peated network evaluations to transform noise into an action
chunk. Therefore, their action generation is inherently time-
consuming, motivating faster one-step action generation with
a single network evaluation.
We propose ESP (Energy-Score Policy), where we cast
conditional action generation as strictly proper distributional
regression and generate an action chunk in a single network
evaluation (Fig. 1). The proposed ESP predicts the entire
multimodal distribution of actions that are valid for a given
context. We use the energy-score objective [4, 5]; in ex-
pectation over the data, it becomes smallest exactly when
the generated actions match the distribution of the training
data (demonstrations), and larger for every other distribution.

Unlike previous one-step and few-step approaches, such
as distillation and consistency-based methods [6–9], ESP
directly trains on the training data alone in a single stage,
with no pretrained teacher, no trajectory or velocity targets,
and no auxiliary critic. Different from recent teacher-free
generators [10, 11], whose objectives can also be minimized
by distributions other than the training one, ESP retains this
uniqueness while keeping single-evaluation inference.
To apply ESP to an existing VLA model, we retain its
pretrained vision-language backbone and policy conditioning
unchanged and replace its action head with our ESP for
one-step action generation. The VLM backbone can remain
frozen while the ESP action-head parameters are trained
without teacher distillation. For each training context, we
generate several action chunks from different noise inputs,
and the energy score evaluates the generated chunks against
the demonstrated chunk and against one another. At infer-
ence, one single noise draw produces one action chunk in one
network evaluation, without multi-step numerical integration
or candidate ranking.

Contributions. Our chief contributions are:
• We cast conditional action generation as strictly proper
distributional regression and propose an energy-score
policy (ESP) for action generation.
• Unlike diffusion/flow matching policy that requires
multi-step integration, our ESP can learn the conditional

• With the same action-head architecture, in one-step,

further improves the mean success rate given the same
inference time budget to the flow matching policy.
II. RELATED WORK
Learning robot actions from demonstrations is a condi-
tional distribution modeling problem: the same observation
and instruction can correspond to several valid behaviors.
We review multimodal prediction and generative policies,
followed by objectives for efficient one-step generation.
Multimodal prediction and generative action policies.
Early approaches modeled multimodal conditional distribu-
tions with mixture density networks [12], conditional latent-
variable models [13], and conditional adversarial genera-
tors [14]. Generative moment matching networks [15] train
feedforward generators using Gaussian-kernel MMD (Maxi-
mum Mean Discrepancy) [16]. These Gaussian-kernel MMD
objectives require selecting a kernel bandwidth, whereas our
energy-score objective does not require the kernel-bandwidth
hyperparameter. Diffusion and flow matching subsequently
emerged as expressive high-dimensional samplers that trans-
port noise to data through a learned denoising process or
vector field [17–19].
Representing such multimodal, high-dimensional distri-
butions is particularly relevant to robot action prediction,
where the same visual observation and language instruction
can correspond to several successful behaviors. Diffusion
Policy [1] models action sequences with conditional de-
noising, while more recent VLAs such as π0 [2], π0.5 [3],
GR00T N1 [20], and FLOWER [21] use flow matching
to generate action chunks. These methods can represent
multimodal action distributions, but require multiple network
evaluations. Our work preserves the stochastic & multimodal
action representation while achieving action generation only
with a single network evaluation.
One-step generation and distributional objectives. Reduc-
ing the cost of iterative generation has motivated distillation,
consistency training, and objectives designed for few-step or
one-step sampling. Distillation methods compress a multi-
step model into a shorter sampler [6], while consistency
models can learn outputs that agree along a diffusion tra-
jectory [7]. In robot learning, FlowPolicy imposes self-
consistency on a flow field [8], MeanFlow-VLA learns an
average velocity [9], and Let It Be Simple retains flow
matching while emphasizing the high-noise regime used by
one-step inference [22]. Drifting methods construct a training
target by moving the generator’s own samples toward the
data with a data-adaptive drift, then train the generator to
match this detached (stop-gradient) target [10, 23]. However,
without additional assumptions, even globally minimizing
the ideal population drifting loss does not guarantee that the
generated distribution matches the data distribution [10].
Efficiency alone, however, leaves open how a direct
one-step generator should learn a multimodal distribution.
Multiple-choice learning, multiple-hypothesis prediction, and
IMLE emphasize the best-matching candidates through hard
or relaxed assignments [24–27]. Forward XM follows this
candidate-selection principle: for each training example, it
evaluates the loss under several sampled noise inputs and
updates the generator using the lowest-loss candidate [11].
While effective, with a fixed finite candidate budget, the
hard-min endpoint-distance objective is not, in general,
strictly proper: its minimum does not necessarily correspond
to the data distribution.
Energy-score training instead follows the statistical lineage
of proper scoring rules. The multivariate energy score com-
bines distances to the observation with pairwise distances
between generated samples. Under finite first moments, its
expected value is uniquely minimized by the data distri-
bution [4]. DISCO Nets train conditional generators using
an energy-score objective and apply them to 3D hand pose
estimation from depth images [5]. We also use this estab-
lished objective for one-step action generation, scoring the
conditional action samples produced by the deployed action
head. Minimizing its expected value recovers the target
conditional distribution when the model can represent it.
III. METHOD
At each control step, we seek to sample from the demon-
strated conditional action distribution without integrating a
learned sampling trajectory. Rather than learning a time-
dependent velocity field and selecting a numerical solver,
we parameterize the action generator itself. We first formalize
this direct generator and explain why adding noise to a point-
wise regressor is insufficient. We then describe its energy-
score training objective and one-step sampler (Fig. 2).

The generator fθ induces an implicit conditional distribu-
tion qθ(· | c), which we can sample, but its density cannot
be evaluated. We therefore train it with the energy score,
which needs only samples but not likelihood. For a predictive
distribution Q on Rd with finite β-th moment, the energy
score [4] is

and discrete predictive distributions. The energy score is
strictly proper: when the observation is drawn from any
distribution with finite β-th moment, the expected score is
uniquely minimized by predicting that same distribution.

D. Direct One-Step Generation
Flow and diffusion models realize a push-forward by
numerically integrating a learned field. For example, rectified
flow [19, 29, 30] introduces a time variable t ∈[0, 1] and
an intermediate state xt ∈Rd that transports the prior
sample x0 = z into an action chunk x1. It learns a velocity
field vθ(c, x, t) and obtains x1 = z +
R 1
0 vθ(c, xt, t) dt,
typically through several discrete solver steps, each requiring
one network evaluation. Our model instead represents the
endpoint transport directly as x = fθ(c, z). At inference,
one draw z ∼pz and one evaluation of fθ produce an action
chunk, so the number of function evaluations (NFE) is one,
regardless of the training sample count K.
ESP’s computational path does not restrict the policy to a
single action per context, because multimodality is achieved
by the energy-score objective used to train fθ, not by whether
an action is produced in one network evaluation or by many
integration steps. In principle, any conditional network that
can take as input a context c and a noise vector z can serve as
fθ, whether a multilayer perceptron (MLP), a convolutional
network, or a transformer. Our approach can therefore be
naturally applied to existing policy architectures, including
VLA-based policies, without modifying their architectures.
By replacing the pointwise loss with the energy-score loss,
ESP enables one-step action generation while achieving
performance comparable to iterative generative policies.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate one-step multimodal generation through syn-
thetic regression, VLA-based action generation on LIBERO,
and real-world robot manipulation.
Hyperparameters and comparisons. We compare ESP
against two alternatives under matched network and train-
ing data: XM, a hard minimum-of-K candidate-selection
method, and native flow matching, an iterative sampler. ESP
uses Eq. (5) with β=1. Both ESP and XM generate K=8
candidates from independent noise samples during training.
XM uses the objective Lθ = mini ∥fθ(c, zi) −y∥, which
is a fixed-endpoint adaptation of Forward XM [11]: only
the lowest-loss candidate contributes to the gradient. At
inference, both methods draw one sample without candidate
selection and use NFE=1. Iterative flow or diffusion base-
lines retain their native samplers, with NFE stated for every
experiment. We compare objectives through distributional
metrics when the target distribution is known and through
rollout success otherwise.
B. Controlled Multimodal Regression
Dataset and implementation. We construct a controlled
bimodal regression problem, which we call TwoBranch, to
evaluate how well each method recovers a known conditional
distribution. We draw c ∼U[0.05, 1] and set y = s√c+0.08ϵ
with equiprobable sign s ∈{−1, +1} and ϵ ∼N(0, 1).
For each input condition c, it produces two symmetric target
branches centered at ±√c. Figure 3 shows samples from
this ground-truth distribution alongside the outputs of the
trained models. All methods use the same MLP architecture
with three hidden layers of width 128 and SiLU activations,
with inputs adapted to each objective. We train them for 30k
iterations at a constant learning rate of 2×10−4. We evaluate
the same trained flow matching model with either one or ten
Euler steps. We count a prediction as a match if its output
differs from either center by at most 0.24, which is 3× the
data noise standard deviation of 0.08. Match is the percentage
of predictions that satisfy this criterion, and we use it as the
distributional-fidelity metric.
Results. MSE regression concentrates its predictions at
the conditional mean rather than the two target branches,
consistent with the characterization in Proposition 1. Flow
matching learns a velocity field that is integrated to generate
samples. With only one step, its predictions also concentrate
between the two branches. MSE and one-step flow obtain
Match rates of 0.7% and 4.2%, respectively. ESP and XM
both generate the two branches in one network evaluation,
but XM produces more outliers in the low-density region be-
tween them. ESP reaches a Match rate of 96.2%, comparable
to 91.9% of XM and 97.1% of ten Euler step flow matching.
Thus, directly training the endpoint generator with the energy
score captures both branches and approaches the Match rate
of iterative flow without repeated network evaluations.

C. π0.5 on LIBERO
π0.5 [3] is a VLA policy with a flow matching action
expert that requires multiple Euler steps at inference. We
evaluate whether replacing this iterative decoder with a one-
step ESP preserves closed-loop task success.
Model adaptation. We freeze the π0.5 vision-language back-
bone and retain its tokenization and conditioning. We train
only the action-side modules and replace the flow matching
action head with a one-step ESP or XM head fθ(c, z).
Since the architecture assumes diffusion time-step t as an
additional input, we simply fix it to t=1 during the training
and inference time for both ESP and XM. All methods train
from scratch on the action side with random initialization.
The optimizer, schedule, and initialization are shared, so the
substitution isolates the action-head objective and its sampler.
Training and evaluation. We train on the SPATIAL, OB-
JECT, GOAL, and LONG10 suites of LIBERO [31] with batch
size 32, AdamW, global gradient clipping at 1.0, and EMA
decay 0.999. We use a linear warm-up from approximately
5×10−9 to 5×10−5 over the first 10,000 optimization steps,
after which the learning rate remains constant.
At each generation, the policy predicts ten actions as
a chunk and executes the first five. All models have the
same number of trainable parameters and use the same base
checkpoint for the frozen backbone. We train each model for
60k steps with seeds 42, 43, and 44, and evaluate the last
checkpoint using the fixed evaluation protocol. Each suite
contains ten tasks with 20 rollouts per task and seed, totaling
2,400 rollouts per head.
Closed-loop success. We show the suite-wise LIBERO suc-
cess rates at 60k training iterations in Fig. 4. ESP exhibits
competitive success rates over the ten-step flow sampler. Its
advantage over XM under the same one-step architecture
further supports energy scoring over hard-min candidate
assignment for learning effective action policies.
Latency. Table I compares inference latency across methods.
We measure the latency using π0.5 on a single A100 GPU
with batch size one and action horizon H=10, using 15
warm-up and 100 timed calls per path. Cached inference

V. LIMITATIONS
The main limitation of ESP is its training cost. Because the
energy score compares samples, each update draws K noise-
conditioned candidates per example instead of one, and its
pairwise repulsion term evaluates O(K2) sample distances,
with both the forward and backward passes running over
all K candidates. This increases computation and activation
memory compared to single-sample flow matching training.
Indeed, during π0.5 training, each flow matching optimizer
step runs about 3.81× faster than an ESP step when K=8.
The per-step overhead is partly offset by sample efficiency.
As Fig. 7 shows, ESP reaches a success rate comparable to
flow matching (93.0% vs. 93.5%) using only about a quarter
as many optimizer steps and expert actions, so the two reach
comparable success in nearly the same wall-clock time (ESP
at 15k steps in 4.78h vs. flow matching at 60k steps in 4.98h).
Although this sample efficiency compensates for much of
the training-time overhead in this experiment, reducing the
training overhead remains an important direction for future
work. ESP’s computational advantage instead appears at
deployment, where it generates each action chunk in a single
network evaluation (NFE=1).
VI. CONCLUSION
We studied conditional action generation as a direct noise-
to-action map rather than an integrated trajectory. The energy
score trains the generator to match the full conditional distri-
bution from demonstrations with a single target per context.
Building on this, we introduced ESP, a one-step generative
action policy that directly maps policy context and noise to
an action chunk in a single forward pass. Across simulated
and real-world manipulation tasks, ESP achieves competitive
task success while substantially reducing action-generation
latency compared to flow matching policies that require
iterative sampling. These results demonstrate that ESP can
be an efficient alternative to iterative generative policies
for robotic control. Beyond reducing latency, our results
suggest a different allocation of computational resources
for generative robot policies. Eliminating iterative sampling
allows the same time budget to be used for larger action
heads rather than repeated evaluations of a smaller model.
The improved performance of wider one-step action heads
indicates that, under practical control-frequency constraints,
increasing policy capacity may be a more effective use of
compute than iterative action generation.
