# VAMPS: Visual and Motor Policies from Sampling-Based Planning 

Mohamed Yassine Kabouri 1,2, Pietro Noah Crestaz 1,3, Quang-Nam Nguyen 2, Qilong Cheng 2,Ludovic Righetti 2,4, and Nicolas Mansard 1,4

Abstract — Learning robot policies directly on physical sys-tems remains difficult because data collection is costly and pol-icy exploration can be unsafe. We introduce Visual and Motor Policies from Sampling-Based Planning (VAMPS), a framework that uses Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control to train reusable policies without human demonstrations. VAMPS supports two training modes. For one-step proprioceptive poli-cies, it operates iteratively in simulation: the policy warm-starts MPPI, and the refined trajectories provide new supervision as the policy changes. A learned terminal value improves short-horizon planning, while an Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) critic guides the policy update. Iterative refinement outperforms training once on frozen MPPI data, and we transfer the learned locomotion policy to a Unitree Go2. For visuomotor policies, VAMPS operates directly from real-robot data. MPPI uses task-specific state estimates to plan and execute trajectories while recording RGB and sensor observations on a Flexiv Rizon 10S. Action Chunking with Transformers predicts action chunks, reducing the effective prediction horizon, and is trained offline on this fixed dataset. We demonstrate visuomotor pick-and-place and force-aware whiteboard erasing. In the latter task, the policy additionally observes the measured 6-D wrench and desired normal force. These results show that VAMPS can learn policies either in simulation followed by hardware transfer or directly from autonomously collected real-robot data. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Learning directly on physical robots, through interaction and sensor feedback, remains a major challenge, despite being the most direct route to acquiring effective sensori-motor behaviors. This is especially compelling for contact-rich robotics, including pick-and-place manipulation, force-regulated interaction, and quadruped locomotion. Current learning methods do not yet solve this problem at scale: they typically rely on human demonstrations or move exploration to simulation before transferring the resulting policy to hardware. This work takes a step toward methods that can autonomously and efficiently collect sensorimotor experience on robots and turn it into better policies. This objective has been pursued through three main routes. 

Imitation learning methods such as Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) [1] and Diffusion Policy [2] clone human teleoperation and learn rich behaviors. Their bottle-neck is data: demonstrations are expensive, performance is bounded by the operator, and cloning a fixed dataset leads to compounding errors [3]. DAgger [3] corrects those states by querying an expert policy on states visited by the learned pol-icy. Reinforcement learning [4] needs no demonstration, but it has to explore, and exploration is a bottleneck on contact-rich tasks with short episodes [5]. Trajectory Optimization

(TO) [6], [7], in contrast, optimizes individual rollouts for a given cost. While gradient-based TO relies on derivatives that are challenging to compute in contact-rich tasks, sampling-based planners [8] handle non-smooth contact dynamics and run in parallel on GPUs [9]. Yet their search space scales exponentially with the planning horizon [10]. They are commonly used in receding horizon, which requires online replanning and does not directly produce a reusable policy. The complementarity between trajectory optimization and policy learning has long been recognized, notably by Guided Policy Search (GPS) [11] and subsequent optimizer-policy coupling methods [12], [13]. This perspective combines the precision of model-based optimization with the fast execution of a learned controller, while avoiding direct exploration by the learned policy. We build on this principle and use MPPI as a behavior generator from a model and a task cost. In this work, we introduce Visual and Motor Policies from Sampling-Based Planning (VAMPS), a framework that distills trajectories generated by MPPI into reusable robot policies without human demonstrations or simulator gra-dients. MPPI plans with a model, a task cost and task-specific state estimates, while the learned policy maps the observations available at deployment directly to actions. VAMPS supports two training modes that share this planner-to-policy principle. 

VAMPS-Iterative trains a one-step proprioceptive policy in simulation. Small prediction errors can move such a policy away from the states in a fixed training dataset and compound over time [3]. The policy therefore repeatedly warm-starts MPPI, which refines its behavior and provides new supervision as the policy evolves. This loop does not re-quire simulator gradients. A learned terminal value improves short-horizon planning, while an IQL [14] critic guides the policy update through Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR) [15]. We train the policy in simulation and transfer it to a real robot. 

VAMPS-Batch uses MPPI to autonomously collect areal-robot dataset and train a visuomotor policy from it. MPPI uses task-specific state estimates to plan and execute trajectories on the robot while RGB images, robot sensors and actions are recorded. ACT [1] predicts action chunks, reducing the effective prediction horizon. For the tasks considered here, we collect the MPPI episodes once, freeze the dataset and train ACT offline. At deployment, ACT maps images and robot sensors directly to action chunks and does not use the task-specific state estimates. Our contributions are threefold. (1) We introduce

GPS [11] and its visuomotor extension [16] pioneered the idea of using local TO controllers to supervise policy learning. In the same cycle of ideas, [12] formalized the TO-policy coupling via ADMM to enforce agreement between them. [13] later used augmented-Lagrangian updates and sensitivity information to improve this coupling. CACTO-SL instead combines TO with actor-critic learning and uses the policy to warm-start the optimizer [17]. More recently, [18] trained diffusion policies to warm-start gradient-based TO. Their method alternates policy learning and trajectory refinement, and uses the feedback gains returned by the optimizer in the training loss. These methods rely mainly on gradient-based TO and differentiable dynamics. PI 2GPS [19] replaces the gradient-based local optimizer with the derivative-free PI 2 algorithm. However, it initial-izes the local policies from kinesthetic demonstrations and pretrains its visual features on a pose-prediction task. This work studies how to scale the GPS idea using modern tools from derivative-free optimization [20], offline RL [21] and imitation learning [1]. VAMPS-Iterative uses GPU-parallel MPPI in receding horizon. It requires a model and a task cost but does not require simulator derivatives, feedback gains or a human warm start. The policy repeatedly warm-starts MPPI, which refines its behavior and provides new super-vision. To improve short-horizon planning, we introduce a learned terminal value trained on the planner transitions. To filter the planner actions, we train an IQL critic [14] on MPPI trajectories and policy rollouts, and use its advantage in AWR [15] to give more weight to high-advantage actions. Visuomotor imitation methods such as ACT [1] and Diffu-sion Policy [2] predict sequences of actions from visual ob-servations. Action-sequence prediction reduces the effective prediction horizon and limits the accumulation of one-step errors. These methods are commonly trained from human demonstrations. VAMPS-Batch instead uses MPPI to collect real-robot RGB, sensor and action episodes, and trains ACT on the resulting fixed dataset. Task-specific state estimates guide MPPI during data collection but are not provided to the deployed policy. For force-aware erasing, MPPI records RGB images, the measured wrench and the desired normal force, and ACT learns a force-conditioned policy from these episodes. We deploy the proprioceptive policy learned by VAMPS-Iterative on a Unitree Go2 and the visuomotor policies learned by VAMPS-Batch on a Flexiv Rizon 10S. III. P RELIMINARIES

B. VAMPS-Batch 

VAMPS-Iterative uses a one-step proprioceptive policy. Small prediction errors can move this policy away from the states in a fixed training dataset and compound over time [3]. Our frozen-data baseline show this effect in Sec. V-F. VAMPS-Iterative addresses it by repeatedly refining the policy trajectories with MPPI and using them as new super-vision. VAMPS-Batch instead uses ACT [1], which predicts an action trajectory rather than one action at each step. This reduces the effective decision horizon and motivates training the visuomotor policy once on a fixed dataset. VAMPS-Batch uses MPPI to autonomously collect this dataset on the real robot. MPPI evaluates candidate rollouts in simulation using task-specific state estimates and executes the refined controls on the robot. For example, it uses the cube position for pick-and-place and the contact height z

for erasing. During execution, we record synchronized RGB images, robot observations, task sensors and actions. We freeze the resulting episodes and train ACT offline with (5). At deployment, ACT does not use the task-specific state estimates. Its evaluation is reported in Sec. VI. 

C. Algorithm Summary 

Algorithms 1 and 2 summarize the two VAMPS modes. Both use MPPI to generate supervision from a model and a task cost. VAMPS-Iterative updates the planner, policy and value functions together: the current policy warm-starts MPPI, and each refined trajectory updates the policy before the next outer iteration. VAMPS-Batch instead completes data collection first, freezes the recorded real-robot episodes and then trains ACT offline. V. VAMPS-I TERATIVE EXPERIMENTS & R ESULTS

Setup. We vary the penalty coefficient ρ and the batch size, one at a time, over the values shown in the legend of Fig. 6. All other hyperparameters keep their values from Sec. V-A. All runs use a Gaussian policy.

Both VAMPS-Batch experiments run on a 7-DOF Flexiv Rizon 10S with a gripper and a side-mounted Orbbec Femto Bolt camera. MPPI evaluates 2048 sampled rollouts in sim-ulation over a horizon T =20 . It replans in receding horizon and executes the refined controls on the robot at 25 Hz through an impedance controller. We record RGB images, sensor observations and actions, and train ACT on these real-robot episodes using the HuggingFace LeRobot implemen-tation [30], [1]. At inference, the full action trajectory is executed open-loop before ACT is queried again. 

A. Experiment 1: Visuomotor pick-and-place 

Setup. The robot grasps a cube from a randomized posi-tion on the table and drops it in a fixed bowl. We localize the cube with HSV color filtering and a calibrated homography. This estimate is used only by MPPI during data collection. We autonomously collect 100 MPPI episodes. The policy predicts a chunk of Tu=100 joint-position targets. 

Result. The VAMPS-Batch policy succeeds in 8 of 10 trials from randomized cube positions. The middle row of Fig. 2 shows four frames of one successful execution. 

B. Experiment 2: Visuomotor and force-aware erasing policy 

Setup. The robot erases a whiteboard placed on the table. The eraser has to stay in contact with the board while the arm moves over it, so MPPI needs the height at which to hold the eraser: too high and it loses contact and does not erase, too low and it presses through the board. During data collection, we obtain this height with the task-space impedance-admittance nonlinear MPC of [31]. It regulates the contact and gives the z position MPPI plans around. This contact-height estimate is used only during data collection. We collect demonstrations at discrete desired normal-force setpoints from 5 to 14 N to cover different contact conditions. The resulting dataset contains 84 episodes ( 1.59 hours on the robot). For this task, ACT also takes a second camera mounted on the wrist. Its observation at time t contains the two synchronized images and a 14 -D vector with the seven joint positions, the measured 6-D wrench and the desired normal force. ACT reads the wrench at t without averaging a force window. The policy predicts a chunk of Tu=50 joint-position targets ( 2 s at 25 Hz), executed open-loop as in pick-and-place. Result. The same VAMPS-Batch checkpoint succeeds in four consecutive trials and erases the writing in all of them. Fig. 7 shows the desired and measured normal force during one successful trial. The bottom row of Fig. 2 shows four frames of the same execution. These preliminary re-sults show that VAMPS-Batch can train visuomotor policies from autonomously collected real-robot data without human demonstrations. We plan to extend this mode to more sensor-based tasks in future work. VII. C ONCLUSIONS 

We presented VAMPS, a framework that uses a sampling-based planner to train reusable robot policies without human demonstrations or a differentiable simulator. We studied two modes of this framework. VAMPS-Iterative alternates MPPI trajectory refinement and policy learning for one-step proprioceptive policies. Our experiments show that iterative refinement outperforms training once on frozen MPPI data, simple planner-policy coupling is sufficient, and a learned terminal value improves short-horizon planning. We transfer the resulting locomotion policy to a Unitree Go2. VAMPS-Batch uses MPPI to autonomously collect a fixed real-robot dataset and trains chunked visuomotor policies offline with ACT. The dataset contains RGB images, robot observations, task sensors and actions. We demonstrate this mode on pick-and-place and force-aware whiteboard erasing with a Flexiv Rizon 10S. The VAMPS-Batch hardware results are preliminary, and we plan to extend it to more sensor-based tasks. For long-horizon tasks, TAMP [32] could provide a high-level planner. Scene generators such as SimFoundry [33] could build sim-ulations from real footage, allowing VAMPS to collect data and learn visuomotor policies in a real-to-sim-to-real loop. Future work will also study tighter planner–policy feedback in architectures such as TD-MPC2 [34] and CAST [35]. REFERENCES [1] T. Z. Zhao, V.
