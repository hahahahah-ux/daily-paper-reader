# Robot Learning with Visual Predicted Force 

Haonan Chen 1,∗, Feiyang Wu 1,∗, Yuxiang Ma 2, Mustafa Mete 1, Pengfei Ye 2,Junxuan Shen 2, Cheng Zhu 3, Aurora Ruggeri 1, Kelvin Cheung 1,Jiayuan Mao 3, Edward Adelson 2, Jiajun Wu 4, Robert D. Howe 1, Yilun Du 1

Abstract — Force-aware manipulation typically relies on spe-cialized force or tactile sensors. We show that force-aware manipulation can instead be achieved through visual force prediction from the deformation of a compliant Fin Ray gripper. Our approach trains two models. First, we train a visual force estimator on calibration data and use it to annotate task demon-strations with force estimates. Second, we train an action–force proposal policy on these force-augmented demonstrations to jointly generate candidate robot actions and their associated forces. At test time, we sample candidate actions and the forces they are expected to produce, then execute the action whose pre-dicted force is closest to a target from the demonstrations. We evaluate our approach on berry picking, empty-can grasping, in-hand reorientation, and plug insertion. Our results show that visual force prediction can guide inference-time action selection for contact-rich manipulation without requiring force or tactile sensors at deployment. 

I. INTRODUCTION Controlling interaction forces is important for contact-rich manipulation, where excessive or insufficient force can damage objects or cause task failure. This is particularly important when grasping soft objects (e.g., fruits, soft pack-ages), handling deformable objects, or performing precise insertion [1], [2]. Existing robotic systems obtain such feed-back using wrist-mounted force/torque sensors [3], flexi-ble printed circuit board (FPCB)-based tactile sensors [4], [5], or optical tactile sensors such as GelSight Mini [6]. However, these sensing solutions introduce hardware and integration overhead and can require changes to the manip-ulator’s form factor and mechanical structure. Commercial six-axis force/torque sensors from manufacturers such as ATI and Bota can cost several thousand dollars per unit, excluding robot-specific mounting hardware and system in-tegration [7], [8]. FPCB-based tactile sensors often require custom fabrication, wiring, and calibration, while optical tactile sensors require embedded cameras, illumination, and compliant sensing surfaces that may wear under repeated contact. These limitations motivate a natural question: Can a robot estimate force directly from visual observations and use these estimates to guide contact-rich manipulation? In this work, we investigate learning force-aware manip-ulation using visual observations of a compliant Fin Ray gripper. A. Hardware-Based Force and Tactile Sensing 

Dedicated force and tactile sensors obtain contact informa-tion through several sensing mechanisms. Force-torque sen-sors provide accurate measurements but require mounting, wiring, and calibration, while piezoelectric tactile sensors capture high-frequency stimuli [11]. Optical tactile sensors instead image a compliant sensing surface [12]. GelSight re-covers contact geometry and force-related signals [13], [14], with subsequent designs exploring compact integration and biomimetic structures [15], [16], [17]. Related approaches reconstruct contact geometry and estimate forces from tactile images [18], [19], [20], while GelSight Fin Ray variants integrate optical tactile sensing into compliant fingers [21], [22]. Another direction observes the gripper structure itself. De Barrie et al. [9] use finite-element simulation data to train a model that predicts contact forces and stress distribu-tions from finger deformation images. Kitamura et al. [10] relate camera-observed finger deformation to contact forces measured by 16 sensors embedded in the grasped object. Stuhne et al. [23] introduce SofIA, which uses a depth camera to observe the state of its compliant fingers. Forces for Free estimates contact force from visual observations of a tendon-driven underactuated hand optimized for force observability [24]. Building on visual observation of com-pliant structures, our work uses estimated forces to label demonstrations and train a policy whose generated force values guide action selection. 

B. Force and Tactile Feedback for Robotic Manipulation 

Force and tactile information support both contact percep-tion and manipulation control. For perception, prior work uses touch to assess and improve grasps [25], [1], [3], estimate object pose from contact [26], and track object shape and pose during manipulation [27]. These contact cues also support grasp adjustment, sliding, pivoting, and rolling [28], [29], [30]. Policies combining vision and touch extend these capabilities to learned manipulation and in-hand rotation [31], [2], with further work incorporating audio [32] or adapting vision-only policies to force/torque sensing [33]. For control, tactile feedback supports cable following and needle threading [34], [35], while tactile-informed dynamics support predictive control for packing [36]. Hierarchical and reactive approaches integrate contact feedback into broader manipulation systems [37], [38], [39]. Other methods ex-plicitly learn force or compliance control: RETAF separates tactile grasp-force control from arm pose prediction [40], Adaptive Compliance Policy learns variable compliance from demonstrations [41], and UMI-FT uses force-labeled demon-strations collected with a handheld interface to learn motion, grasp force, and stiffness [42]. Our method uses visual force estimates as training labels and policy-generated forces for inference-time action selection, without dedicated contact-sensing hardware at deployment. 

C. Test-Time Searching 

Prior work has explored inference-time search over sam-pled robot actions using learned interaction models [43] and diffusion-based planning [44], [45], [46], [47]. Frozen generative robot policies can be improved at test time by ranking action samples with auxiliary objectives. Value-Guided Policy Steering (V-GPS) [48] ranks policy samples using an offline-learned value function. Bidirectional Decod-ing [49] selects action chunks using consistency with previ-ous decisions and contrast among future plans. Generative Predictive Control (GPC) [50] ranks and refines policy sam-ples using a learned world model. DynaGuide [51] guides generative policies with learned dynamics, VLS [52] uses VLM-based trajectory rewards, and TouchGuide [53] steers pretrained generative visuomotor policies [54], [55] by using a tactile-informed Contact Physical Model to refine early visual-policy samples toward physically feasible contact. Our method uses visual force estimates to label demonstrations for policy training. At test time, the policy jointly generates candidate actions and force values. We select the action whose policy-generated force is closest to the target. The visual force estimator does not score candidates at test time. III. METHOD Our method consists of three stages. First, we collect paired images of gripper deformation and reference force measurements to train a visual force estimator. Second, we collect task demonstrations and use the trained estimator to annotate them with forces and select a target force for each task. Third, we train an action–force proposal policy on the annotated demonstrations. At test time, we sample candidate actions and the forces they are expected to produce, then execute the action whose predicted force is closest to the task’s target. We first formulate the problem, then describe each stage. Fig. 2 illustrates visual force estimation and inference-time action selection.

B. Force Calibration and Visual Estimator Training 

We estimate the axial interaction force from the visible deformation of the compliant Fin Ray gripper. The wrist-mounted camera provides a close-up view of the gripper fingers, whose internal stripes and overall geometry change under external loading. Our visual force estimator uses only this visual deformation representation as input. 

Visual Force Data Collection. Training the visual force estimator requires paired observations of gripper deformation and ground-truth force measurements. To collect these pairs, we mount 19 3D-printed contact shells (Fig. 3(b)) on a reference force sensor. During contact, the fixtures transmit the force applied by the gripper to the reference sensor, while the wrist-mounted camera records the corresponding deformation of the Fin Ray fingers. We collect data using both a Mitsumi MMS101 resistive force sensor and an ATI six-axis force/torque (F/T) sensor as reference sensors. We press the gripper against the fixtures at diverse contact locations and with varying force magnitudes, ranging from gentle contact to firm loading. We collect 117 episodes totaling 47.5 minutes. Images are recorded at 30 Hz and force measurements at approximately 20 Hz. We align the streams by timestamp and use only Fz to train the force estimator.

C. Manipulation Evaluation 

Experiments Design. We evaluate our method on four force-sensitive manipulation tasks. Grasping an empty soda can tests force regulation when handling a thin-walled object: too little force causes slippage, whereas too much force permanently deforms the can. Berry picking additionally re-quires precise control at a much smaller scale, as the berry’s small size and compliance produce subtle deformation cues and leave only a narrow margin between a stable grasp and crushing. In-hand reorientation requires the robot to pivot a metal plate upright without slipping or lifting it from the table, while plug insertion requires sufficient gripping force to prevent the plug from slipping within the gripper during insertion. Success requires transferring the can or berry without damage, reorienting the plate upright, or fully inserting the plug. 

Demonstration Data and Policy Training. For each task, we collect 40 demonstrations using the ARX robot arm. We then freeze the visual force estimator gφ and use it to annotate every demonstration timestep with the estimated force magnitude ˜ ft . The resulting action–force pairs are used to train a stochastic policy that jointly generates an end-effector action and its associated force value. 

Compared Methods. Both procedures use the same trained policy checkpoint and differ only in how policy samples are selected at inference time:  

> •

Policy (single sample): We draw one action–force sample from the policy, ignore its generated force component, and execute the corresponding end-effector action.  

> •

Ours (Force Search): We draw K = 32 action–force samples from the same policy and execute the action whose generated force component is closest to the task-specific target force. For each task, we select a target force from the estimated force values in its demonstration dataset. 

Evaluation Protocol. We evaluate each method for 15 trials on every task. Both methods are evaluated under the same initial-condition distribution. We report the number and percentage of successful trials. V. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

Our work has several limitations. First, our method re-quires a task-specific target force, which is obtained from successful demonstrations in our experiments. Automatically inferring and adapting the desired force based on object prop-erties, contact state, and task phase remains an important di-rection for future work. Inferring object properties to parame-terize adaptive grasp policies, as explored in DeliGrasp [58], offers one possible direction for selecting task-appropriate target forces. Second, our current method estimates only the normal gripping force Fz and does not recover tangential forces or torques. Extending visual force estimation to the full contact wrench would require deformable gripper with rich multi-axis deformation cues. Third, action selection relies on policy-generated force predictions without online visual force feedback, limiting correction of prediction errors and unexpected contact disturbances. Future work could incorporate this feedback into action execution and evaluate robustness under external disturbances. VII. CONCLUSIONS In this work, we presented a force-aware manipulation approach that estimates normal gripping force directly from the visible deformation of a soft gripper. We use these visual force estimates to supervise a policy that jointly generates end-effector actions and their associated force values. At inference time, force search samples multiple action–force candidates and selects the action whose predicted force best matches a task-specific target. This enables force-aware action selection without dedicated force or tactile sensors, policy updates, or changes to the robot control interface at deployment. Across four force-sensitive manipulation tasks, force search consistently improves success over single-sample policy inference. These results show that visual gripper deformation can provide a useful contact signal not only for force estimation, but also for guiding policy inference. More broadly, our results suggest that a robot’s compliant structure can serve as a visually observable sensing medium. We hope this perspective encourages the development of low-cost manipulation systems that recover physical interaction signals directly from visual observations of robot deforma-tion.
