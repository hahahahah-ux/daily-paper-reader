Abstract — Dexterous manipulation policies trained in simu-lation often fail to transfer to the real world because of errors in contact timing and force regulation. Yet these policies can retain useful multi-finger coordination for task progression. We propose ReDex, a framework for adapting a simulation-trained base policy to the real world by correcting local contact failures and incorporating tactile feedback. Starting from a proprioception-only base policy, ReDex allows a hu-man operator to physically correct contact failures at selected fingers under compliant control during real-world rollouts, while the frozen base policy continues to control the remaining fingers. These rollouts combine base policy execution, human-corrected finger motion, and fingertip force observations. We reconstruct force-informed targets from these rollouts to train a standalone force-conditioned policy via behavior cloning. This design reduces human correction effort, enables learning of contact regulation from real-world interaction, and introduces force feedback into a proprioception-only policy without tactile simulation or complex full-hand teleoperation. We evaluate ReDex on two challenging, contact-rich dexterous manipulation tasks on real hardware. Compared with sim-to-real transferred base policies, ReDex increases Object Flipping success rate from 14% to 86% across two objects and average Screwdriver Rotation progress from 26.0% to 95.3% across three objects. 

I. Introduction For multi-finger hands, large-scale simulation training pro-vides an efficient way to learn the coordination required for dexterous manipulation [1]–[5]. However, sim-to-real transfer proves difficult for several reasons such as errors in contact placement, timing, or contact force regulation. Fingers may touch the wrong surfaces, release contact prematurely, or apply excessive or insufficient force [3], [6]. Addressing these errors through further simulation training is challenging because real-world contact dynamics are difficult to model accurately. Repeatedly retuning rewards or ranges of domain randomization and retraining policies in simulation can be costly without necessarily resolving the specific contact fail-ures observed in real-world experiments. Learning from human corrections on a real robot offers a direct way to address failures due to real-world physics. Existing correction approaches typically rely on teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching to modify the robot’s end-effector or arm-level motion [7], [8]. Extending human corrections to multi-finger hands remains difficult due to three challenges: 1) kinesthetic teaching for multi-finger hands is difficult to execute as finger motions must be simultaneously controlled to ensure stable contact during manipulation, 2) teleoperation (a) (b)

does not accurately reflect the optimal contact force profile for completing the target task and 3) collecting successful full-hand demonstrations may require substantial effort. Existing methods address some of these but not all. A skill-assisted interface reduces this burden by combining human wrist control with learned finger motions [9], but does not allow adjustment of individual finger contacts. Hand-motion retargeting enables finger pose corrections [10], [11] but does not ensure appropriate contact forces: a corrected finger pose may still produce insufficient force to prevent slipping or excessive force that impedes motion. Further-more, identifying and correcting these force errors remains difficult because human operators typically lack direct per-ception of the contact forces experienced by the robot [12], [13]. Therefore, a key gap remains in multi-finger dexterous manipulation on enabling human operators to easily correct finger motion and contact forces from simulation-trained policies to enable better task performance and real-world success rate. To this end, we introduce ReDex, a framework that adapts a simulation-trained dexterous policy to the real world through finger-level physical correction under compliance control, together with real-world fingertip force feedback (Fig. 1). Our key insight is that these sim-to-real failures arXiv:2610.07525v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 are usually local : the frozen base policy still produces the multi-finger coordination the task needs, and only a few finger contacts are misplaced or apply the wrong force. ReDex therefore preserves this coordination and lets a human physically repair only the failing fingers, while fingertip tactile sensing captures the contact force each correction applies. Confining correction to where the real world breaks the policy preserves the simulation-learned behavior and keeps the required human effort small. Specifically, ReDex proceeds in three stages. First, we train the proprioceptive base policy in simulation. Second, we roll out the frozen base policy on the real hand and let the operator physically correct only the fingers that deviate, selectively increasing their compliance so the operator can guide them while the base policy continues to control the remaining fingers in closed loop; each rollout records the full-hand motion and fingertip forces throughout execution, capturing both base-policy-controlled behavior and human physical corrections. Third, we reconstruct force-informed joint-position targets and distill the recorded human correc-tions and successful base-policy execution into a standalone force-conditioned policy via behavior cloning. We evaluate ReDex on two challenging dexterous ma-nipulation tasks, Object Flipping and Screwdriver Rotation, across five objects with varying sizes and geometries. Our real-world experiments show that ReDex substantially im-proves autonomous task performance with a small amount of finger-level correction per object. Ablation studies further highlight the value of tactile feedback both in constructing force-informed supervision and in enabling the learned pol-icy to respond to contact changes during execution. In summary, our contributions are threefold:  

> •

We introduce a human correction framework based on selective finger compliance, enabling direct physical correction of individual finger behavior while the base policy continues to control the remaining fingers, reduc-ing the operator’s coordination burden;  

> •

We develop efficient learning of finger contact reg-ulation via a relabeling pipeline that turns corrected motion, real-world tactile feedback, and base policy continuation into dense supervision;

A. Learning Dexterous Manipulation via RL 

Training dexterous policies directly on hardware is difficult because collecting demonstrations and running real-world reinforcement learning are costly [14]. Simulation instead enables large-scale parallel training, privileged state informa-tion, and domain randomization for dexterous manipulation, including in-hand reorientation [1], [2], [4] and tool use [5], [9]. However, sim-to-real transfer remains challenging due to differences in object properties, contact mechanics, and robot actuation, even with domain randomization and system identification [3], [6], [9]. Our method preserves simulation-learned multi-finger coordination while correcting selected fingers on real hardware. 

B. Learning from Human Correction 

Human corrections provide a direct way to improve robot policies from deployment failures. Interactive imitation learn-ing methods including DAgger [15] and its variants [16]– [18] collect corrective actions on learner-visited states, while human feedback has also been incorporated into real-world reinforcement learning [19]. More recently, human correc-tions have been used to adapt simulation-trained policies during real-world deployment. TRANSIC [7] learns a gated residual policy from teleoperated corrections, while CR-DAgger [8] uses compliant physical interaction to collect corrections and learn residual actions over a base policy. These methods primarily consider arm motion, whereas correction for dexterous hands requires controlling multiple fingers through changing contacts. DexScrew [9] reduces this burden by combining human wrist control with a learned full-hand manipulation primitive, preserving learned finger coordination but not allowing direct correction of individual finger contacts. DexHiL [10] and HandITL [11] instead retarget human hand motion for full-hand or hand–arm intervention, enabling direct finger control but requiring the operator to coordinate the hand during correction. ReDex instead intervenes only on the failed fingers while the base policy continues controlling the remaining fingers, preserving learned coordination during correction. 

C. Learning with Contact Feedback 

Learning contact-rich manipulation requires both motion coordination and contact regulation. Force feedback provides contact information that is difficult to infer from motion alone. One line of work incorporates real contact infor-mation through demonstrations or physical guidance [12], [20], [21]. In particular, DexForce [12] uses contact forces measured during kinesthetic demonstrations to construct force-informed actions for policy learning. Compliance has also been used to support physical guidance, allowing an operator to directly modify contact through physical inter-action [20], [22], [23]. While these approaches provide real contact information, collecting force-rich demonstrations or physical guidance for dexterous manipulation can still re-quire substantial human effort. Another line of work models tactile sensing in simulation to support contact-aware policy learning and sim-to-real transfer [24]–[26]. These approaches require sensor-specific modeling and calibration, while accu-rately simulating contact-rich interactions remains challeng-ing. More recent work introduces force or tactile feedback after learning an initial policy [8], [9], [27]. We follow this post-training setting and use finger-level compliance to correct only selected fingers while the base policy continues to control the remaining fingers. The measured forces are then used both to reconstruct force-informed supervision and as policy observations, enabling learning from real contact without full-hand force-rich demonstrations. 𝜋 !"

III. Method Trajectory targets from RL policies can often accomplish dexterous manipulation tasks in simulation, but sim-to-real deployment must account for real-world variations in surface friction, mass/inertia distribution, and hardware degradation over time. Our objective is to correct local finger behavior while retaining the base policy’s control of the remaining fin-gers, and to use these interactions to learn a force-conditioned real-world policy. As shown in Fig. 2, ReDex adapts a frozen simulation-trained policy (III-A) through physical correction via finger compliance (III-B), action target reconstruction (III-C), and policy learning (III-D).

B. Finger-Level Compliant Correction 

The key challenge is enabling physical correction without disrupting task execution, since execution and correction require different compliance profiles on the same hand. Reducing stiffness alone is insufficient: a compliant joint is still pulled toward the evolving base policy target, which may conflict with the operator’s correction. We combine finger-level compliance control with overrides of the selected joints’ position targets. Selected fingers use guidance targets and compliant control settings; unselected fingers track base policy targets under nominal settings. We realize finger-level compliance through the onboard joint position controller of the RobotEra XHAND1 [30], which allows independent per-joint position control gains and torque limits. We set the integral gain to zero and use proportional and derivative gains to regulate effective joint stiffness and damping, respectively. A. Experimental Setup 

Hardware and Sensing. All experiments use the RobotEra XHAND1 [30], a five-finger, 12-DoF hand with finger com-pliance control implemented as described in Sec. III-B. Each fingertip contains a magnetic tactile array with 120 taxels, each taxel providing a three-axis force measurement. During each rollout, we record taxel measurements and compute each fingertip’s three-axis net contact force. We transform the net forces into the hand-base frame for force-informed action target reconstruction (Sec. III-C). 

Tasks and metrics. We evaluate two skills on five real objects. Object Flipping tests brief contact transitions during in-hand reorientation using a cuboid (15 × 7 × 3 cm) and a cellphone (13.15 × 6.42 × 0.74 cm), with dimensions reported as length × width × height. Success requires a 90 ◦

reorientation ending in a stable grasp. Screwdriver Rotation tests finger gaiting and sustained contact using normal, small, and large screwdrivers, with handle diameters of 2.6, 1.8, and 3.5 cm, respectively. We measure final progress toward a 360 ◦ rotation in 90 ◦ increments, normalized as a percentage. This metric captures partial task completion; we report success rates only for Object Flipping. The main evaluation uses 25 autonomous trials per object–method pair. The overall flipping result pools 50 trials across both objects, while overall screwdriver progress averages the three object-specific means. An overall value is reported only when all objects in that task family are evaluated. Component and representation variants use 10 trials each; their full-method results are separate from the main 25-trial evaluation. 

Training setup and details. For each skill, we use one frozen simulation-trained policy across objects without additional simulation training and train a separate policy for each object using real-world correction data. We use 30 training episodes per flipping object and 10 per screwdriver variant, selected from successful human-corrected executions. We train the behavior cloning (BC) policy with separate two-layer MLP encoders for joint positions and force observations. Each encoder has hidden dimensions 128 and 64. We train for 100 epochs using the AdamW optimizer [33] with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4 and batch size 64. Inputs and targets are normalized. All methods command joint positions at 20 Hz. 

Baselines and comparison scope. We evaluate the baselines on cuboid flipping and normal-screwdriver rotation. RMA directly executes the frozen simulation-trained policy without training on correction data. Gated Correction [7] and Con-tinuous Correction [8] learn a residual action that modifies the base policy output. Gated Correction uses a learned binary gate to activate the residual and otherwise executes the base policy alone, whereas Continuous Correction applies a residual at every step. Both correction baselines use the same rollouts and reconstructed training targets as ReDex. Their inputs are the base policy joint targets and the same five-frame histories of joint positions and fingertip forces used by ReDex. Since the base policy produces single-step actions, both baselines predict a single-step residual at each control step. Training targets are expressed as residuals relative to base policy targets. Training and residual combination follow the original methods. These baselines provide reference comparisons with residual-correction approaches, rather than controlled ReDex ablations. V. Results

Adaptation across object variants. We next test whether these simulation-trained base policies can be reused through human correction on new objects of different sizes and geometries requiring the same manipulation skill. For each skill, we use one simulation-trained base policy for all objects and train object-specific policies from correction rollouts. Together, these results show consistent gains across all objects with limited correction data. We at-tribute this to the complementary roles of simulation training, domain randomization, and human correction. Tactile representations. In addition, we evaluate tactile rep-resentations with different encoder architectures. As shown in Table III, net-force observations outperform raw taxels with both MLP and GRU encoders. MLP and GRU perform similarly with net-force input, suggesting that this compact representation does not require a complex encoder. Although raw taxels retain richer spatial contact information, their lower performance may reflect greater sensitivity to sensor noise and contact variation. We therefore use net-force ob-servations with an MLP encoder in our experiments.

During the full kinesthetic teaching attempts on Screwdriver Rotation, coordinating re-peated contact establishment, release, and reestablishment across fingers was difficult: adjusting one finger could disturb other contacts, causing slipping or interrupting rotation. Even demonstrations that succeeded during teaching could replay inefficiently or unreliably, so we limit the quantitative comparison with full kinesthetic teaching to Cuboid Flipping. 

Failure analysis and limitations. In our experiments, 1) we observe that the base policy generally maintains useful coor-dination during local finger corrections. We hypothesize that this behavior reflects both the robustness acquired through simulation training with domain randomization and the restoration of task-relevant contacts through human guidance. However, this tolerance is still limited: excessive or poorly timed interventions can disrupt inter-finger coordination and move the hand outside the policy’s training state distribution. For example, in Screwdriver Rotation, moving the thumb too far from its intended contact point can disrupt the gait phases of other fingers, making subsequent base policy continuation unsuitable for supervision; 2) Distillation from limited real-world trajectories may not fully preserve the generalization ability of the simulation-trained policy, leaving the distilled policy more sensitive to conditions outside the collected data distribution; 3) Magnetic sensor drift remains a limitation, potentially degrading force-derived training labels and policy robustness. Initial taring removes zero offset, but subsequent drift cannot be distinguished from contact force changes without additional references or constraints. 4) Our evaluation is limited to two in-hand skills and a single hardware platform. Extending it to more complex tasks requires further validation of how joint coupling, feedback mechanisms, and intervention magnitude affect coordination and stability. VI. Conclusion ReDex adapts simulation-trained policies through finger-level physical correction and real-world tactile feedback. During rollout, the base policy controls uncorrected fingers while the operator repairs local contact failures. Force-informed target reconstruction and transition blending con-vert corrected rollouts into supervision for a standalone policy. Across two skills and five objects, ReDex improves real-world performance with limited correction data, without tactile simulation or additional simulation training. Future work could test other tactile sensors, extend ReDex to whole-body manipulation, and investigate preserving simulation-learned adaptability during real-world correction training.
