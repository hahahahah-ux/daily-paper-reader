Abstract: An always-on robot faces an endless stream that never resets: instruc-tions arrive and lapse, the scene changes, and its own past actions reshape what it must reason about. Today’s action models are built for the opposite: a fixed instruction, no mid-task intervention, single-step reasoning. In an open-ended world a robot must watch a live stream for far-future cues, recall its own far-past actions, and act on them under dual-arm concurrency. We present ARMS 

(Always-on Robot in Multi-modal Streams), a deliberately simple streaming pol-icy: a single pretrained π0.5 backbone augmented by three lightweight modules that turn live perception, embodied states, and the robot’s own past actions into context the backbone reads before it acts. The modules update this context asyn-chronously , so watching and recalling never block acting and the two arms act at once. Rather than inventing new mechanisms, ARMS integrates these learned context providers with an agent-causal self-history that logs which arm did what, and when. To supervise them without extra annotation, we build ARMS Dataset ,whose staged construction script itself labels every module from real dual-arm teleoperation. Trained on it, ARMS reaches 45% on the combined task against 28% for the strongest of our four main baselines, and ablations confirm the mem-ory module, the embodied-state head, and asynchronous concurrency are each necessary. 

Keywords: Streaming Model, Vision-Language-Action Model, Dual-Arm Robot 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent multimodal models are moving from offline frameworks toward streaming understanding over an endless live stream [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Such a model is at once proactive , monitoring the stream and acting on a standing condition only when it is met, and retrospective , recalling what happened far earlier in the stream. Either way, the information that decides an action is gathered far apart in time from the moment to act. For a robot, streaming is not optional. Unlike a video model that can watch a clip offline and then answer, an embodied agent lives in a single, never-reset timeline: its camera never closes, instructions arrive and lapse over hours, and it must perceive and act at once. Proactive watching and retrospective recall are thus prerequisites for long-horizon deployment, not add-ons. Action matters just as much to streaming in return: its proper output is a physical act, not an answer, and because a robot’s own actions reshape the very scene it must interpret, what it did becomes part of what it must understand, something no passively observed stream demands.

On the timeline of an always-on stream, different tasks may overlap: a foreground task, a proactive task, and a retrospective query. Handling them requires the model to watch, recall, and act at the right moment with the right embodiment. Three demands define this always-on regime (Figure 1), which we abbreviate watch, recall, act : the robot must watch a never-ending first-person stream for far-future triggers (“stow anything placed in front of you”), recall its own far-past actions to resolve self-referential commands (“the second one you stowed”), and act on a real dual-arm robot rather than answer a question about the video. Vision-language-action models already supply these ingredients in isolation: streaming memory [6, 7], mid-task switching [8], and always-on operation [9]. None sustains their combination on a dual-arm robot, where three things must hold at once. A standing rule can fire long after it is set, so the robot must keep watching. A self-referential command can be answered only from a record of what the robot itself did, not from the current frame. And because two arms share and continually disturb one scene, acting safely means knowing when they are about to collide. The combination, not any one capability, is the hard part; and it is genuinely an action problem, since replacing the arms with a multiple-choice answer makes the live scene, the foreground to sustain, and the self-perturbed grasp all disappear. We make three contributions. (1) We present ARMS (Always-on Robot in Multi-modal Streams), a deliberately simple policy: a single pretrained π0.5 backbone augmented by three lightweight mod-ules that turn live perception, embodied state, and the robot’s past actions into context the backbone reads before it acts (§3); the contribution is this integration and the agent-causal self-history, not a new network or planner. (2) We build ARMS Dataset , a benchmark for this combination, from real dual-arm data whose staged construction itself supplies the supervision each module needs (§3.7). 

(3) We deploy ARMS on a real dual-arm robot and, through a Probe/Online comparison, localize the difficulty in execution rather than perception (§4). On ARMS Dataset and a real dual-arm robot, ARMS reaches ∼45% versus ∼28% for the best baseline in Table 2, and ablations confirm that the memory module, the embodied-state head, and asynchronous concurrency are each necessary.

Streaming, proactive, and interruptible control. On the perception side, proactive video LLMs decide when to respond on a live stream [18, 19, 20, 21]: ProactiveVideoQA contributes a timeliness metric [2], OmniPro the Probe/Online protocol we adopt [3], Em-Garde a propose–match scheme [5], and RIVER mirrors our retrospective/live/proactive framing [4]. Yet all answer : the output is recognition, never a physical act, which is where ARMS differs. We inherit Dispider’s non-blocking asynchronous skeleton [1] and replace its blocking reaction track with embodied dual-arm action; lacking a foreground task, physical action, or self-history, Dispider never faces ARMS’s conflicts. On the control side, SwitchVLA switches tasks mid-execution [8], VITA-E interleaves seeing, hearing, speaking, and acting via a dual active/standby model [10], and Habilis-β targets reset-free operation [9]; recent dual-system and real-time policies push streaming action further [22, 23, 24, 25]. None runs the foreground arm in parallel with an inserted action, commits it to a queryable self-history, or arbitrates more demands than free hands: the dual-arm, concurrent, overload conflicts at ARMS’s center. 

Robot Foundation Models. ARMS builds on the growing family of general robot foundation mod-els [26, 27, 28, 29, 30] rather than competing with them: its action expert is fine-tuned from π0.5,whose pretraining and language-subtask conditioning we build on [31]. Complementary founda-tions ARMS does not require but could draw on include spatially grounded encoders for relative references [32, 33] and discrete skill-tokenization vocabularies [34, 35]. Our contribution is orthog-onal: the always-on, dual-arm, self-history layer that turns such a policy into an agent which acts on, records, and is later queried about its own behaviour. 

# 3 ARMS 

We present ARMS, a deliberately simple policy for the always-on, dual-arm setting. Rather than building a dedicated mechanism for each new demand, ARMS keeps a single pretrained backbone and turns everything else into context : three lightweight modules each convert one signal (live per-ception, embodied state, and past actions) into tokens the backbone reads before it acts. 

3.1 Problem setup

3.2 Overview: one backbone, three context providers 

The backbone is a pretrained π0.5 vision-language-action model [31] (a vision-language transformer with an action expert), reused with its original architecture, including its vision encoder. Around it, ARMS adds three light modules (Fig. 2): a visual perception path reusing π0.5’s vision head as a streaming, Dispider-style trigger gate (§3.3); an embodied-state head reporting, in one sentence, each arm’s current action primitive and whether the two arms are on a colliding path (§3.4); and a memory module compressing past actions and re-injecting the relevant ones for recall (§3.5). Each emits tokens (visual tokens or a short sentence) appended to π0.5’s context, over which the backbone autoregresses the dual-arm action. The modules update this context asynchronously while the heavier action forward reads it on demand, so perception and memory continue even while the arms execute, non-blocking, following Dispider [1]; this is what makes task-level concurrency 

possible. The perception gate runs at high frequency to keep watching the stream, while the heavier backbone reasons and acts at a lower frequency , woken only when a trigger fires. Each module is small and purpose-built, yet the three share one backbone and one interface, its context, with no separate planner, scheduler, or arbiter: watching, recalling, and collision-safe acting all reduce to what context is placed in front of π0.5. Because each module’s output is explicit, it can also be read 

without executing, giving the recognition probe of §4. 

3.3 Visual perception: a streaming trigger gate 

ARMS adds no new perception network: the live first-person stream is encoded by π0.5’s own vision head. Following Dispider [1], we decouple this visual path from the heavy action forward and run it continuously as a lightweight, non-blocking trigger gate . Against each active guard, the gate makes a binary decision on the current observation: whether the guard’s target object is present and its condition holds (fire) or not (keep watching). While the answer is no , the gate keeps scanning at full rate without waking the action expert, so an always-on stream stays cheap; the moment it turns 

yes , the encoded visual tokens enter π0.5’s context and the action expert is invoked to act. 3.4 Embodied-state head: action primitive and collision in one sentence 

A small embodied-state head reads the current observation and proprioception and appends one short sentence to the context, carrying two things: each arm’s current action primitive (grasping, releasing, reaching, idle), giving the backbone an explicit handle on what it is doing (exactly what a recall query or a newly fired guard must reason against), and a collision check , whether the two arms are on a colliding path given their current and intended motions. Phrasing both as one sentence (e.g. “left arm is stowing a cup; right arm reaching the plate is clear”) lets the backbone, rather than a separate geometric planner, decide how to proceed: dispatch a concurrent action to a free arm when the path is clear, or serialize when it is not. Collision avoidance and arm coordination thus reduce to a sentence the backbone reads, not a module that overrides it. Physical execution additionally uses joint velocity limits and an operator-controlled emergency stop. 

3.5 Self-action memory by compression 

To answer recall, ARMS keeps a memory H of its own past actions and re-injects the relevant part into the context. Following MemoryVLA [6], H is compressed rather than a growing log: each completed action is summarized into a compact entry (what was done, with which arm, to which object, and when), and entries are consolidated so memory and retrieval stay bounded on an unbounded stream. When a recall instruction arrives, the module retrieves the matching past actions and writes them back as a short sentence (“earlier the right arm stowed the carrot second, the banana first”) so the backbone resolves “the second one you stowed” from what it actually did. This is also what tells two same-class objects apart: two identical bananas are distinguished by when and how each was handled, recorded in H, not by appearance. At recall time, objects are matched to memory entries using stored object IDs under a last-known-identity assumption. 3.6 Training 

ARMS is not trained from scratch. The π0.5 backbone, including its vision head, is initialized from released pretrained weights and fine-tuned for the dual-arm setting, inheriting its pretraining. The three added modules are light and trained on ARMS Dataset (§3.7): the embodied-state head from dataset-derived action-primitive and collision labels, the memory module from the recorded action history and instruction–target pairs of the same construction. Because this supervision comes from the data rather than from rollouts of the backbone, the modules train without a circular dependency on the policy they augment. ARMS is a single model and runs inference on a single 80 GB acceler-ator. 

3.7 ARMS Dataset 

To train and evaluate ARMS under this combination, we build ARMS Dataset , a benchmark that requires always-on streaming, dual-arm concurrency, agent-causal self-history, end-to-end action, and overload arbitration together . Its core is data we collect ourselves on a Cobot Magic dual-arm platform [36] (a low-cost Mobile-ALOHA-style rig [37], teleoperated): about ∼200 never-reset staged streams (minutes each: a person places objects on cue while both arms work and a third demand forces deferral) and ∼1,200 short skill demonstrations over ∼6 dual-arm skills and 

∼15 object classes, with ≥2 same-class distractor pairs (e.g. two bananas). Controlling our own collection is what unlocks the regime (triggering, concurrency, and overload are enacted on the real robot, not approximated in simulation), and every scored action is a real teleoperated demonstration, never synthetic; we optionally add LIBERO [38] only to scale up. Because we construct the streams, the build script is itself an oracle: each module is supervised by a label the script already wrote when it staged the situation. We will publicly release ARMS Dataset and the construction code. The primary Online protocol scores end-to-end action over four families: a guard firing concur-rently with a foreground task (A), recalling a past self-action by language (B), relocating a self-displaced target (C), and arbitrating when both arms are busy and a new guard fires (D), all under six metrics (cross-interruption recall, foreground-continuation, self-history consistency, dual-arm non-interference, overload-ordering, and a PAUC-style timeliness). A complementary Probe protocol, which we adopt from OmniPro [3] (not a contribution of ours), scores recognition alone (whether the agent can tell when to act on what without moving), so contrasting the two exposes a perception– 6action dissociation . Evaluation is on the real Cobot Magic robot ( ∼150 trials per method) with LIBERO as an optional scale-up. 

# 4 Experiments 

Our experiments answer three questions. (Q1) Does the combination ARMS targets open a measur-able gap that current policies fall into, and is that gap one of action rather than perception (§4.2)? 

(Q2) Is each added module necessary (§4.3)? (Q3) Does ARMS hold up on a real dual-arm robot (§4.4)? 

4.1 Setup Baselines. Our main comparison includes four policies, each strong on one ingredient but missing the combination: a Fixed-rate VLA runs the π0.5 backbone in a sliding window with no streaming context (“always acting”); MemoryVLA [6] adds memory but neither sustains a foreground task through an insertion nor records which arm acted; SwitchVLA [8] switches tasks mid-execution but keeps no queryable self-history; and Recognition →Controller drives a naive executor from the strongest Probe front-end, with no memory or embodied-state module, the “answers but cannot do” control. All four baselines use the same pretrained π0.5 initialization, fine-tuning data, and backbone training budget as ARMS; ARMS additionally trains its context modules. We evaluate the default 

ARMS (∼5B) and, in §4.3, its ablations. 

Protocol and metrics. Evaluation is closed-loop : we recompose recorded scenes into always-on streams and roll the policy out on the real robot (the live N = 60 runs of §4.4 skip recomposi-tion), so every physical metric reflects executed actions, not open-loop matching. We use a 90/10 train/validation split over complete staged streams, with held-out paraphrases for recall evaluation. Beyond the six per-family metrics (§3.7) we report combined-task action success (EAS): an episode succeeds only if, in one uninterrupted run, the foreground completes, every fired guard is served on a free arm without collision, recall returns the correct past object, and overload ordering matches the priority labels. Overall EAS is weighted by evaluation counts across task families, whereas di-agnostic metrics are computed on their respective applicable subsets. EAS is the headline; the six metrics localize where a policy breaks. All percentages.

# 5 Conclusion and Limitations 

We cast always-on dual-arm manipulation as acting at moments decoupled in time from the deciding information, a regime that is hard only when streaming memory, mid-task concurrency, and always-on operation must hold at once. ARMS meets it with a deliberately simple design: a single π0.5

backbone integrating three lightweight context modules (visual perception, an embodied-state head, and a compressed agent-causal memory) through one shared context interface; the contribution is this integration, the agent-causal self-history, and a dataset whose construction supplies each mod-ule’s supervision. On ARMS Dataset and a real dual-arm robot, ARMS beats policies built for any single ingredient, every module is necessary, and a Probe/Online dissociation places the residual difficulty in execution, not perception. 

Limitations. Our real-robot evidence is small-scale and on a single Cobot Magic platform, with only 

∼200 staged always-on streams, so large-scale evidence (especially for the full overload regime, exercised mainly in family D) remains limited. Standing guards are predefined, and the default overload policy uses fixed, dataset-defined priority tiers.
