ABSTRACT
An enduring and richly elaborated dichotomy in cognitive neuroscience is that of
human behavior control mechanisms, divided into habitual versus goal-directed.
While existing human-like agent frameworks primarily focus on modeling goal-
directed behavior, habitual behavior has been largely overlooked, though it plays
a crucial role in human daily life. In this paper, we address this gap by studying
multiple behavior control systems that jointly model goal-directed and habitual
behaviors. We propose a human behavior control mechanism-inspired framework
which the Habitual Controller retrieves cue-triggered behaviors from personal-
ized habit memory, while the Goal-directed Controller employs a context-aware
world model to predict action consequences and estimate their values. The Arbiter
dynamically balances the influence of both systems according to individual differ-
ences and momentary internal states. To reconstruct diverse human-level behavior
instructions in 3D environments, we further develop a keyframe-guided 3D mo-
tion generation module. Through extensive evaluation methods, human studies,
and ablations studies, experimental results demonstrate that human-likeness per-
formance is significantly improved by our approach. The efficacy of our approach
indicates the benefits of leveraging habitual behavior and multiple behavior con-
trol system coordination for believable embodied human-like agents.

Building human-like agents (Sloman, 1999; 2000) that can replicate human behavior in the realistic
3D world has been a long-standing pursuit since the inception of AI (Fetzer, 1990). The study

cooperation, populate virtual reality communities (Zhou et al., 2026), and accelerate Embodied AI
(Feng et al., 2026). With recent advances in large language models (LLMs), building embodied

increasing attention. Existing frameworks (Park et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Leng et al., 2026;

actions are likely to bring about, i.e., action–outcome (A–O) representations, such as satisfying
desires (Wang et al., 2025) or completing plans (Park et al., 2023).
However, habitual behaviors, which are driven by stimulus–response (S–R) associations, account for
a substantial proportion of everyday human behavior (Rebar et al., 2025; Wood & R¨unger, 2016).
These behaviors rely on direct links between environmental cues and actions, contributing to the
diverse and natural patterns observed in human daily activity. Insights from cognitive psychology
and neuroscience (Miller et al., 2019; Dolan & Dayan, 2013; Lee et al., 2014) suggest a multiple
system view of behavioral control, in which separable goal-directed and habitual systems jointly
shape the behavior. Motivated by these observations, a pertinent question arises: How can we
integrate multiple behavioral control systems to simulate human-like behavior?
Addressing this question poses three key challenges: (1) how to model habitual behavior; (2) how
multiple behavior control systems jointly shape the final behavior; and (3) how to generate diverse
habitual and goal-directed behaviors naturally and accurately in 3D environments. In this work, we
explore the use of multiple behavior control system in 3D human-like daily behavior simulation.
Specifically, we propose a Generative Embodied Multiple Behavior Control System framework
(GEMS), inspired by human behavioral control mechanisms that jointly models goal-directed and
habitual behaviors, enabling more diverse and naturalistic behavior generation.
GEMS framework consists of a cognitive module and a motion generation module. The cogni-
tive module uses the current state and goal as input, retrieves relevant habitual actions, evaluates
goal-directed action values, dynamically arbitrates their decision weights, and selects the final ac-
tion. The motion generation module then translates high-level human behavior descriptions into 3D
human motion sequences. In the cognitive module, we design a Habitual Controller to address
challenge (1), which models the cue-triggered nature of habits through a personalized habit memory
and a habit retrieval algorithm that first identifies contextually relevant habits and then selects the
most appropriate habitual action. To support this module, we manually curate a dataset of 571 ha-
bitual behaviors synthesized from prior empirical habit studies. On another hand, the Goal-directed
Controller employs a world model (Wu et al., 2023) as the agent’s internal model to predict the
consequences of candidate actions and estimate their values. Furthermore, To address challenge (2),
the Arbiter dynamically balances goal-directed action values and habit strengths, while accounting
for individual control biases and the agent’s momentary psychological state to determine the final
action, enabling more diverse behavioral patterns. Additionally, existing 3D human motion genera-
tion works (Wang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2026), have limited support for the diverse
human-level behavior instructions required by our framework. To address this, we adopt a scene-
agnostic Text-to-Motion (T2M) model Rempe et al. (2026) for expressive motion synthesis, and
further incorporate keyframe-guided conditioning to generate motions directly from scene-aware
spatial and interaction constraints, thereby tackling challenges (3).
We systematically compare our method against existing human-like agent frameworks (Leng et al.,
2026; Lin et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024) through comprehensive
quantitative evaluations and human studies, demonstrating its superiority in simulating more human-
like behaviors. With extensive ablation studies 4.4, we offer further insight into the efficacy of our
designs and showcase the benefits of multiple behavior control systems.
Our contributions are three-fold. (i) We study the value of multiple system behavioral control for
more believable human-like daily behavior simulation by jointly modeling goal-directed and habit-
ual behaviors. (ii) We introduce GEMS, a human-like agent framework to effectively simulating
huamn behavior in 3D environment, with a Habitual Controller, a Goal-directed Controller, an Ar-
biter, and a 3D motion generation module. (iii) Extensive evaluation methods, human studies, and
ablations demonstrate the effectiveness of GEMS and validate the benefits of introducing habitual
behavior and multiple system coordination.

RELATED WORKS
Human-like LLM agent.
Human daily activity modeling supports applications in psychology,
sociology, and virtual human simulation (G¨arling & Garvill, 1993; Adler et al., 1987; Schmidt et al.,
2024; Fraser et al., 2024). Recent advances in LLMs have enabled increasingly realistic simula-
tions of human behavior. Existing works mainly follow two directions. One line models cognitive
mechanisms behind behavior, including memory and planning (Park et al., 2023), desires (Wang
et al., 2023; 2025), individual differences (He & Zhang, 2024; Li et al., 2025), and social interac-
tions (Zhang et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026). Another line grounds LLM-generated behaviors in 3D
environments (Leng et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). Most methods
rely on predefined atomic actions, while ACTOR (Liang et al., 2025) uses motion clips but may
suffer from spatial misalignment and transition drift. Despite these advances, existing frameworks
are largely driven by explicit goals, plans, desires, or events, following a goal-directed paradigm.
Habitual behaviors, however, are outcome-insensitive and triggered by environmental cues, form-
ing personalized stimulus–response patterns that remain underexplored in current human simulation
frameworks.
Human behavior control mechanism study.
An enduring and richly elaborated dichotomy in
cognitive neuroscience is that of human behavior control mechanism, divided into habitual versus
goal-directed (Dolan & Dayan, 2013; Miller et al., 2019; Wood et al., 2022). Existing computational
frameworks typically associate goal-directed control with model-based planning and habitual control
with model-free learning (Daw et al., 2005; Dolan & Dayan, 2013; Wood & R¨unger, 2016). Compu-
tational accounts further introduce arbitration mechanisms to dynamically allocate control between
the two systems (Daw et al., 2005; Lee et al., 2014). Related cognitive architectures, including
ACT-R, Soar, and CoALA, also use proceduralized or cue-triggered responses for efficient action
selection (Taatgen et al., 2008; Laird et al., 1987; Sumers et al., 2024). More recent work argues that
habits are not equivalent to model-free RL and can instead be characterized by repetition-dependent
habit strength (Miller et al., 2019). However, most prior studies primarily focus on investigating
and understanding the mechanisms underlying human behavior, while contextual habit triggering
and state-dependent shifts in behavioral control remain relatively underexplored in realistic envi-
ronments (Wood & R¨unger, 2016; Neal et al., 2013). In contrast, our motivation is to translate
human-inspired design principles into realistic 3D environments and explore their application to
human-like daily behavior simulation.

3.2.3
ARBITER
The Arbiter coordinates the relative influence of the goal-directed and habitual controllers at each
decision step and selects the final action. Existing work in psychology and neuroscience suggests
that this influence is mainly shaped by three sources: controller competition (Miller et al., 2019),
individual control bias (Piray et al., 2016), and psychological state (Wood et al., 2022).
Controller competition: For a candidate action ai,t, stronger habit strength H(ai,t) increases habit-
ual support when the action is habitually triggered while a higher estimated value Q(ai,t) increases
goal-directed support (Wood et al., 2022). For actions proposed only by the Goal-directed Con-
troller, H(ai,t) is set to 0. Individual control bias: We further introduce ρ ∈[0, 1] as the agent’s
baseline preference for goal-directed control, where larger values indicate the agent has greater re-
liance. Current psychological state may further suppress goal-directed control and increase reliance
on habits. We design a psychological state interference function F(spsy
t
) ∈[0, 1] to characterize the
suppressive effects of spsy
t
on goal-directed control. For each psychological factor in spsy
t
, quantified
on a 0–10 scale, a higher score (e.g., a stress score of 7 compared with 3) increases the probabil-
ity that its interference becomes behaviorally active, thereby increasing the expected suppression
of goal-directed control. In this work we consider three factors: stress (str.) (Schwabe & Wolf,
2011), depletion (dep.) (Neal et al., 2013) and cognitive load (cog.) (Otto et al., 2013). Because
the underlying psychological mechanisms are complex, we empirically calibrate F using existing
human behavioral data rather than modeling these mechanisms explicitly. More details are provided
in Appendix E.
Integrating these sources of influence, we propose the joint weight of goal-directed control at time t
as wG

t

across individuals with different characteristics, under different scenarios and psychological states.
3.3
MOTION GENERATION MODULE
The generation module reconstructs human-object interactions in 3D environments from the human-
level action at generated by the cognitive module. It employs a High-Level Planner and a keyframe-
guided Low-Level Motion Generator, as shown in Fig. 3.
High-Level Planner. The planner takes the high-level language action instructions and scene state,
including the scene layout, objects, and their current states, as input and determines the desired
scene state after action completion. The planner then specifies an ordered sequence of sub-actions
and object interactions to ensure plausible execution. For instance, if the agent wants to sit on a chair,
the chair should be pulled out before sitting down. An LLM produces this ordered decomposition,
yielding a sequence of textual sub-action descriptions and matched keyframe constraints used to
guide Low-Level Motion Generator.
Low-Level Motion Generator. Given the resulting sub-action descriptions, the generator exe-
cutes them sequentially by synthesizing human motion segments and coordinating object interac-
tions, yielding diverse human-object interaction sequences. In particular, the scene-agnostic T2M
model (Rempe et al., 2026) we adopt offers expressive motion synthesis but does not directly per-
ceive scene geometry. To address this limitation, keyframes bridge high-level planning and low-level
motion generation by encoding planned sub-actions and scene information as 3D constraints for mo-
tion synthesis (Xie et al., 2024). Given a sub-action instruction and the perceived scene point cloud,
a VLM predicts target human skeletal poses and corresponding object poses for a sparse set of
keyframes. This mechnasim guidance supports flexible sub-action composition and smooth motion
transitions. The T2M model then synthesizes each motion segment from the sub-action description,
incorporating keyframe constraints and a planned navigation path when applicable. When the gener-
ated motion reaches a designated keyframe, the system triggers the corresponding interaction event,

EXPERIMENT
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND EVALUATION METRICS
To evaluate the human-likeness performance of GEMS, we separately evaluate its two main mod-
ules: (i) the cognitive module, by assessing the human-likeness of the behavior sequences it pro-
duces; and (ii) the motion generation module, by assessing whether the selected behaviors can be
faithfully and naturally realized in 3D environments. For the cognitive module, we follow prior
LLM-based evaluation protocols (Wang et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2025) and report three metrics, each
scored on a 0–100 scale: Naturalness (Nat.) for human-likeness (Wang et al., 2025), Coherence
(Coh.) for logical consistency (Wang et al., 2025), and Personality Alignment (PA) for consistency
with the given persona (Li et al., 2025). Mean denotes their average. For the motion generation mod-
ule, we evaluate Step Success Rate (SSR), which measures individual-step success, Goal Success
Rate (GSR), which measures whole-plan success, and Distance to Goal (DTG), which measures
the minimum distance from wrist to goal object (Liang et al., 2025; Diomataris et al., 2024). We
also use Quality Score (Qual.) for motion naturalness and Action Score (Act.) for consistency
with the target behavior description (Wang et al., 2022). The evaluation criteria and the definitions
of successful task execution generation follow those in the corresponding prior works.
We compare GEMS with five previous human-like agent frameworks. X-VirtualHome, AGA, and
ACTOR support embodied execution and can therefore be evaluated on both the cognitive and mo-
tion generation module metrics, whereas D2A and ASVO focus only on language behavior genera-
tion and are therefore evaluated only on the cognitive module metrics. We further evaluate a variant
of ACTOR, denoted as ACTOR†, which retains the original ACTOR pipeline while using the same
Habit Memory, T2M model, and simulation platform as GEMS; habitual behaviors are sampled
from the shared Habit Memory using an LLM. For a fair comparison, we evaluate all methods on 30
shared daily life goals (such as preparing dinner and cleaning the kitchen) with identical initializa-
tion settings. Each goal runs five times, yielding 150 runs per method, and all scores are averaged
across runs. Full implementation details are provided in Appendix A.2.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we study multiple system behavioral control for human-like daily behavior simula-
tion in 3D environments. We introduce GEMS, a human-inspired generative framework that jointly
models goal-directed and habitual behaviors through a Habitual Controller, a Goal-directed Con-
troller, and an Arbiter, together with a keyframe-guided motion generation framework for realizing

high-level behaviors as 3D human motions and interactions. Extensive automatic evaluations and
human studies demonstrate that GEMS generates more human-like behaviors while maintaining
high-quality 3D motions. Further ablation studies validate the benefits of habitual control, multiple
system coordination, and keyframe-guided motion generation. We hope this work provides a step
toward more natural and diverse human-like agents in interactive 3D environments.

Implementation setup. We use GPT-5.5 as the default LLM, GPT-5.6 as the automatic evaluator,
and Deepseek V4 pro as the VLM. The default controller-bias coefficient is set to ρ = 0.5, and the
initial internal state is (3, 3, 3), corresponding to stress, depletion, and cognitive load, respectively.
For the planning procedure P, we use a rollout horizon of 30 steps.
Controlled-goal evaluation. We first evaluate all methods under a controlled-goal setting. All
methods use the same persona, initial state, current time, and recent behavior history. We construct
a shared daily-activity pool based on the semantic overlap among activities that can be generated or

Under Review
executed by different methods, such as preparing breakfast, cleaning the kitchen, and working from
home. We randomly sample 30 goals from this pool as the common test set.
For methods that support explicit task specification, including ACTOR, AGA, and X-VirtualHome,
we directly provide the corresponding high-level goal. For desire-driven methods, including D2A
and ASVO, we set the desire state associated with the target goal such that a semantically aligned
behavior is triggered through the method’s original mechanism. Once the target goal is provided or
triggered, we record the resulting behavior sequence until goal termination and evaluate only this

Each method is independently run five times for each of the 30 goals, resulting in 150 behavior
sequences per method. We allow each method to generate its own behavior plan; therefore, the
resulting plans and sequence lengths may differ across methods.
Long-horizon evaluation. We additionally evaluate the methods under long-horizon daily simu-
lation in Tab. 3. In this setting, we control only the persona and initial state across methods. For
ACTOR and GEMS, which do not include a complete daily-schedule generation module, we con-
struct a shared pool of high-level daily plans generated by the other methods and randomly sample
a complete daily plan as input. ACTOR and GEMS then sequentially execute the activities specified
in the sampled plan. Each simulation starts from the initial state of the day and continues until all
scheduled daily activities are completed.
Metric details.
For the cognitive module, we follow prior LLM-based evaluation protocols and report GPT-based
scores on three dimensions. Naturalness (Nat.) measures the degree to which the generated be-
havior resembles human behavior (Wang et al., 2025). Coherence (Coh.) measures the logical and
temporal consistency of the behavior sequence (Wang et al., 2025). Personality Alignment (PA)
measures consistency with the given persona (Li et al., 2025). Mean denotes the average of the three
scores.
The sequence quality ranking from human subjective evaluation in Fig. 6 is consistent with the above
ranking, further validating the effectiveness of our evaluation protocols.
For motion generation module, we evaluate both task execution and motion quality. Step Success
Rate (SSR) measures individual-step success, while Goal Success Rate (GSR) measures whether
the complete behavior plan is successfully executed (Liang et al., 2025). Distance to Goal (DTG)
measures the minimum wrist-to-goal distance during execution (Diomataris et al., 2024). For motion
quality, Quality Score (Qual.) measures motion naturalness, while Action Score (Act.) measures
consistency with the corresponding behavior description (Wang et al., 2022).

ADDITIONAL DISCUSSIONS
C.1
HUMAN-LIKE LLM AGENT
Human daily activity modeling supports a wide range of applications, from understanding every-
day decision-making in psychology (G¨arling & Garvill, 1993) and social interaction and structures
in sociology (Adler et al., 1987), to creating realistic virtual humans for metaverse and VR appli-
cations (Schmidt et al., 2024; Fraser et al., 2024). However, collecting real-world behavioral data
is costly and raises privacy concerns. Recent advances in LLMs have therefore spurred growing
interest in human behavior simulation. The pioneering Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) in-
troduced memory, reflection, and planning to simulate believable human behaviors. Building on
this, Humanoid Agents (Wang et al., 2023) incorporated desire-driven processes, while AFSPP (He
& Zhang, 2024) introduced agent preferences and personalities to capture individual differences.
More recent works model action selection from different psychological perspectives: CVA (Zhang
et al., 2026) uses a human-data-trained Value Verifier, D2A (Wang et al., 2025) adopts desire-driven
behavior based on the Theory of Needs, and ASVO (Lin et al., 2026) incorporates Social Value
Orientation for multi-agent interactions. For 3D realization, X-VirtualHome (Leng et al., 2026)
generates persona-conditioned routines with LLM-based planning, while AGA (Yu et al., 2024)
grounds high-level instructions into VirtualHome’s predefined action space. X’s Day (Li et al.,
2025) models personality-driven long-term behaviors, Visually-grounded Humanoid Agents (Ye
et al., 2026) grounds LLM-driven behaviors through visual perception and spatial planning, and
Ella (Zhang et al., 2025) introduces long-term multimodal memory for lifelong embodied behavior.
Most of these frameworks realize high-level behaviors through predefined atomic actions. In con-
trast, ACTOR (Liang et al., 2025) matches planned behaviors with pre-collected motion clips and
uses Transformer-based motion completion to connect them, which can introduce spatial misalign-
ment and skeleton drift during transitions. Note that existing methods are predominantly driven by
explicit plans, desires, or events, resulting in a goal-directed behavior generation paradigm. Habitual
behaviors, however, are often cue-triggered, non-optimal, and highly individualized, accounting for
a major portion of everyday human behavior. Modeling habits can therefore enable more natural
and personalized agents and benefit human behavior understanding and recognition.
C.2
HUMAN BEHAVIOR CONTROL STUDY
Goal-directed and habitual control have long been viewed as two ends of a spectrum of human be-
havioral control (Dolan & Dayan, 2013). The same action may arise from goal-directed control,
habitual control, or their joint influence (Miller et al., 2019; Wood et al., 2022), making the un-
derlying source of control difficult to infer from overt behavior alone. Moreover, habits are often
executed automatically with limited deliberation, while their underlying triggers may escape con-
scious awareness, leading their influence on behavior to be underestimated (Wood & R¨unger, 2016).
Experimental studies commonly distinguish the two forms of control using outcome devaluation,
which tests sensitivity to current outcome value (Dolan & Dayan, 2013). In cognitive neuroscience,
18


Under Review
human behavioral control is often modeled through reinforcement learning: goal-directed control is
associated with model-based planning, whereas habitual control is associated with model-free learn-
ing (Daw et al., 2005; Dolan & Dayan, 2013; Wood & R¨unger, 2016). Early dual-system models
emphasized competition between the two controllers, while later work introduced arbitration mech-
anisms that dynamically balance their influence based on relative reliability (Daw et al., 2005; Lee
et al., 2014). Related cognitive architectures exploit proceduralized responses for efficient action
selection: ACT-R enables perceptual cues to trigger productions after practice (Taatgen et al., 2008),
Soar represents procedural knowledge with production rules (Laird et al., 1987), and CoALA ex-
tends this principles to language agents (Sumers et al., 2024). More recent accounts question the
direct equivalence between habits and model-free RL, instead linking habitual behavior to habit
strength shaped through repetition (Miller et al., 2019). However, existing studies mainly focus on
habit formation and control allocation in constrained tasks, leaving contextual habit triggering and
state-dependent shifts in realistic environments underexplored (Wood & R¨unger, 2016; Neal et al.,
2013). In contrast, we study multiple systems behavioral control for human-like daily behavior
simulation in realistic 3D environments.
C.3
HUMAN HABITS
Habit memory is inherently dynamic. A habit may gradually weaken or even be forgotten due to
interference from external factors, while others may be further reinforced through long-term repe-
tition. However, this work focuses on fine-grained human behavior simulation over relatively short
time horizons, typically within a single day. Therefore, habit memory can be reasonably assumed to
remain stable over the simulated time period.

Context Cue Representation.
Potentially, any internal or external event may trigger habitual be-
havior (Gardner et al., 2016). Since the full space of possible cues cannot be exhaustively repre-
sented, we consider four cue dimensions that are both empirically supported and available in the
agent’s current context: visual episodes (Hommel, 1998), psychological states (Waszak & Pholu-
lamdeth, 2009), events (Qiu et al., 2023), and active goals (Schneider & Logan, 2009). Each cue
is represented in natural language and assigned to one of these dimensions. Although a habitual
response may depend on multiple contextual factors, we use one primary cue dimension for each
habit instance to obtain a simple and interpretable representation.

HABIT RETRIEVAL EVALUATION SET
To evaluate the cue retrieval mechanism of the Habitual Controller, we construct an agent-specific
retrieval evaluation set based on each personalized habit memory ˆDH. Each agent contains 30 habit
memories. Each memory consists of a context cue qcue
i
, a habitual action aH
i , and its habit strength
H(aH

For each habit, we construct four types of retrieval cases. Each case has one target habitual action,
which serves as its ground-truth retrieval label. Direct Match provides a context that directly matches
the cue of the target habit. It tests whether the retrieval algorithm can identify the correct habit under
a clear triggering context. Boundary Reversal changes a key contextual attribute, such as object
state, event timing, or subject identity. This creates a more confusing context and tests whether
the algorithm can use the key contextual information to identify the target habit. Partial Transfer
preserves part of the semantic or categorical similarity while changing the location, object, state,
intensity, or event source. It tests whether the algorithm can distinguish the target habit from other
similar habits under partial contextual changes. Hard Negative introduces strong distractors that are
semantically similar to other candidate cues or contain misleading contextual information. Examples
include objects shown in photographs, descriptions of psychological states, or objects with similar
but different states. These cases test whether the algorithm can retrieve the correct target habit under
highly confusing contexts.

Under Review
For each retrieval case, the corresponding target habitual action is used as the ground truth. The
retrieval is counted as correct only when the retrieved habitual action matches this ground-truth ac-
tion. Retrieving another habit or retrieving no habit is counted as incorrect. Therefore, the evaluation
directly measures whether the retrieval algorithm can identify the correct habitual behavior under
different types of contextual variation and interference.
This process produces 30×4 = 120 base retrieval cases for each agent. We further include challeng-
ing cases in which multiple candidate habits have high cue compatibility with the current context.
We also include cases in which cue compatibility and habit strength favor different candidate habits.
These cases test whether the retrieval algorithm is affected by strong distractors, high habit strength,
or superficial semantic similarity.
To evaluate robustness to linguistic variation, we generate four linguistic variants for each base case.
These variants modify the wording, temporal descriptions, and irrelevant background information
while keeping the same ground-truth habitual action. Each agent therefore contains 120 × 4 =
480 retrieval cases. We independently construct the evaluation sets for three agents, resulting in
1,440 retrieval cases in total. We randomly inspect 10% of the generated cases to verify the context
descriptions, case types, and ground-truth habitual actions.
D.4
RETRIEVAL ALGORITHM EVALUATION
Habit retrieval and selection are mainly influenced by two factors: the compatibility between the
current context and the stored context cue, and the strength of the habit itself. We use cue similarity
to characterize context–cue compatibility, which measures how well the current context matches
the stored cue of a habit. Habit strength characterizes the strength of the stimulus–response (S–R)
association. A stronger S–R association indicates a higher tendency for the corresponding habit-
ual response to occur and guide behavior. Based on these two factors, we compare three retrieval
strategies to examine their different roles in habit retrieval and selection.
We first introduce Similarity-only as a baseline. This method performs both retrieval and selection
solely according to cue similarity, allowing us to isolate the contribution of habit strength. We then
consider a direct combination of the two factors, denoted as Similarity × Habit Strength, which
multiplies cue similarity by habit strength before retrieval. In this formulation, cue compatibility
and habit strength jointly affect habit activation from the beginning. However, a high habit strength
may compensate for relatively weak cue compatibility, while a low habit strength may prevent a
well-matched cue from being retrieved.
Motivated by the observation that habitual responses are triggered by recurring context cues, while
stronger habits are more likely to guide behavior (Wood & R¨unger, 2016), we further introduce Two-
stage Cue Retrieval. The first stage uses only cue similarity to identify habits that are sufficiently
compatible with the current context and constructs the set of activated habits. The second stage
incorporates habit strength to weight and select among these activated habits. This design separates
context–cue matching from the effect of habit strength. Habit strength therefore does not interfere
with the initial cue matching, while stronger habits remain more likely to be selected after activation.
We next describe the three retrieval strategies in detail. Given the current context st and the per-
sonalized habit memory bDH, the retrieval algorithm compares the current context with the stored
context cues and returns a corresponding habitual action aH
i,t.
We consider four types of context cues for habit triggering: visual episodes, psychological states,
events, and active goals. Each stored context cue qcue

WORLD MODEL
Inspired by (Zhao et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026), we introduce a context-based world model to ap-
proximate state transitions for the human-like agent. Specifically, we use a frozen LLM to predict
possible next states and action consequences based on the current world state and the agent’s action.
As shown in Fig. 8, we represent the world as a graph. Entities in the environment, including the
agent and objects, are represented as nodes, while their states are represented as node attributes.
Relations and interactions between entities are represented as edges. In this way, each node–edge–
node tuple represents a factual relation in the current world state.
At each state transition, the LLM predicts changes in node attributes and relations between enti-
ties. These changes represent how the world may evolve after an action is executed. The predicted
updates are then organized into a structured JSON format and applied to the current world represen-
tation to obtain the predicted next state.
The context-based world model has several limitations. First, its predictions depend on the domain
knowledge of the underlying LLM, and prediction errors may accumulate over long trajectories.
Second, the frozen LLM does not dynamically update its world knowledge during interaction. How-
ever, our setting mainly focuses on daily-life activities, for which LLMs generally provide sufficient
prior knowledge to predict common action consequences. We therefore adopt a simple frozen LLM
as the agent’s internal model. This design also reflects a property of human internal models, whose
predictions are similarly constrained by prior knowledge and experience.

TEXT TO MOTION BASED 3D EMBODIED PLATFORM
3D Platform Overview
The 3D Platform translates the natural langrage action description into
human motion within an interactive scene. As Fig. 3 shows, the platform comprises high-level
planner, keyframe constraints, motion generation, and 3D execution. Given a intent description, and
the current character avatar and scene state, the high-level planner organizes the intended behavior
into an ordered sequence of executable actions.
Based on the factor such as action complexity, interaction detail and spatial location, etc., the planned
actions are categorized into two type: actions require spatial guidance and standard actions that do
not require guidance. For those actions requiring spatial guidance, the high-level planner provides
sparse keyframe constraints that specify relevant body configurations and interaction relationships.
27


Under Review
These constraints guide motion generation alongside the action descriptions. Actions that do not
require spatial guidance are generated directly from motion generator.
The generated motions along with associated object interactions are executed in 3D simulation plat-
form. Character avatar and scene states are carried forward across successive actions to support
multi-step behavior. The following subsections describe the execution setup and examine the con-
tribution of keyframe constraints through qualitative ablations.
Scene Representation and State Management
The 3D environment is represented by a struc-
tured scene description containing object identities, spatial information, and interaction states. Ob-
ject identities provide consistent references across task planning and execution, while spatial infor-
mation describes the locations and geometric properties of scene objects. Together with the character
avatar’s current position and pose, this representation provides the context for grounding behavioral
descriptions in the environment.
The scene information is refreshed at each task initialization. The information is sent to the cog-
nitive module and high-level planner for the planning and action generation process. The platform
maintains both character avatar and object states throughout execution. character c state includes
the current body configuration and ongoing interactions, such as facing direction or holding an ob-
ject. Object state includes changes resulting from these interactions, such as an updated position or
an opened door. Subsequent actions receive the preceding action’s terminal state, allowing them to
continue from the established configuration.
During continuous execution, the framework carries forward the accumulated interaction state and
incorporates updates from the scene. The 3D simulation platform provides scene data and executes
the corresponding motions and object interactions, while the planner and cognitive module use this
information to prepare subsequent actions.
Semantic Task Decomposition and Execution Planning
Given a intent description and character
avatar and environment states, the high-level planner decomposes the coarse-grained intended be-
havior into an ordered sequence of executable actions. Each executable action needs to be clarified
how to perform, relevant interaction objects and their expected outcomes.
The planner considers spatial relationships and the character avatar’s current states when planning
the action sequence. Movement actions are included when needed to reach an interaction location,
while actions that can be performed from the current position retain that position where appropriate.
Affordance is also considered when determining whether preparatory actions are needed. For exam-
ple, pulling out the chair will be planned when there is no enough sitting space under the table. Such
adjustments remain part of semantic planning, ensuring that the resulting sequence forms a coherent
and spatially feasible course of action.
Execution planning translates the resulting action sequence into inputs for the Low-Level Motion
Generator. It aligns action descriptions with applicable keyframe constraints, assigns their timing,
and schedules interaction events. Sequences exceeding the motion generator’s per-call duration are
divided into consecutive generation batches while preserving the planned action order. Boundary
states are carried across batches to support continuity.
Keyframe Constraints for Scene-Grounded Motion
In the current stage, we use Nvidia Kimodo
Rempe et al. (2026) as Low-Level Motion Generator. Although Kimodo provides stable and high-
quality action generation capabilities, it lacks the ability to perceive scene information. In order to
generate the action that can precisely interact with objects in the scene, we use keyframe constraints
to guided the Kimodo to generate scene-grounded motions.
Given an excusable intended behavior description and current environment states, which include the
character avatar state, the keyframe generating module produces the spares constraints that guide
action generator generating the actions with scene-matched action. For example, opening a door
requires placing the hand at the handle’s spatial coordinates, while sitting requires positioning the
body over the seat in an appropriate posture and orientation. Constraints also specify the avatar’s
facing direction relative to a target object. In general, generated keyframes provide spatial refer-
ence states for the intended behavior, while the low-level motion generator produces the movement
between them.

Keyframe Constraints Study
To better understand the contribution of keyframe constraints, we
conduct a keyframe ablation study on a set of common daily activities. Each task is evaluated us-
ing both the full module and an ablated variant in which the keyframe constraint module is disabled,
while the low-level motion generator and action descriptions remain unchanged. In this ablation, ba-
sic navigation and positional guidance are retained, including the coordinates of the target object or
support surface and the associated body-joint relationships. Only the additional keyframe-level pose
and interaction constraints are removed: the ablated variant receives no explicit keyframe poses,
temporally aligned contact states, or keyframe-level guidance for character position and orienta-
tion. This setting allows us to isolate the contribution of keyframe constraints while retaining the
minimum information required for scene-aware motion generation.
From the complete set of generated results, we select four representative cases for qualitative visu-
alization: (1) sitting on a sofa while watching television and subsequently standing up to cheer, (2)
sitting at a dining table, (3) performing jumping jacks in a suitable open area in the living room, and
(4) walking toward a toilet while holding the abdomen in pain and then sitting on the toilet. These
cases cover different forms of spatial guidance, including body orientation, placement relative to
scene objects, positioning in an open area, transitions between supported and standing poses, and
locomotion accompanied by a specified body gesture. The first and fourth cases are each presented
using four snapshots to illustrate multiple stages of the behavior, while the second and third cases
are shown as paired comparisons. Together, these examples provide a qualitative comparison of the
motions generated with and without keyframe constraints.

Keyframe Constraints Study Conclusion
Across the four scenarios, disabling keyframe con-
straints does not noticeably affect the navigation component: the character can still identify the
intended region and move to the vicinity of the target. However, the ablated module largely loses
the ability to establish reliable interactions with external objects, particularly when the action re-
quires precise spatial relationships among the character, the target object, and the surrounding scene.
Self-interaction actions are generally less affected because they depend less on external geometry.
Nevertheless, when an action description does not reliably elicit the requested motion pattern from
the low-level generator, the ablated module may fall back to a more generic action. The full module
provides more consistent scene grounding and better preserves the intended action characteristics in
these cases. Its motion quality, however, remains partially dependent on the quality of the generated
keyframes; strongly constrained poses, such as some sitting configurations, may occasionally appear
slightly less natural.
