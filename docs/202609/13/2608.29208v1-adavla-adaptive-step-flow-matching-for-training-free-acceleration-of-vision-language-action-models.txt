# AdaVLA: Adaptive Step Flow Matching for Training-free Acceleration of Vision-Language-Action Models 

Sunghwan Han 1, Youngtae Han 1, and Youngmin Yi 1†

Abstract — Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, built upon Vision-Language Models (VLMs), have significantly enhanced robotic capabilities by leveraging internet-scale knowledge and multimodal reasoning. However, the intensive computational overhead of VLAs constrains on-device deployment, hindering real-time responses to environmental changes. While various acceleration techniques have been proposed, they often rely on fine-tuning or access to training datasets, which are frequently unavailable due to privacy and proprietary concerns. Moreover, although flow-matching-based VLAs have emerged as efficient alternatives to standard diffusion models, current acceleration efforts largely target VLM inference costs, failing to address the iterative ODE solving process inherent in flow matching inference. To address these limitations, we propose AdaVLA , an online, training-free adaptive framework for fast yet accurate flow-matching-based Vision-Language-Action models. We introduce a novel metric derived from the flow matching trajectory curvature to quantify action generation confidence during inference. This metric enables the dynamic reduction of inference steps and the adaptive adjustment of MLP pruning ratios through an efficiently com-puted importance evaluation, requiring no access to training data. Experimental results on the LIBERO benchmark using a Jetson AGX Orin device demonstrate that our method achieves 

1.87 × and 2.24 × speedups for π0.5 and X-VLA, respectively, with negligible degradation in success rates. Furthermore, we validate the robustness of our approach on real-world robotic tasks using SmolVLA. 

I. INTRODUCTION Recent advancements in integrating visual and language models have led to the development of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), marking significant progress in multimodal reasoning and generalization. This progress has extended to robotic action planning, evolving VLMs from generating tex-tual responses to predicting direct control actions, resulting in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [1], [2], [3]. These models demonstrate superior robustness and generalization across diverse environments compared to traditional rein-forcement learning policies. However, VLAs inherit the intensive computational costs of the underlying VLM. This results in substantial inference latency between actions, leading to unnatural robotic motion. While several acceleration methods—including lightweight design (TinyVLA [4]), early-exiting (DeeR-VLA [5]), and token pruning with layer-skipping (EfficientVLA [6])— have been proposed, they typically require access to training data for fine-tuning or calibration. A. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models 

VLA models have emerged as a new paradigm for general-purpose robot learning. Early developments such as RT-1 [16] utilized Transformers to output discrete actions, while RT-2 [2] demonstrated that co-fine-tuning VLMs on large-scale datasets enables the transfer of semantic reasoning to robotic control tasks. Moving beyond autoregressive policies like OpenVLA [1], continuous generative approaches have been introduced for modeling complex action distributions. Diffusion Policy [17] pioneered the application of diffusion models to visuomotor control, leading to integrated VLA architectures such as CogACT [18] and DiffusionVLA [19]. Recently, flow matching [9] models, including π0 [10], 

π0.5 [11], SmolVLA [12], and X-VLA [13], have gained prominence. These models leverage continuous normalizing flows to achieve high-quality generation with significantly fewer steps, making them effective for real-time control. 

B. Efficient Vision-Language-Action Models 

Despite their capabilities, the substantial computational costs of VLAs hinder their deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. To address this challenge, var-ious acceleration techniques have been proposed. Robo-Mamba [20] introduced State Space Models (SSMs) [21] for robotic reasoning tasks, achieving linear complexity with re-spect to sequence length. DeeR-VLA [5] proposed a dynamic early-exiting mechanism, allowing the model to terminate inference early for simple tasks to reduce computation. OpenVLA-OFT [22] enables efficient parallel decoding for large-scale backbones. Other efforts mitigate Transformer redundancy; for instance, EfficientVLA [6] and MoLe-VLA [23] utilize token/layer pruning and dynamic layer-skipping to enhance efficiency. However, these approaches rely on retraining or calibration (e.g., layer importance assessment), both of which typically require training data. This dependency limits their applicability in data-limited and privacy-sensitive scenarios, which our work addresses. III. P RELIMINARY

A. Adaptive Inference via Trajectory Curvature 

Generating actions via flow matching is a primary bottle-neck due to its iterative nature. We propose an online adapta-tion framework that dynamically adjusts flow matching step sizes and pruning ratios based on perceived difficulty. As shown in Sec. III-B, the flow matching model learns a vector field that transports samples from the noise ϵ

toward the action At. B. Training-free & Efficient MLP Importance Assessment 

Applying a uniform pruning ratio to the MLP blocks across all layers is suboptimal, as individual layers exhibit varying degrees of parameter sensitivity and information density. Furthermore, computing gradient-based layer im-portance is often prohibitively expensive for edge devices due to their limited computational budgets and data privacy constraints. To address this, AdaVLA evaluates layer importance using only forward passes without access to training data, employing a metric inspired by the Effective Rank [28] of MLP activations. C. MLP Channel Reordering 

Table I shows that VLM computational bottlenecks stem primarily from the MLP blocks. Given that these bottlenecks arise from the massive intermediate channels, we adopt struc-tured pruning, which is more efficient and implementation-friendly than unstructured alternatives. EXPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

Models: To validate the effectiveness of AdaVLA , we evaluated it on two representative state-of-the-art flow-matching-based VLAs: π0.5 [11] and X-VLA [13] with a default step size of s = 0 .1 (10 steps). 

Simulation Benchmarks: We employed the LIBERO benchmark [14], which comprises the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long task suites, to evaluate diverse semantic and spatial reasoning capabilities. We set the curvature threshold Cth 

to 0.15 for π0.5 and 0.002 for X-VLA. These values were determined via a simple grid search, and our ablation studies demonstrate that performance is robust across a range of 

Cth values. For a new model or benchmark, users can readily identify a near-optimal Cth from a single profiling run by inspecting the resulting curvature distribution. All model checkpoints were obtained from the official LeRobot repository on Hugging Face [30], [31]. 

Hardware & Metrics: Following the evaluation protocol in [22], we evaluated each task over 50 rollouts. To avoid prohibitively long evaluation times on the edge device (up to 12 hours per full benchmark), the average success rate was measured on a server-grade NVIDIA A10 GPU. We addition-ally verified on a subset of tasks that the success rates on the edge-grade NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin were comparable to those obtained on the A10. Latency was measured as the time required to generate a single action chunk on both the A10 GPU and the Jetson AGX Orin. To reflect real-world edge constraints, the total system energy consumption (CPU, GPU, and motherboard) was measured on the Jetson AGX Orin. Both latency and energy consumption were averaged over 10 rollouts per task.

C. Ablation Study 

To evaluate the specific impact of each component, we conducted ablation studies on π0.5 using the Jetson AGX Orin. The results are summarized in Table III. 

Impact of Efficient Assessment: While SVD better iden-tifies critical layers leading to a high success rate (99.65% SR), it offers limited latency improvements compared to our Participation Ratio-based pruning method (894.78 ms vs 513.35 ms). It also incurs an unacceptable Time-to-First-Action (TTFA) of 4939.32 ms and 23.60 J energy cost due to decomposition overheads. In contrast, the Participation Ratio (PR) approach obviates the need for explicit decom-position, utilizing computationally efficient trace and norm calculations instead. This yields a 5.63 × TTFA speedup (877.13 ms) and lowers energy usage to 13.17 J without meaningful performance degradation. Although PR adds a marginal 21 ms latency overhead compared to the fixed variant (+Fxd), it effectively preserves a 99.45% SR by accurately prioritizing representational density.

Depending on deployment requirements, users may prioritize lower latency or higher success rate; AdaVLA provides this flexibility through Cth ,which can be selected via a lightweight grid search.

E. Analysis of Adaptive Inference 

We visualized the actual trajectories generated by flow-matching-based VLAs to analyze the behavior of AdaVLA .Fig. 3 illustrates 250 randomly sampled trajectories recorded during the LIBERO-Long benchmark [14] for both π0.5 [11] and X-VLA [13] under the same setup described in Sec. V-A. In the baseline (left), both models consistently execute a high number of flow matching iterations, leading to a substantial number of forward passes in the Action Expert. In contrast, our method (right) generates clean actions with significantly fewer forward passes while adaptively allocat-ing additional steps only when necessary. Furthermore, we analyzed the specific task segments where these additional steps are triggered, particularly fo-cusing on instances where the model reaches the maximum step limit. Our findings reveal that the model intensifies its computational effort during critical task moments, such as grasping (5a) and placing (5b) objects. Moreover, addi-tional steps were dynamically invoked when the environment shifted unexpectedly—for instance, when an object fell dur-ing a pick-up attempt (5c). These results demonstrate that the curvature of the trajectories can serve as an intuitive proxy for perceived difficulty and action confidence, reducing reliance on complex, explicitly modeled difficulty estimators. VI. CONCLUSION We presented AdaVLA , a training-free, online adaptive framework for fast yet accurate flow-matching-based VLAs. Inspired by human cognition, our method autonomously regulates computational effort by utilizing flow-matching trajectory curvature to adjust inference steps and MLP prun-ing ratios. On the Jetson AGX Orin, we achieved 1.87 ×

to 2.24 × speedup with negligible success rate degradation on the LIBERO benchmark and 1.65 × speedup in real-world experiments. By eliminating the need to access training data for fine-tuning or calibration, AdaVLA offers a highly practical solution for deploying flow-matching-based VLAs on resource-constrained robotic systems.
