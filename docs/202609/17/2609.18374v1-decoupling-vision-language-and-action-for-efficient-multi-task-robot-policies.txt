# Decoupling Vision, Language, and Action for Efficient Multi-Task Robot Policies 

Xiatao Sun 1∗, Chen Liang 1, Ziyao Zeng 1, Qian Wang 1,Haoyang Zhang 2,3, Yue Sun 3, Qiucheng Li 3, and Daniel Rakita 1

Abstract — Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models attach an action module to a Vision-Language Model (VLM) with billions of parameters and pay for that backbone at every control step. For a low-level manipulation policy, this cost may be unnecessary: the VLM supplies vision and language embed-dings, and recent standalone vision encoders and encoder-only language models now match or exceed large VLMs on visual embedding and language understanding benchmarks. We study this question with a controlled experiment. Holding the demonstrations, the training budget, the tasks, and the measurement platform fixed, we vary the vision encoder, the language encoder, and the action head of a decoupled policy and compare against seven VLA baselines. The study yields the Decoupled Embodiment Model (DEM), which pairs afine-tuned DINOv3 encoder and a frozen NeoBERT encoder with a MeanFlow head that generates each action chunk in a single forward pass. On 18 simulated manipulation tasks with held-out language paraphrases and randomized scenes, and on three real-robot tasks, DEM achieves observed success comparable to state-of-the-art VLM-backbone policies under our evaluation protocol, while running at eight to seventeen times their inference frequency and drawing six to fifteen times less energy per inference. Within this task scope, modern decoupled components offer a better success–latency–energy trade-off. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Most Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models today are built the same way: take a large Vision-Language Model (VLM) and attach an action module [1, 2, 3]. The backbone costs billions of parameters, and the robot pays for it at every control step. π0.5 [4] spends on the order of 100 ms per inference, so its perception-to-action loop runs at a few hertz. Action chunking [5] and asynchronous execution [6] hide the latency but do not shorten the loop underneath. We ask whether this cost is necessary for a low-level manipulation policy. Inside a VLA, the VLM’s job is to encode images and an instruction into an embedding that conditions the action module. That job does not generate language, which is what the decoder-only VLM is built and sized for; autoregressive VLAs such as OpenVLA do run the decoder, but to emit action tokens, and most recent VLAs hand even that to a separate action module. Transformer language models split early into encoder-only models for un-derstanding [7] and decoder-only models for generation [8]. A VLM may still contribute joint vision-language knowledge that separate encoders lack, so the question is empirical: how

Earlier generalist policies were already decoupled: RT-1 [9] ran EfficientNet alongside a frozen sentence encoder in 35M parameters, and Octo [10] conditioned a small transformer on frozen T5 embeddings. What limited them was the quality of their components: encoder-only language models had seen little new invest-ment since ChatGPT pulled pretraining toward decoder-only models, and robotics, lacking native training data, followed the strongest available checkpoints into the VLM [2]. DI-NOv3 [11] and NeoBERT [12] have since restored frontier pretraining for standalone encoders, which now match the representations of decoder-only models several times their size (Section II). A VLM backbone may be unnecessarily costly for low-level control policies. Such policies must be reactive and run under energy constraints, whereas LLMs and VLMs scale without regard to efficiency and live on remote servers that reactive control cannot call. On the robot, autoregres-sive VLAs run at 3–5 Hz against the 25–50 Hz that high-frequency control needs [13], and asynchronous execution hides the delay without shortening the reaction time [6]. Because inference speed and power both matter, we test the alternative directly: a decoupled policy with a compact vi-sion encoder, an encoder-only language model, and an action arXiv:2609.18374v1 [cs.RO] 16 Sep 2026 head, with no VLM in the loop. We run a controlled study over the three modules, in which every variant is trained on the same demonstrations for the same number of steps and evaluated under the same protocol, so architecture is the only variable. The study yields the Decoupled Embodiment Model (DEM) . Thinking in decoupled terms also led us to re-examine the action module: an ablation over action heads (Section V-E) led us to MeanFlow [14], which produces a whole action chunk in one forward pass and accounts for a large share of DEM’s speed. On 18 RoboCasa tasks with held-out language paraphrases and randomized scenes, DEM achieves observed success comparable to VLAs seven to nine times its size under our evaluation protocol, while drawing six to fifteen times less energy per inference and running inference eight to seventeen times faster. A controlled study of the vision encoder, the language encoder, and the action head of a decoupled policy on the same demonstrations, tasks, training budget, and measurement platform, against seven prior and concurrent VLAs, on 18 simulated tasks and three real-world tasks.  

> •

> •

Decoupled policies and VLM backbones. The first language-conditioned generalist policies kept separate en-coders: RT-1 [9] paired an EfficientNet with a frozen sen-tence encoder, Octo [10] conditioned a small transformer on frozen T5 embeddings, and BAKU [15] put interchange-able action heads behind separate encoders. Their compo-nents dated from 2019: encoder-only language modeling saw little follow-up after RoBERTa [16], and no pretrained visual representation of the time worked across embodied tasks [17]. As pretraining investment moved to decoder-only generation [8], robotics repurposed VLMs as embedding providers, and the recipe settled into OpenVLA [1], the π

series [2, 4], and GR00T [3]. The backbone’s latency is handled by action chunking [5], parallel decoding [13], real-time chunk stitching [6], smaller or truncated backbones [18, 19], and token caching or early exit [20, 21], all of which keep the decoder-only structure. A separate line makes compact policies cheaper without a VLM, through low-rank or geometry-structured training of diffusion policies [22, 23], pose- or mesh-based observations [24, 25], learned viewpoint selection [26], and attention shaping against shortcut learning [27]. 

Encoder revival. The components that limited the decou-pled policies have since improved. DINOv3 [11] features

exceed the language-supervised encoders that current VLMs use as vision towers on dense tasks and match them on clas-sification. ModernBERT [16], NeoBERT [12], and mmBERT [28] renewed encoder pretraining, and paired-training exper-iments that hold data, architecture, and parameter count fixed show encoders beating decoders on representation tasks, with a 150M encoder above a 400M decoder on MNLI [29]. Inside VLMs, with the language model held fixed, perceptual performance is set by the vision tower [30]. Whether these encoders can replace the VLM inside a policy is the question this paper tests. 

Concurrent work. TurboVLA [31] and ReactVLA [32] arrive at a similar design and evaluate it on LIBERO, where a 0.54M-parameter policy conditioned on a task index reaches 95.1% [33], so parity there says little about capability. MeanFlow [14] has entered manipulation through per-task point-cloud policies [34] and, concurrently with our work, as the action expert of language-conditioned policies [35, 32]. We evaluate on RoboCasa [36] with held-out paraphrases and randomized scenes, vary the vision encoder, the language encoder, and the action head under one protocol, and include TurboVLA and ReactVLA as baselines. III. DEM: D ECOUPLED EMBODIMENT MODEL 

DEM does not share a backbone across modalities. A vision encoder, a language encoder, and an action head are three separate networks whose outputs meet only in the cross-attention inside the head (Fig. 2). The specific modules, DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B for vision, NeoBERT for language, and MeanFlow for the action head, were selected by the controlled study of Sections V-C to V-E. 

A. Vision encoder 

Each camera frame passes through a DINOv3 ConvNeXt-B [11]. We take the stage-3 feature map, a 16 × 16 grid of 512-dimensional features for a 256-pixel input, and flatten it into 256 tokens per camera; with a scene camera and a wrist camera the vision pathway emits Nv = 512 tokens per control step. The encoder is initialized from the public DINOv3 checkpoint and fine-tuned together with the head, which the ablation in Section V-C supports. 

B. Language encoder 

The instruction goes through NeoBERT [12], an encoder-only language model with 222M parameters as loaded. We pad or truncate the instruction to Nl = 32 tokens and keep the final-layer hidden state of every token with its attention mask rather than a pooled sentence vector. NeoBERT stays frozen, since fine-tuning it did not change success in our experiments (Section V-D). Because the language tokens are computed without reference to the images, the language pathway is optional at each step: when the instruction has not changed, the policy reuses the cached tokens, and when a new command arrives, it re-encodes. Every policy and every variant is trained for 300k steps on the same demonstrations and evaluated over 100 rollouts per task. With 100 rollouts, the binomial standard error of a task’s success rate is at most 5 points and that of the 18-task mean about 1.2 points, so we treat differences of up to 2.5 points in the mean as within noise. 

Baselines. We compare against OpenVLA [1], Octo [10], SmolVLA [19], π0.5 [4], GR00T N1.7 [40], TurboVLA [31], and ReactVLA [32], all fine-tuned on the same demonstra-tions with their official implementations and default recipes, updating all parameters. DEM and the chunked baselines generate 16 actions per inference and replan every eight control steps. 

Compute. Training runs on eight H100 GPUs. Evalu-ation and every latency and power measurement run on one workstation with an RTX PRO 6000 GPU, an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 9965WX CPU, and 128 GB of memory. Latency covers the policy’s forward pass at batch size 1 and excludes camera capture, preprocessing, and robot communication, which are common to all policies; energy is the mean GPU power during inference times the median latency, with idle draw not subtracted. 

Real-world setup. Real-world experiments use an xArm 7 mounted on a linear rail driven by a linear motor, which together form an eight-degree-of-freedom manipulation sys-tem. We evaluate three FurnitureBench [41] assembly tasks, drawer, lamp, and cabinet, with 200 teleoperated episodes per task, 100k training steps for every policy, the same paraphrase protocol, and 50 rollouts per task and method (standard error up to 7 points per task, about 4 points for the three-task mean). On the robot we compare against GR00T N1.7, the strongest VLM-backbone policy in simulation, 

π0.5, the most widely deployed one and the architecture of Fig. 2, and TurboVLA, the strongest concurrent decoupled policy; the remaining baselines score below TurboVLA in simulation while spending more energy per inference (Ta-bles I and II). V. R ESULTS 

A. Overall benchmark 

Table I reports success rates on the 18 RoboCasa atomic tasks. DEM averages 55.6%, within 1.3 points of GR00T N1.7 (56.9%) and above π0.5 (54.6%), differences inside the sampling error of Section IV, with 417M parameters, 195M of them active per step, against roughly 3B for either VLM-backbone policy (Table II). It has the highest success rate on drawers, levers, and buttons, trails GR00T N1.7 on pick-and-place, doors, and insertion, and has its largest deficit on the knob task (37.0% against 49.0%), which demands the finest positioning of the 18 tasks and is where the 16 ×16 token grid is most likely to limit precision. The two other decoupled policies sit below the VLM-backbone group, TurboVLA at 46.3% and ReactVLA at 41.9%, and Octo, which pairs 2024-era encoders with a diffusion head, is last at 25.6%. Octo and DEM share the decoupled structure and differ only in their components, which answers the question of Section II: with 2024 components the recipe finishes last, with 2025 components its observed success is comparable to the best VLM-backbone policy.

VI. D ISCUSSION 

Within the scope of our tasks, what the policy needs from its backbone is an embedding, and modern standalone encoders supply it: DEM achieves observed success com-parable to VLM-backbone policies under our protocol at a fraction of their latency and energy. Comparable is a descriptive statement; with 100 rollouts per task the study resolves differences of a few points and does not establish equivalence. The ablations locate where the cost of a VLM goes: its towers, used on their own inside DEM, add three to four times the latency and energy of DINOv3 and NeoBERT and no success (Tables III and IV), and the iterative heads that VLAs inherit from diffusion and flow matching cost four to six times the latency of MeanFlow for the same success (Table V). Several limits bound these conclusions. The evaluation tasks are the training tasks: scenes and instructions vary, but no rollout asks for an object or a task absent from training, so the experiments do not test the joint vision-language knowledge that a VLM’s pretraining might provide on unseen objects, instructions, or tasks; our results say only that this knowledge did not show up as success on the tasks studied here. C ONCLUSION 

We studied, under one protocol, how much of a VLA’s suc-cess a decoupled policy reaches on multi-task manipulation. The configuration the study arrives at, DEM, pairs a fine-tuned DINOv3 encoder and a frozen NeoBERT encoder with a one-step MeanFlow head and achieves observed success comparable to VLM-backbone policies on 18 simulated tasks with held-out paraphrases and randomized scenes and on three real-world tasks, at a fraction of their latency and energy. On these tasks the VLM’s towers bring no additional success when used as parts, fine-tuning pays on the vision side and not on the language side, and one-step generation makes action chunking optional. The properties that make DEM efficient, a 163 Hz inference rate and an instruction encoded only when it changes, also suggest two directions for future work. Agentic robot systems, in which a language model plans and calls lower-level skills, are drawing growing interest, and a policy with these properties is a natural skill for such a system to call. Inference speed also matters for reinforcement learning: online RL of a billion-parameter VLA is limited by the compute its forward passes and parameter updates demand [43], so recent work runs it in large-scale parallel simulation [44], freezes the VLA and trains a small latent policy around it [45], or replaces the online loop with iterated offline updates [46]. A policy whose forward pass takes 6.1 ms lowers the cost of every step of that loop and may make direct online RL of a multi-task policy on the robot practical again.
